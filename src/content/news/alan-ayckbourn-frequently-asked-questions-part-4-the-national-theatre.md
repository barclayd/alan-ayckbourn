<p><em><a href="/">Alan Ayckbourn’s Official Website</a> constantly receives questions about Sir Alan Ayckbourn and his plays – many of which recur quite frequently.</em></p>

<p><em>As a result of this, there’s a substantial <a href="/life/ayckbourn-faqs">FAQs section</a> on the website looking to answer many of the questions. For the next few weeks, the blog will be reproducing – and occasionally expanding – the FAQs hopefully answering a few questions and offering a bit of insight into Alan Ayckbourn.</em></p>

<p><em>In part 4, we’re looking at questions regarding Alan Ayckbourn’s work with the National Theatre…</em></p>

<p><strong>Frequently Asked Questions: The National Theatre</strong></p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Sir Peter Hall with Alan Ayckbourn on the set of Bedroom Farce in 1977 (© National Theatre)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>1) When did Alan Ayckbourn first work with the National Theatre?<br></strong>The first suggestion of Alan working for the National Theatre was a letter from its Artistic Director, <a href="/life/influences/hall-sir-peter">Peter Hall</a>, to Alan on 29 August 1973 hoping “one day you will think of writing a play for the National Theatre.”</p>

<p>Hall then lobbied hard for the National Theatre to produce <em><a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a></em> after he saw the world premiere production at <a href="http://theatre-in-the-round.co.uk">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a>, Scarborough, in 1974. Unfortunately, it had already been optioned by the producer Michael Codron for the West End.</p>

<p>By 6 February 1975, Alan had agreed in principle to write a play for the under-construction National Theatre on the South Bank, according to correspondence from Hall. This would be <em><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></em>, commissioned by their National Theatre but on the understanding it would premiere in Scarborough in 1975 before opening at the National in 1976.</p>

<p>However, the London premiere was delayed and it did not open until 16 March 1977 in the Lyttelton at the National Theatre, marking Alan’s debut with the company.</p>

<p><strong>2) Which Ayckbourn plays have been produced at the National Theatre?<br></strong>The following Ayckbourn plays have been produced at the National Theatre (year of production in parentheses):&nbsp;<em>Bedroom Farce</em> (1977);&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/sisterly-feelings">Sisterly Feelings</a></em> (1980);&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/way-upstream">Way Upstream</a></em><a href="/plays/a-chorus-of-disapproval"> (1982);&nbsp;<em>A Chorus Of Disapproval</em></a> (1984);&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/a-small-family-business">A Small Family Business</a>&nbsp;</em>(originally in 1987 and revived in 2014);&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/invisible-friends">Invisible Friends</a></em> (1991);&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/mr-as-amazing-maze-plays">Mr A’s Amazing Maze Plays</a></em> (1993);&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/house-and">House &amp; Garden</a></em> (1999);&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/seasons-greetings">Season’s Greetings</a></em> (2010). The revues&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/me-myself-and-i">Me, Myself And I</a></em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/mere-soup-songs">Mere Soup Songs</a></em>&nbsp;have also been produced at the National Theatre.</p>

<p><strong>3) What plays has Alan Ayckbourn directed at the National Theatre?<br></strong>Alan Ayckbourn has directed the following plays at the National Theatre (all plays by Ayckbourn unless otherwise indicated): <em>Bedroom Farce</em> (1977 with Peter Hall);&nbsp;<em>Sisterly Feelings</em> (1980 with Christopher Morahan);&nbsp;<em>Way Upstream</em> (1982);&nbsp;<em>A Chorus Of Disapproval</em> (1984);&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/tons-of-money-evans-and-valentine">Tons of Money</a></em> (by Evans &amp; Valentine, 1986); <em>A Small Family Business&nbsp;</em>(1987);&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/miller-arthur">A View From The Bridge</a></em> (by Arthur Miller, 1987); <em><a href="/career/national-theatre/tis-pity">‘Tis Pity She’s A Whore</a></em> (by John Ford, 1987); <em>Invisible </em>Friends (1991); <em>Mr A’s Amazing Maze Plays</em> (1993), <em>House</em> (1999), <em>Garden</em> (1999).</p>

<p>He also directed two of his musical revues – created with the composer Paul Todd – at the National Theatre: <em>Me, Myself and I</em> (1985) and <em>Mere Soup Songs</em> (1987).</p>

<p><strong>4) Which of Alan Ayckbourn’s National Theatre productions have transferred into the West End?<br></strong>The following Ayckbourn plays have transferred into the West End (year of transfer in parentheses): <em>Bedroom Farce</em> (1978, later transferring to Broadway in 1979);&nbsp;<em>A Chorus Of Disapproval</em> (1986);&nbsp;<em>A View From The Bridge </em>(1987).</p>

<p><strong>5) Which awards has Alan Ayckbourn received from his National Theatre productions?<br></strong>Alan Ayckbourn has received the following awards from his National Theatre productions: Olivier Award for Best Comedy (<em>A Chorus of Disapproval</em>); Evening Standard Award for Best Comedy (<em>A Chorus Of Disapproval</em>); DRAMA Best Comedy Award (<em>A Chorus Of Disapproval</em>); Evening Standard Award for Best Play (<em>A Small Family Business</em>); Plays and Players Award for Best Director (<em>A View From The Bridge</em>).</p>

<p>He was also nominated for an Olivier Award for Best Director for <em>A View From The Bridge</em> and for Tony Awards for Outstanding Direction and Best Play with <em>Bedroom Farce</em>.</p>

<p>In addition, it’s worth noting that the late actor Michael Gambon won Olivier Awards for his performances in Alan’s productions for the National Theatre of <em>A Chorus of Disapproval</em> and <em>A View From The Bridge</em>.</p>

<p><strong>6) Did Alan Ayckbourn really close the National Theatre with <em>Way Upstream</em>?<br></strong>Yes he did. Infamously, the fibre-glass tank used to hold the water on which the boat ‘floats’ split open on 9 August 1982; it should be noted that the world premiere production in Scarborough had no such production problems and the design team warned the NT away using a brittle material such as fibre glass – but, alas, what would a small regional theatre know about such things…</p>

<p>Built in-house at the NT rather than by structural engineers, the weight of water the tank could hold was apparently severely misjudged and – as it was filled for the first time – the tank split, spilling water into the bowels of the Lyttelton Theatre, the sub-basement and the switch rooms which operated the entire National Theatre, warping the Lyttelton stage in the process. With thousands of gallons of water pouring into the switch rooms and everyone desperately trying to avert an even bigger disaster, Alan made the decision to close the whole of the National Theatre building – as the Artistic Director Peter Hall was not in London at the time.</p>

<p>“I watched all the lights going out in the whole theatre until it switched off and I realised that single-handedly I had closed the National Theatre for the evening.”</p>

<p><strong>7) When did Alan Ayckbourn take a sabbatical from the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round to join the National Theatre?<br></strong>Alan’s sabbatical from the <a href="/career/ayckbourn-and-the-sjt/sjt-venues/stephen-joseph-theatre-in-the-round">Stephen Joseph Theatre In The Round</a> was from 1986 to 1988. During this period he became a company director at the&nbsp;<a href="/career/national-theatre">National Theatre</a>&nbsp;and had his own acting company within the complex. He was initially asked by Sir Peter Hall to form his own company whilst he was directing <em>A Chorus of Disapproval</em> for the National Theatre in 1984.</p>

<p><strong>8) What plays did Alan Ayckbourn direct for the National Theatre during this sabbatical?<br></strong>The National Theatre’s Artistic Director&nbsp;Peter Hall&nbsp;originally arranged for Alan to direct three plays in each of the National Theatre’s auditoria. This was later extended to four plays after <em>A View From The Bridge</em> transferred into the West End (venues in paranthesis).</p>

<p>The plays were&nbsp;<em>Tons Of Money</em>&nbsp;(Lyttelton),&nbsp;<em>A View From The Bridge</em> (Cottesloe),&nbsp;<em>A Small Family Business</em>&nbsp;(Olivier) and&nbsp;<em>‘Tis Pity She’s A Whore</em>&nbsp;(Olivier).</p>

<p>The transfer of <em>A View From The Bridge</em> to the West End – at the Aldwych Theatre – also meant Alan had to recast&nbsp;<em>A Small Family Business</em>&nbsp;during the latter part of its run. He also presented his musical revue <em>Mere Soup Songs</em>&nbsp;as a late night revue in the Lyttelton buffet restaurant.</p>

<p><strong>9) What was Alan Ayckbourn’s connection with Scarborough during this period?<br></strong>Alan remained the&nbsp;<a href="/career/artistic-director">Artistic Director</a>&nbsp;of the&nbsp;Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round&nbsp;and this position was listed in programmes during the period. Robin Herford was appointed Co-Artistic Director and managed the day-to-day running of the theatre and seasonal programming decisions. During the one full year he was away (1987), Alan still wrote and directed&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/henceforward">Henceforward…</a></em>&nbsp;for Scarborough.</p>

<p><strong>10) Is it true Alan Ayckbourn considered staying at the National Theatre and not returning to Scarborough following his sabbatical at the NT?<br></strong>No. This was originally reported by Michael Coveney in the&nbsp;<em>Financial Times</em>&nbsp;during November 1986, but appears to have been reported on the basis of rumour and gossip rather than actual facts.</p>

<p>Alan had made it clear in interviews prior to leaving that he would return to Scarborough in 1988, a point given extra weight as he remained Artistic Director throughout his sabbatical period. The fact that Alan had also committed himself in 1986 to writing and directing a new play in Scarborough,&nbsp;<em>Henceforward…</em>, for the 1987 season also suggests he was committed to Scarborough.</p>

<p>Possibly as a riposte to Coveney’s statement, Alan made it clear in the February 1987 issue of&nbsp;<em>Plays International</em>&nbsp;his full intentions: “I have every intention of returning to Scarborough in 1988 and indeed we are already beginning to think of what will be in the repertoire there then.”</p>

<p>The final nail in the coffin of this myth is that Alan also announced he would direct his new play&nbsp;<em>Man Of The Moment</em>&nbsp;for the 1988 Scarborough season while he was still at the National Theatre in 1987.</p>

<p>Without any substantive evidence to prove otherwise, the ‘news’ was probably based on gossip, rumour and the fact the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round’s relationship with Scarborough Council was going through a rocky patch at the time. To this day, there has been no credible or reliable source given for Michael Coveney’s original story.</p>

<p><strong>11) What was the most successful Ayckbourn production to be staged at the National Theatre?</strong></p>

<p><em>Bedroom Farce</em>. By a very long shot. So successful was <em>Bedroom Farce</em> that Sir Peter Hall, Artistic Director of the National Theatre, refered to it as the first bona fide hit of the National Theatre in its then new home at the South Bank.</p>

<p>The National Theatre’s production of <em>Bedroom Farce</em> opened in the Alexandria Theatre, Birmingham, on 1 March 1977 before transferring to the Lyttelton at the National Theatre on 16 March 1977 and by April 1978 had become the longest running show in the National’s repertory having been seen by 140,429 people in the first year. It played in repertory at the NT until 17 August 1978.</p>

<p>The National Theatre production then transferred into the West End  where it opened at the Prince of Wales Theatre on 7 November 1978 and ran until 29 September 1979. <em>Bedroom Farce</em> has the second longest London run of any Ayckbourn play after the 1973 West End production of <em><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></em> (<em>Bedroom Farce</em> actually ran longer, but <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> had more performances).</p>

<p>Meanwhile, the NT production also toured to North America on 22 January 1979 before transferring to the Brooks Atkinson Theatre on Broadway on 29 March 1979 where it ran for 276 performances.</p>

<p>It really was quite a successful production!</p>
