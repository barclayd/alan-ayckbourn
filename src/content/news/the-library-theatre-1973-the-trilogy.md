<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1973 and the debut of an extraordinary theatre event…</p>

<p><strong>1973: Staging a Conquest </strong>by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>At the climax of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre’s 1972 summer season, there were two rather notable occurrences; one of which would become the company’s defining event of 1973.</p>

<p>The first was the appointment of Alan Ayckbourn as the venue’s permanent <strong><a href="/career/artistic-director">Artistic Director</a></strong> – a position he would hold for 37 years until 2009.</p>

<p>The second – and key to 1973 – was a local journalist asking him what his next play would be…</p>

</div>

<p>Flushed with success from the summer’s production of&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em>, Alan rather flippantly – and, according to some reminiscences, because he could think of nothing better to say – casually mentioned he was thinking of writing a trilogy of plays.</p>

<p>Which he promptly forgot about and mentioned to no-one else.</p>

<p>Fast forward to spring 1973 and the journalist ‘apparently’ published the story, noting Alan was to write an unnamed trilogy for the summer season at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. A fact no-one – not even the playwright – was aware of.</p>

<p>Now, we have to pause here a moment because this story has been told repeatedly through the decades but, from an archival perspective, it has a major issue. There’s no actual physical record of this story being published. The interview undoubtedly took place but whilst The Scarborough News – then the Scarborough Evening News – has frequently claimed credit, it has never actually found nor reproduced the specific article.</p>

<p>In fact, there’s no evidence the article was ever published by anyone. So what probably happened, given the events which followed? Coming from a journalistic background, my guess is the reporter contacted the theatre about the story to see if it was accurate and to get more information only for alarm bells to start ringing at the theatre and history to head down the same path keeping the story largely intact.</p>

<p>What we do know happened is that Alan, away in London, suddenly received a panicked phone-call from the theatre manager at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre asking if it was true he was going to write a trilogy.</p>

<p>Possibly followed by the question, why didn’t you tell us?</p>

<p>After an apparently brief consideration, Alan decided it wasn’t such a bad idea, confirmed he was going to write a trilogy and began working on the challenge of completing it.</p>

<p>The original idea for a trilogy though – as told to the journalist – had not been completely spontaneous as the Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre’s 1972 summer season had actually begun at the recently opened Sheffield Crucible with Alan’s production of David Campton’s&nbsp;<em>Carmilla</em>. The company had spent a week there and, during that time, Alan had been asked separately if he wanted to write plays for both performance spaces in the building.</p>

<p>Committing to both – although it never went ahead – he had the idea of two plays running at the same time in both spaces with the same cast; of course, this is also arguably the genesis – 27 years before he wrote it – of&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/house-and">House &amp; Garden</a></strong></em>.</p>

<p>Back in 1973 in London, Alan now developed the idea into three plays set in the same house over one weekend sharing the same characters.</p>

<p>What was paramount in Alan’s mind during its creation was the plays not be seen or sold as a hard trilogy; that all three plays could be satisfactorily seen independently of each other and whether you saw one, two or three of them, you’d still have an enjoyable experience.</p>

<p>At the time, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was reliant on tourists and Alan felt it was too much to ask three nights of a week’s holiday be spent in the theatre!</p>

<p>As a result, the summer brochure made no mention of a trilogy. Just the titles of three plays which patently had links to one another but which could be seen independently or in any combination.</p>

<p>The plays were written simultaneously over a week in May 1973 with Alan writing each one cross-wise (i.e. all the scenes ones after each other, then all of the scenes twos etc) and were called&nbsp;<em>Fancy Meeting You</em>,&nbsp;<em>Make Yourself At Home</em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em>Round And Round The Garden</em>. Astonishingly, Alan finished writing two of the plays in one night!</p>

<p>They opened during June and were an enormous success – especially once people had realised the benefits of seeing two or more of the plays. The Stage newspaper reported every box office record at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre had been broken that season.</p>

<p>The plays were also fondly recalled for the extraordinary occasion when a member of the audience laughed so hard she spat her false teeth out, which her husband came to recover from the box office the next day!</p>

<p>Throughout this period, when discussed as a whole by Alan or the company, the plays were referred to only by one name…</p>

<p>The Trilogy.</p>

<p>It was only a year later when ‘The Trilogy’ transferred to Greenwich Theatre in London, having proved a trilogy of plays could be successful, that Alan gave the plays a true title.</p>

<p>You’ve no doubt guessed it by now, but it’s strange to think one of the most famous and acclaimed works written by Alan Ayckbourn did not come to be called by the name we know today until a year after it opened in Scarborough.</p>

<p>The name, of course,&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></strong></em>.</p>
