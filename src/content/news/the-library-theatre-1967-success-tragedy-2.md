<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1967 and a significant success for the company followed by tragedy…</p>

<p><strong>1967: Success &amp; Tragedy </strong>by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>In July 1967, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre re-opened as a professional venue having been closed by its founder, Stephen Joseph, 22 months earlier.</p>

<p>It was the beginning of a new phase of the company’s life which would eventually lead to Alan Ayckbourn being appointed Artistic Director of the company in 1972.</p>

<p>But that was still relatively far in the future. At the time, just surviving each year was the main issue for the company.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Rodney Wood &amp; Stephen Joseph (image held in the Ayckbourn Archive)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>The relaunched company’s founder and inspiration, Stephen Joseph, was no longer involved and one of his protégés, Rodney Wood, had taken over the reins as Director of Productions for the 1967 summer season; the winter seasons having been abandoned since 1962 die to limited finances. </p>

<p>Rodney proved to be a safe pair of hands and guided the theatre that year through four productions, re-establishing professional theatre-in-the-round in Scarborough. It was a season which featured a notable company including Robert Powell, John Nettles making his professional stage debut, Heather Stoney – later to marry Alan Ayckbourn, Pamela Craig, Colin Bell, Marion Dawson, Mary Piercy and Douglas Fielding.</p>

<p>Two of the four plays were world premieres by Alan Ayckbourn and Alan Plater – Rodney keeping the spirit of Stephen’s company alive – alongside productions of Priestley’s <em>Eden End</em> and Anouilh’s <em>Romeo &amp; Jeanette</em>. In one sense, little had changed. The company was still presenting both new and eclectic work as Stephen Joseph had conceived it.</p>

<p>Whilst it was a successful season for the venue and ensured short term survival at the very least, the most notable events of the year however took place away from Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre itself.</p>

<p>Two years previously in 1965, Alan Ayckbourn had written the play, <em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Meet My Father</a></strong></em>, which had been directed by Stephen Joseph and proved to be a huge success with the London producer Peter Bridge optioning it for the West End.</p>

<p>Over the next two years, <em>Meet My Father</em> began a laborious journey which involved extensive re-writing and four changes of title. The journey concluded at the Duke Of York’s Theatre in London.</p>

<p>It was the first play from The Library Theatre to transfer to the West End and arguably it changed the fortunes of both its author and Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. The play, renamed <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, was a phenomenal hit and launched Alan Ayckbourn to theatrical fame.</p>

<p>Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre had produced its first bona fide breakout hit – which 60 years later is still going strong around the world – and Stephen Joseph finally saw one of his protégés begin the steps to a career and success neither could have imagined.</p>

<p>It was a success followed by tragedy.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Stephen Joseph (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Less than six months after <em>Relatively Speaking</em> had opened in the West End and three months after Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was relaunched, Stephen Joseph died on 5 October 1967.</p>

<p>He had been diagnosed with terminal cancer early in the previous year and given just months to live. Determined as he was, Stephen Joseph actually lived for 18 months and although increasingly bed-bound was practically working to the last.</p>

<p>What might have been a devastating blow for Theare in the Round at the Library Theatre was tempered by the fact that Stephen had, to all intents and purposes, ceased to be an active participant in the venue the previous year.</p>

<p>On 12 October 1966, Stephen announced he would step down as Chairman of Scarborough Theatre Trust. The decision was attributed to the state of his health; the extent of his condition – largely unreported at the time – became clear the same evening when the minutes for the AGM which followed noted Stephen had had to leave ‘through illness.’</p>

<p>Although he remained a member of the Trust until his death, he did not attend any further meetings and there are no records of what, if any, involvement Stephen had in the running of the relaunched theatre.</p>

<p>Despite his illness, he never stopped working and completed work on two books, <em>New Theatre Forms</em> and <em>Theatre In The Round</em> (the latter offering a fascinating exploration of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre’s first decade of existence), before his death in his Scarborough home.</p>

<p>Stephen’s legacy was never to be forgotten and just one month after his death, Scarborough Theatre Trust held a Special Meeting during which two important decisions were made. The first to buy the Claremont building on Castle Road – today the Scarborough Christian Fellowship Elim Church – as a new home for the Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre company.</p>

<p>Whilst this never came to fruition, it did set the wheels in motion for the prolonged hunt for a new home for the company. A new home which would eventually bear the legacy of the second decision made in 1967.</p>

<p>“It was further proposed and seconded: ‘That the name of the theatre should bear the name of Stephen Joseph.’ This was carried unanimously.”</p>

<p><em>Simon Murgatroyd (2025)</em></p>
