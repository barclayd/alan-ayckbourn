<p>We’re going back to the future – and the past – this week in the second part of our look at Alan Ayckbourn’s science fiction plays.</p>

<p>This is all part of a tenuous link to this weekend’s <strong><a href="https://scifiscarborough.co.uk">Sci-Fi Scarborough</a></strong>, which is Scarborough largest annual geek event with a convention that takes over the entire Spa facility and attracts visitors from around the country.</p>

<p>Last week, we looked at Alan’s early sci-fi plays and his catalogue of android-driven work. This week, it’s time-travel and a peek at the other sci-fi plays.</p>

<p>But before that, I’ve often been asked the question of whether Alan is really a sci-fi fan? The simple answer to which, I think, can be seen in his programme note for his play <em>Callisto 5</em> from 1990 where he lists his influences. As you can see, it’s – as of 1990 – pretty much every major sci-fi touchstone you can think of. So I’d say it’s pretty definitive how much of a sci-fi fan Alan is.</p>

<p><em>This play is dedicated to Arthur C. Clarke, Isaac Asimov, Brian Aldiss, Stanley Kubrick, whoever it was that made Forbidden Planet, Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin, every Astronaut who ever flew, Ridley Scott and the team who made Alien, Robert Heinlein, Ray Bradbury, Patrick Moore, Kurt Vonnegut Jr., Pohl and Kornbluth, Paul Anderson, Heather Cooper, Bernard Lovell, Captain Kirk and the crew of the Starship Enterprise, John Carpenter, Philip K. Dick, A. E. Van Vogt, Stephen Hawking, John Wyndham, all the Voyager Spacecraft and everyone who has ever excited and intrigued our minds with their thoughts and conjectures about Other Worlds and Other Life Forms (real or unreal). Callisto 5 is my own small contribution to the sum of their wonderings and imaginings…</em></p>

<p>Time is an important feature in Alan Ayckbourn’s work as a playwright. Inspired by the likes of J.B. Priestley, Alan twists time within his writing to create interesting structures and opportunities from leaping forward years in the likes of <em><strong><a href="/plays/joking-apart">Joking Apart</a></strong></em> and <strong><em><a href="/plays/a-brief-history-of-women">A Brief History of Women</a></em></strong> to going backwards in <em><strong><a href="/plays/birthdays-past-birthdays-present">Birthdays Past, Birthdays Present</a></strong></em>, in real time in <em><strong><a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a></strong></em> to moving forwards, backwards and in real time in <em><strong><a href="/plays/time-of-my-life">Time of My Life</a></strong></em>, Alan has always been fascinated by the possibilities the use of time offers him as a writer.</p>

<p>But – and of more interest here – he’s also literally used time travel as a plot device in his work starting with <strong><em><a href="/plays/communicating-doors">Communicating Doors</a></em></strong> (1994). The play is initially set in a London hotel 20 years hence where the UK is in the midst of a civil war. The room’s communicating door goes back 20 years – where a murder has taken place. The contemporary person in that time can also go back 20 years to the same room where a previous murder was also committed. It sounds more complicated that it is!</p>

<p><em><strong>Ruella:</strong> If we really wanted to go back a long way, say to Shakespearean times, for instance, we’d have to set up a sort of relay team, with different people handing over every twenty years. Wouldn’t that be fascinating?</em></p>

<p><em><strong>Poopay:</strong> Yes, I can’t wait. To be or not to be. Pass it on.</em></p>

<p>The play, inspired by Alan’s love of movies from Hitchcock’s thrillers to <em>Back to the Future</em> sees time being altered – or more realistically, an alternative timeline being created – in order to thwart the murders and make sure the heroine has a happy-ish ending and the antagonist meets a suitably <em>Psycho</em>-inspired doom. I once spent a happy afternoon sorting out the timelines, which can be seen on Alan’s website <strong><a href="/plays/communicating-doors/chronology">here</a></strong> (not that I’m that geeky. Really…).</p>

<p>If – as previously noted – many of Alan’s adult plays have counterparts in his family plays, then we can find <em>Communicating Doors</em>‘ younger sibling in the very intriguing <em><strong><a href="/plays/miss-yesterday">Miss Yesterday</a></strong></em> (2004).</p>

<p>This saw Alan writing for a slightly more mature younger audience and demonstrates Alan’s view that you should never patronise younger audiences. The play centres on Tammy who, after her brother is killed in a motorcycle accident, is given the chance to alter time by a mysterious stranger, Tamara.</p>

<p>But Tammy’s decision has fateful consequences. The stranger returns, dying from an incurable disease which has devastated the future populace. The only person who can stop this happening is Tammy as her brother’s death motivated her to become a pioneering doctor.</p>

<p><em><strong>Tamara:</strong> There’s this old story about a time traveller who went back to the age of the dinosaurs just to have a look – he didn’t intend doing anything – but then, quite by accident, he stepped on this butterfly and when he finally returned to his own age – his whole world had altered. I should have listened one tiny change … one tiny single change</em></p>

<p><em><strong>Tammy:</strong> Just because my brother is alive. I don’t believe it.</em></p>

<p><em><strong>Tamara: </strong>That’s all it takes. Believe it.</em></p>

<p> Tammy has to contend with the extraordinary moral decision whether to let her brother die again  so the world can be saved in the future. As a Sci-Fi fan, one knows the quote above draws on Ray Bradbury’s short story <em>A Sound of Thunder</em> with one seemingly inconsequential incident having extraordinary ramifications whilst Tammy’s decision is, essentially, one of <em>Star Trek: The Wrath of Khan</em>‘s essential lines</p>

<p><em><strong>Spock:</strong> Logic clearly dictates that the needs of the many outweigh the needs of the few.</em></p>

<p><em><strong>Kirk:</strong> Or the one.</em></p>

<p>The stranger Tamara is, of course, Tammy’s future self which probably leads to a case of ‘the bootstrap paradox’. Rather pleasingly for the geeky among us, Tamara was played by Susan Twist who will be soon time-travelling again when she appears in the new series of <em>Doctor Who</em> opposite the 15th Doctor, Ncuti Gatwa.</p>

<p>Alan’s sci-fi influences can also be viewed in <em><strong><a href="/plays/whenever">Whenever</a></strong></em> (2000), which is best described as <em>Doctor Who</em> meets <em>The Wizard of Oz</em>. A young girl sees her Victorian inventor uncle killed and is then whisked off in his Time Machine through an increasingly dark future, picking up companions along the way until she reaches the end of time.</p>

<p><em><strong>Time Keeper:</strong> Oh, yes, 1886, of course I remember. Martin Bonny’s makeshift Heath Robinson contraption. It’s amazing you survived at all. Well, first you should know that we don’t encourage these wretched machines. They’re disruptive and can easily damage the whole fabric of time: Dreadful things!They should be permanently banned.</em></p>

<p>From there, as the machine bounces back to 1886, she has just one chance to avert disaster and restore time to its proper path. With obvious nods to Cybermen, Wookies, H.G. Wells and even Stephen Hawkings’ <em>A Brief History of Time</em>, this is a time-travelling play which readily shows its influences.</p>

<p>There are a couple of other plays with time-travel inspired moments such as <em><strong><a href="/plays/surprises">Surprises</a></strong></em> (2012) – covered in the previous article – and <strong><em><a href="/plays/the-jollies">The Jollies</a></em></strong> (2002) before we get to Alan’s most recent and affecting time-travel work, <strong><em><a href="http://thegirlnextdoor.alanayckbourn.net">The Girl Next Door</a></em></strong> (2021).</p>

<p>Written during the Covid 19 pandemic and premiered between the UK’s lockdowns, <em>The Girl Next Door</em> compared what it was like to live through the blitz of 1942 and the pandemic of 2020 when actor Rob inexplicably finds that his next door neighbour and their house are in 1942.</p>

<p><em><strong>Rob:</strong> Listen, years ago I was in an episode of Star Trek – nothing major, just a guest Romulan – two or three lines – but the point was he went on to warn someone about the future and so he changed it and of course this had this devastating kock-on effect and virtually destroyed the whole civilisation…. The point is these are not thing she should ever meddle in. We really shouldn’t.</em></p>

<p>Try as he might not to, Rob falls in love with the married Lily despite being separated by 78 years. Normalcy is only restored when a bomb falls on Lily’s house and time re-asserts itself. Despite having managed to explore the wreckage before the present day house is restored, Rob is unable to discover if Lily had survived the bombing all those decades before.</p>

<p>It is a beautiful play which compares and contrasts between the extraordinary hardships faced during World War II and life during lockdown and the attitudes to both. It ends, heartbreakingly, with Rob receiving a letter from his No.1 fan… Lily, who has recently died aged 94 having watched Rob’s life from afar.</p>

<p>Alan has long argued that all good science fiction writing is actually a comment on the world today. By extrapolating to another world or time, the writer comments on the state of the world and where it’s going. Within the time travel plays, Alan particularly addresses our situation in the world and how we have to take action ourselves rather than hoping for some miraculous intervention.</p>

<p>And what of the other sci-fi plays? There’s <strong><em><a href="/plays/callisto-5">Callisto 5</a></em></strong> (later revised as <em>Calliso#7</em>) set on one of the Jovian moons with an unseen predator stalking two young people, whose parents vanished long before, with only an omnipotent computer and sociopathic android to help (and those detecting nods to <em>Alien</em> and <em>Predator</em> aren’t far wrong). It’s probably the most obviously sci-fi of his work – complete with exploding consoles and video effects.</p>

<p>More recently, <em><strong><a href="/plays/welcome-to-the-family">Welcome to the Family</a></strong></em> (2023) took a journey into a world where people’s personalities and experiences are recorded and available to enter after death – another alarmingly prescient play given recent news reports about developments in recreating the deceased and allowing interaction with them through ‘AI’.</p>

<p>There’s also <em><strong><a href="/plays/a-word-from-our-sponsor">A Word From Our Sponsor</a></strong></em> (1995), which is superficially a sci-fi play being set in the future with some interesting gadgets, but it is essentially a morality play with the Devil coming to tempt a community as they attempt to stage a mystery play. Which leads onto the subject of the fantastic.</p>

<p>Because depending on how we define sci-fi – an increasingly flexible term – Alan has a whole host of other work which heads into other genres, but which I categorise as the fantastic. From ghosts stories such as <em><strong><a href="/plays/haunting-julia">Haunting Julia</a></strong></em> and <em><a href="/plays/snake-in-the-grass"><strong>Snake in the Grass</strong></a></em> to body-swapping with <strong><em><a href="/plays/body-language">Body Language</a></em></strong> and <strong><em><a href="/plays/if-i-were-you">If I Were You</a></em></strong>. Fairy tales such as <em><strong><a href="/plays/dreams-from-a-summer-house">Dreams From A Summer House</a></strong></em> and <strong><em><a href="/plays/awaking-beauty">Awaking Beauty</a></em></strong> to real people in literary realms such as <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-boy-who-fell-into-a-book">The Boy Who Fell Into A Book</a></strong></em> and <em><strong><a href="/plays/improbable-fiction">Improbable Fiction</a></strong></em>.</p>

<p>So if you happen to be in Scarborough this weekend, knocking about with some Daleks or Mandalorians, just have a pause and consider this. Sci-fi is traditionally a genre that theatre has historically avoided, yet Scarborough’s most famous theatre practitioner has embraced it and given us numerous sci-fi and fantastic plays, some of which such as <em><strong><a href="/plays/henceforward">Henceforward…</a></strong></em> and <em><strong><a href="/plays/comic-potential">Comic Potential</a></strong></em> made waves and won awards in the West End.</p>

<p>It’s another reason – alongside Sci-Fi Scarborough – why the town is, to quote my favourite Doctor… “Fantastic!”</p>
