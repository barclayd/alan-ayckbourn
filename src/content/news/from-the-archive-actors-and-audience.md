<p>In recent years, Alan Ayckbourn in interviews has increasingly noted how – to him – the key ingredients of theatre have always boiled down to just two things.</p>

<p>Actor and audience.</p>

<p>Indeed, this argument even featured in his most recent play <strong><em><a href="/plays/show-and-tell">Show &amp; Tell</a></em></strong> when the potential lack of an audience for the show within a show leads to a confrontation between characters.</p>

<p><strong>Peter</strong> <em>(reasonably)</em>: All right, if we’re talking principles, one of the basic principles of live theatre for me is it’s essentially the meeting of two key components, actors and audience. Those are the two ingredients without which live theatre cannot happen. Forget the preliminaries, the design meetings, the sound-scapes and the lighting plots – you can even forget the bloody director for all I care, most of them are a waste of space in my opinion, anyway. No, it’s the moment when those two, actor and audience, meet, that’s when the magic happens. And every single performance, depending on what goes on in that meeting, creates an entirely different magic. God knows why. Same show, different magic. Now, if for some reason, one of those key elements is absent, namely the audience itself, then what’s the bloody point? We might as well be back in the rehearsal room, wanking at a brick wall.</p>

<p>But where does this philosophy originate for Alan? As with many things, it can be traced back to his mentor and most significant influence, <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong>. Among many other achievements, he founded both the UK’s first professional theatre-in-the-round company and venue and championed what he dubbed ‘new theatre forms’ – although he always acknowledged that theatre-in-the-round was arguably the oldest known form of theatre.</p>

<p>One of his reasons for championing theatre-in-the-round – later taken up by Alan – was it stripped theatre to the basics. There is no need – and often no sense – in creating elaborate sets or effects. Theatre-in-the-round puts the focus directly on the actors with, if desired, nothing to distract. </p>

<p>Indeed in his book, <em>Theatre in the Round</em>, Stephen states plainly this perspective of what he believed.</p>

<p>“Acting, then, is the primary business of the theatre. Actors initiate the drama. Next come audiences. We can stop there. We have got most of what is important. Playwrights, producers [directors] and so on are, comparatively, of secondary importance.”</p>

<p>Whilst the statement is provocative – and Stephen liked nothing more than to be provocative in order to get people to talk about theatre – it was a philosophy that had a huge impact on Alan Ayckbourn. Alan views and approach to theatre are essentially forged from his experiences working in-the-round with Stephen as a young man from 1957 – 1962. </p>

<p>When, a decade later, he took over Scarborough’s <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> following Stephen’s death in 1967, he essentially took Stephen’s ideals and – with refinements and his own ideas – continued down the path his mentor has started, not least placing the emphasis on theatre-in-the-round, actors and audience.</p>

<p>Which leads us to Alan’s own thoughts on the subject and the earliest reference we have to them in archive. Although undated, there is a piece of correspondence to a student probably going back to the mid 1970s, just after Alan has taken over the Library Theatre and as his fame is at a high.</p>

<p>Here, Alan simply articulates what his philosophy is, refined from Stephen and later articulated in <em>Show &amp; Tell</em>. But if we want to know what he considers the most important elements of theatre are, this document and its one paragraph sums them up as well as anything else he has said or written and demonstrates how it has always driven his work in theatre be it as writer, director or artistic director.</p>

<p>“Audiences are half the ingredient of a play – if you believe as I believe , theatre is principally the live exchange of thoughts, ideas and emotions that occurs between one set of people (actors) and the other set (audience). The rest of us writers, directors, scenic artists etc are important but we only prepare the way to make this final event possible.”</p>

<p>It may be simple, but it also may be the essential insight into Alan Ayckbourn’s six decades as a theatre practitioner.</p>

<p>Actor and audience. The pure essentials of theatre.</p>
