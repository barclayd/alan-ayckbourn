<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1971 and look at how the company viewed itself in the new decades compared to when it had been founded in1 955</p>

<p><strong>1971: State of Play </strong>by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>It was a time of flux and uncertainty at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough.</p>

<p>Having re-opened as a professional concern in 1967 – the same year the company’s founder, Stephen Joseph, had died – the company had still not truly found a firm footing and direction.</p>

<p>There was little continuity with the Director of Productions appointed each year and the company restricted to just a summer season running from July to September. The company was – arguably – struggling both for its survival and to find a new sense of direction.</p>

<p>By 1971, the company had had three Directors of Productions in five years and losses were steadily mounting for the company as it searched unsuccessfully for a new home. It must have felt like a company gasping for air.</p>

<p>The situation of the company that year – from it s own perspective – can actually be told through two archival documents held in archive which offer quite a frank appraisal of the theatre’s position.</p>

<p>The first is taken from the Scarborough Theatre Trust minutes and was written “for the benefit of new members also to refresh the memories of founder members” of the board.</p>

<p><em>Since 1955 the theatre has always been run on a shoestring budget. Rostrums, lights, facia board [the signage outside Scarborough Library] and the dressing room tables are the property of the local branch of British Drama League, who permit us to use this equipment to run our summer season.</em></p>

<p><em>Administration, front of house duties, coffee bar were all done on a voluntary basis until 1969. For the 1970 season, the Secretary received a small salary for his duties doing the summer season only. No pay for work done out of season.</em></p>

<p><em>The Artistic Directors from 1967 to 1970 received no salary for their work only accommodation found by the Trust during their stay.</em></p>

<p><em>Costs of running Theatre have risen considerably particularly higher wages for actors.</em></p>

<p>The ‘new members’ probably relates most to the incoming Director of Productions, Caroline Smith, who ran the company during summer 1971, having been recommended by Alan Ayckbourn who had previously worked with her at the Victoria Theatre, Stoke-on- Trent.</p>

<p>What is remarkable is how, even 16 years on, the theatre was still true to the founding principles of Stephen Joseph. Not just theatre-in-the-round and new playwriting, but also its reliance on the Scarborough community and its close links with the amateur dramatic community in the town; the theatre had been founded with voluntary help and without which it is abundantly clear the theatre would not have been able to survive even in 1971.<br><br>It’s perhaps sad that this third strand of Stephen Joseph’s legacy – forging astern links between the professional and amateur community – was subsequently diminished and, essentially, lost as the theatre moved towards an all-year round, fully professional company.</p>

<p>It’s also highlights an extraordinary fact – and one that perhaps had significant impact on the company in its modern era after Alan Ayckbourn retired as Artistic Director in 2009. That fact is that between 1955 and 2009, there is only one year – 1971 – when the Artistic Director / Director of Productions drew a salary! Essentially, for more than five decades, the most senior figure in the company chose not to draw a wage</p>

<p>Stephen Joseph – as far is known – never drew a salary between 1955 and 1965, neither did Rodney Wood nor Alan Ayckbourn as Directors of Productions between 1967 and 1970. And, famously, Alan Ayckbourn never drew his salary at any point between 1972 and 2009 as Artistic Director. Only Caroline Smith in 1971 was paid a salary of £35 a week.</p>

<p>Back to the report and an addendum makes it clear that the company was still not necessarily tied to Scarborough and could move wherever there was interest.l</p>

<p><em>[Scarborough Theatre Trust] could offer our money in the Building Fund to other local authorities who might be interested; we had not pledged ourselves to build a theatre in memory of Stephen Joseph in Scarborough.</em></p>

<p><em>The Theatre Trust was a professional one wanting to create a theatre centre to cater for all organisations professional for amateur connected to the arts.</em></p>

<p>Ever since 1961, Stephen Joseph had made it clear that Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre company was not beholden to Scarborough. In fact, he had never viewed it as a permanent home, just a launchpad and showcase for theatre-in-the-round which he hoped would find a permanent home elsewhere.</p>

<p>In 1965, when he had closed Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Stephen had made the point to Scarborough Theatre Trust that its sole purpose was to find a way to keep the company running even if it was not in Scarborough. As it was, the company had reopened in 1967 in the town, but four years on and Scarborough Theatre Trust obviously still did not feel they had the complete support of the town council and would authorise a move to another town willing to financially support the company.</p>

<p>A grim thought for theatre-lovers in the town.</p>

<p>The second document in archive is written by the theatre’s general manager / secretary Ken Boden and was intended for the new company – possibly as notes for a speech, possibly as a letter – and again emphasises the position of the theatre at the time.</p>

<p><em>The Theatre itself 90% belongs to the amateurs who do front of house, usherettes, shop and coffee.</em></p>

<p><em>The arrangement here in Scarborough between the professional and amateur is unique. We are tenants in this library and I have to make an agreement with the Librarian and the Cirporation [sic]. In the past I have always had difficulty but I hope there will be none this year.</em></p>

<p><em>The Librarian is strict and one of the staff here reports regularly to him any marks on walls or any damage whatsoever. Then the Librarian is on to me straight away and I am in trouble. I don’t like trouble and I don’t want any.</em></p>

<p><em>Our other Staff consist of Mr Houghton F of H [Front of house]. Ann Simpson Shop. PB [Mrs Pemberton Billing] Coffee. Connie Garlick, Hubert Ruff and Margaret Boden Box Office. Jon Boden – Lucky Bricks. We hope to have a new Theatre one day,</em></p>

<p><em>I am personally looking forward to an exciting season. I recently went on an Arts Council tour of Theatres and I was amazed to find that our little Theatre was known and what pleased me was the high reputation of our presentation and standard. I told Caroline [Smith] this weeks ago and I have every confidence that our reputation will be maintained.</em><br><br>Ken was actually wrong in suggesting the relationship between amateurs and professionals was unique to Scarborough as Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre’s sister theatre, the Victoria Theatre in Stoke-on-Trent, also had extremely close links to the community and the Front of House was run by volunteers.<br><br>And for those wondering, the’ Lucky Bricks’ was a fund-raising initiative for the proposed new theatre in which supporters could buy ‘bricks’ to contribute to the move.<br><br>This piece also highlighted the increasingly uncomfortable relationship between the company and Scarborough Library.  It does not imply a great deal of trust on either side and – in retrospect – much of the blame for that probably belongs to Ken. His abrasive and confrontational style of management had eroded the good relationship built up between Stephen Joseph and the library between 1955 and 1965 and when the Libraries Committee actively turned against the library several years later, it can’t have come as any real surprise.</p>

<p>However, despite the seemingly precarious and uncertain position of the company and the direction it was apparently taking, hope was on the horizon and unbeknownst to all concerned, 1972 would change everything.</p>

<p><em>Simon Murgatroyd (2025)</em></p>
