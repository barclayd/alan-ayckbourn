<p>Here on the Ayckbourn Blog, we’re celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in July 1955.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn was a vital and significant presence at <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> and these articles celebrate the plays he wrote for the company between 1959 and 1976. They offer an in-depth look at the creation of and background to some of the playwright’s most famous – and most obscure – works.</p>

<p>Alan’s fourth play – his last to be written under the pseudonym of Roland Allen – premiered in 1961 with <strong><em><a href="/plays/standing-room-only">Standing Room Only</a></em></strong>.</p>

<p><strong>Standing Room Only (1961)</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>If we had to chose a point in time when we can say, this is when Alan Ayckbourn first begins to emerge as an individual playwriting voice, then I firmly believe it’s in 1961 with his fourth play.</p>

<p>Alan had begun writing professionally in 1959 when Stephen Joseph commissioned him to write his first play, <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-square-cat">The Square Cat</a></strong></em>, a successful and well-constructed farce but which gave little indication of the direction the writer would go in the future.</p>

<p>It was followed by a second farce, <a href="/plays/love-after-all"><em><strong>Love After All</strong></em></a>, that same year – enlightening given that despite so many reports to the contrary, Alan considers he has written only one true farce subsequent to <em>Love After All</em> and definitely does not deserve the sobriquet of <em>farceur</em>.</p>

<p>This period comedy – with its borrowed and conventional plot – was something of a step backwards . His third play, <strong><em><a href="/plays/dads-tale">Dad’s Tale</a></em></strong> (1960) was, kindly put, a mess. Commissioned to be co-written and an adaptation with Alan having little interest in either, it ended up being solely written by Alan for a collaboration between the Scarborough theatre company and a Birmingham based ballet company.</p>

<p>As can be imagined, it is a piece which hangs together through Alan’s ingenuity as a playwright, but progressed him little as a writer and was a considerable flop for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>A scene from the world premiere of Standing Room Only at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, in 1961 (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>It is only with Alan’s fourth commission from Stephen Joseph that we began to see the playwright as we know him emerge with a piece that is the first to pick up several threads which will run throughout his career.</p>

<p>Ordinary people in extra-ordinary circumstances, the relationships between men and women, generational conflict as well the first introduction of speculative fiction into his writing. </p>

<p>The play was called <em><strong><a href="/plays/standing-room-only">Standing Room Only</a></strong></em> and it is significant in several other ways too. It stands as both the final play he wrote under his pseudonym of Roland Allen and the first as Alan Ayckbourn – for reasons to be revealed later. It marked the first time he would direct one of his own works and it was also the first of his plays to be optioned for both the West End and for television.</p>

<p>It marks a definite step forward for Alan and, arguably, the first time his authentic and original voice as a playwright is truly heard. This is despite the fact it is the only time that Alan accepted a suggestion for a plot from someone else. In this case, Stephen Joseph. He asked Alan to write a comedy about overpopulation with a strong political message attached, set on Venus which in itself was on the verge of over-population.</p>

<p>Alan had misgivings about this idea. In an interview with his biographer, Paul Allen, he suggests it might have been because “some commercial gene in me” saw a different and greater potential in the idea. Alternatively, as a fan of science fiction, it’s plausible he realised how preposterous Stephen’s idea was – certainly to a general theatre audience – and saw a way to make the same idea much more palatable and interesting as a play.</p>

<p>Whatever the case, Alan decided it was far more interesting to set it in somewhere recognisable and closer to home,  Shaftesbury Avenue in London – home to the theatrical West End. He largely ditched the overt political angle, although reading it from today’s perspective, some of the world-building is fascinatingly topical and would certainly now be viewed as political.</p>

<p><em>Standing Room Only</em> is set in London in the ‘far’ future of 2010 (or 1997 depending on the version of the script) – and was motivated by contemporary concerns about over-population. Gridlock has brought the UK to a standstill years earlier and nothing has moved on the roads since. Stuck in this is a double-decker bus, where the play is set, home to a family expecting an ‘illegal’ baby. An apparently authoritarian government has implemented draconian measures to control the population and the repercussions of overpopulation. Despite this, the play is, essentially, a gentle comedy about a family surviving despite the odds.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn as Nemo in the world premiere of Standing Room Only (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Although not as overtly science fiction as Stephen’s idea of setting the play on Venus, the play is definitely Ayckbourn’s first definable ‘genre’ play and reflected his youthful interest in the golden age novels of the great science fiction writers such as Isaac Asimov, Robert Heinlein and Ray Bradbury. As a young man, Alan Ayckbourn was a voracious speculative fiction reader and fond of the allegorical stories which influenced his later playwriting, as well as having fond memories of some of the classic ‘genre’ movies of the era.</p>

<p>Like his notable speculative fiction works which followed such as <em><strong><a href="/plays/henceforward">Henceforward…</a></strong></em>, <em><strong><a href="/plays/comic-potential">Comic Potential</a></strong></em> and <em><strong><a href="/plays/constant-companions">Constant Companions</a></strong></em>, the emphasis of the play is not the fantasy but the reality of the situation the family finds themselves in and the relationships between them all. Ultimately, like most of Ayckbourn’s work, the setting merely allows the playwright to explore his favourite pre-occupations. It is essentially a play about family and moral values; the speculative elements – even at this stage of his career – are not allowed to overwhelm the human story.</p>

<p>The play was premiered in Scarborough’s Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre on 13 July, 1961, and was the final Ayckbourn play to be premiered at the venue until&nbsp;<em>Meet My Father</em> (<em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></strong></em>), four years later in 1965. </p>

<p><em>Standing Room Only</em> was apparently the most successful production of the summer and led to the unusual step of extra performances being added at the end of the season. Directed by Stephen Joseph, it also featured Alan Ayckbourn in the role of the mysterious stranger Nemo (presumably a nod to Jules Verne’s famous creation from&nbsp;<em>20,000 Leagues Under The Sea</em>, whose name means ‘no-one’). The rest of the cast featured Hazel Burt, Rosamund Dickson, David Jarrett and Stanley Page.</p>

<p>The reviews were generally good, not least&nbsp;<em>The Stage</em>&nbsp;newspaper whose headline challenged “Is there a manager to drive this bus to Shaftesbury Avenue?” What was not obvious at the time was&nbsp;<em>The Stage</em>&nbsp;accepted submitted reviews and the review was written by an uncredited Joan Macalpine, then stage manager at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre!</p>

<p>The review was patently read by the upcoming theatre impresario Peter Bridge, who came to Scarborough to see the play and bought the rights to&nbsp;<em>Standing Room Only</em>&nbsp;circa September 1961 (according to a contemporary report from <em>Plays &amp; Players</em> magazine) with the intention of producing it in the West End in the autumn of the same year; this later being pushed back into 1963. This news was carried in the&nbsp;<em>Daily Telegraph</em>,&nbsp;<em>The Stage</em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em>Plays And Players</em>&nbsp;– largely due to the fact Peter Bridge was making a name for himself as a producer of new plays and high profile revivals in the West End.</p>

<p>Inadvertently, this also set into motion another vital part of Alan’s playwriting career moving forward: the literary agent Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay. Alan was lacking an agent when Peter Bridge made the approach and he introduced Alan to Peggy shortly after optioning the play. Despite having only written four plays, none of which had gone into the West End, Peggy took Alan on as a client. She would remain his agent until her death in 1991 and is one of the most significant figures in Alan Ayckbourn’s professional life and someone who he had enormous respect for.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>A scene from the world premiere of Standing Room Only (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>The play was also optioned for television acquired by ABC Television with a view to adapting it for the popular <em>Armchair Theatre</em> series, however – as would happen with the West End production – it never came to fruition.</p>

<p>Despite an inordinate amount of effort, the play did not transfer into the West End and become Alan’s first London triumph. After Peter Bridge had optioned it, he asked Alan for a number of changes to the script thus beginning a process which saw the script constantly re-written over the course of the following year.</p>

<p>The Ayckbourn Archive, held in the <strong><a href="https://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a></strong> at the University of York, holds more versions of distinctly altered&nbsp;<em>Standing Room Only</em>&nbsp;scripts than any other Ayckbourn play. There are several surviving drafts of proposed scripts for the West End which include additional characters, altered names, a different year for the action and increasingly ambitious and extravagant effects.</p>

<p>As Bridge began to court different stars for the play, he would ask Alan to amend the relevant charter appropriately to appeal to that actor; Alan notably remembers being asked to include more “rudes” or swear words when Peter was trying to convince Sid James of <em>Carry On…</em> fame to take a part.</p>

<p>Alan later noted that by the end of the process, it had become a tedious process. “I kept rewriting till I was heartily sick of the thing. Needless to say it finished up a total mess. I’ve hated re-writing ever since.” Despite all this work, Bridge would lose interest in the play by 1963 and it was never seen in the West End. Bridge, however, would go on to produce Alan’s first West End transfer <em><strong><a href="/plays/mr-whatnot">Mr Whatnot</a></strong></em> in 1964 – a notorious flop – as well as his first bona fide hit <em>Relatively Speaking</em> in 1967.</p>

<p>Perhaps because of the unpleasant associations with all the rewrites and how Bridge had transformed a play which had done so well in Scarborough. Alan went at it one last time, ejecting most of the West End nonsense and bringing it closer to the original production, but with slight alterations and more polish.</p>

<p>This was successfully revived at the <strong><a href="/career/victoria-theatre">Victoria Theatre</a></strong> in Stoke-on-Trent in April 1963; Alan having been one of the original members of the company when Stephen Joseph had founded the UK’s first professional theatre-in-the-round venues the previous year. Alan directed the play – marking the first time he would direct one of his own works – and he would later say that, despite the negative London experience, he was pleased with the revised version and his subsequent production of it. </p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>The 1963 revival of Standing Room Only at the Victoria Theatre (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>It also marked a significant change for Alan. When he had moved to the Victoria Theatre the previous year, it had become obvious that Alan’s professional career, which had begun as a stage manager and actor, was moving decisively towards playwriting and acting. To mark that change, his first new work for that venue, <em><strong><a href="/plays/christmas-v-mastermind">Christmas V Mastermind</a></strong></em>, had been credited to Alan Ayckbourn rather than his pseudonym Roland Allen.</p>

<p>When he revived <em>Standing Room Only</em> the following year, he also credited it to Alan Ayckbourn making it the earliest of his plays to be credited to his real name. As <em>Standing Room Only</em> was credited to Roland Allen on its first production in 1961 and to Alan Ayckbourn for its revival in 1963, it is both the latest play to be credited to Roland Allen and the earliest to Alan Ayckbourn!</p>

<p>Although Alan would withdraw <em>Standing Room Only</em> from production by the late 1960s, correspondence in archive indicates there was a lot of interest in reviving it and producing it by other theatres. So why was it never produced again? Particularly as Alan had expressed satisfaction with it.</p>

<p>The answer is, undoubtedly, <em>Relatively Speaking</em>. When it premiered in 1965, it was huge hit at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre and Peter Bridge optioned it with great confidence for the West End. Two years later, it opened to phenomenal success and made Alan a star. He rapidly became known for his situation comedies and hit after hit followed.</p>

<p><em>Standing Room Only</em>, despite its merits, was not that sort of play and nor was it ever as likely to be as well-received as <em>Relatively Speaking</em> and the plays which followed; its genre nature alone would have turned many people off at the time. So it was quietly withdrawn and largely forgotten.</p>

<p>It was only when, in 1987, Alan made the bold move of writing his ‘first’ speculative fiction work that was a undeniable hit with <em>Henceforward… </em>that <em>Standing Room Only</em> began to feel pertinent as a sign of the shape of things to come for Alan and his playwriting.</p>

<p>As I write this in 2025 with his 92nd play, <strong><em><a href="/plays/the-trial-of-romeo-oscar">The Trial of Romeo Oscar</a></em></strong>, confirmed as a speculative work set in the far, far future and with numerous other genre pieces behind him that, we can now see that <em>Standing Room Only</em> should be considered an essential part of his playwriting career for many significant reasons. </p>

<p><em>Simon Murgatroyd (2025)</em></p>
