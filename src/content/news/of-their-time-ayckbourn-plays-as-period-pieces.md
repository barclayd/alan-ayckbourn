<p>One of the most frequent requests I receive as Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist is from directors looking to update and, consequently, alter Alan’s play texts.</p>

<p>It’s a perplexing issue because, on the one hand, it’s always good to see new interest in staging Alan’s work. But, on the other hand, it shows a fundamental misunderstanding of the playwright and his plays.</p>

<p>Put simply, you can’t update the majority of Alan’s plays because a) they generally don’t work in a modern setting and b) the playwright himself abhors it. As he recently responded with regard to one proposed dramatic reinvention of one of his most famous works, if a director wants something so different from a play, why don’t they write their own play rather than messing with other writers’ work?</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>The world premiere of Bedroom Farce (1975)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Some authors you can re-invent, obviously – the longevity of Shakespeare is testament to that. His plays are vehicles ripe for re-interpretation and re-invention. But the majority of Alan’s plays are not written like this. As the playwright himself notes.</p>

<p><em>“I don’t write timeless plays – unlike Shakespeare – but plays very much of their time. Social records. That’s what life was like back in those days.”</em></p>

<p>This was a point which Sir Peter Hall, former Artistic Director of the National Theatre, understood when he talked about Alan Ayckbourn and the significance of his work on numerous occasions.</p>

<p><em>“If, in a hundred years, anyone wants to know what it was like to live in the second half of the 20th Century, I am quite sure they will turn to the plays of Alan Ayckbourn before they look at historians or sociologists…</em></p>

<p><em>“If people want to know about life in England in the Sixties, Seventies and Eighties, they will need to study his [Alan Ayckbourn’s] plays.”</em></p>

<p>This much is obvious from a close reading of many of Alan Ayckbourn’s most revered works such as <strong><em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></em></strong> (1965), <em><strong><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves">How The Other Half Loves</a></strong></em> (1969), <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em> (1972), <strong><em><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></em></strong> (1973), <strong><em><a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a></em></strong> (1974) and <em><strong><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></strong></em> (1975). The characters and their lives reflect what it was like to live in the ’60s and ’70s. They are, quite obviously, products of their time.</p>

<p>Alan recently spoke about the issues with <em>Absent Friends</em> and a production where it had to be explained to the company why none of the women were employed in the play. That this wasn’t an aberration or something unusual – that this was the social norm in the early 1970s in a country on the cusp of huge changes, where the role of women in society would massively change in subsequent years.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Christopher Godwin in the world premiere of The Norman Conquests</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>You can’t perform <em>Absent Friends</em> in an updated or modern setting because of this – it would look and feel awkward because the characters have been taken out of their original social context. They reflect life in 1974, not life in 2024.</p>

<p>The attitudes found in the plays also reflect those of the times. The discomfort we may find today with some of the attitudes shown in plays like <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> merely reflects the social norms of the ’70s. Thankfully, we have – largely – moved on from these attitudes. But this does mean that to put these plays in a modern setting either means keeping attitudes and language which do not reflect those of today’s society or to re-write or even redact sections of the text to make it plausible for a 2024 setting.</p>

<p>This is unacceptable because as soon as you start re-writing or redacting, you begin to alter who the characters fundamentally are and how they were written and also to change the very nature of the play into something it was never intended to be.</p>

<p>By altering the plays, we change the perception of the characters and, essentially, remove the fact these plays are a social document of their time. </p>

<p>Of course, there is another elephant in the room. Technology or the lack thereof. You just can’t update many of the plays from the 1960s through to the 1980s because the fact mobile phones aren’t ‘grafted’ to people’s hands becomes immediately apparent.</p>

<p>Think of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>  or <em>Bedroom Farce</em> set in today’s world. Both plays would end before the conclusion of the first scene thanks to the ubiquitous nature of the mobile phone. Not a very satisfactory evening at the theatre!</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>The world premiere of Absurd Person Singular (1972)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Equally, even more modern plays such as <strong><em><a href="/plays/gameplan">GamePlan</a></em></strong> (2001) would struggle to be updated as their technological references – this is a play whose central premise involves the internet – are now dated. The internet and how we use it has moved on appreciably in the past two decades, so even plays with technology – the more recent and modern plays – have to still be treated as period works. <em>GamePlan</em> is a piece of the early 2000s, it doesn’t really work were set it unaltered in 2024.</p>

<p>Of course, there are exceptions within the play canon. The sci-fi plays can be updated – <em><strong><a href="/plays/communicating-doors">Communicating Doors</a></strong></em> (1994) and <em><strong><a href="/plays/henceforward">Henceforward…</a></strong></em> (1987) have both had alterations permitted to the scripts, but that’s to reflect the fact they are set in the future and what was the future when they were written is frequently history a couple of decades on. </p>

<p>But there’s also the issue that plays which seemed fantastical when they were written, such as <em><strong><a href="/plays/comic-potential">Comic Potential</a></strong></em> (1998), now can look to be just a commentary on the present day. Less than a quarter off a century on and AI written television scripts is an all too pertinent and familiar issue. Indeed when he wrote <em><strong><a href="/plays/constant-companions">Constant Companions</a></strong></em> (2023), a play centred on androids and artificial intelligence gaining the ability to lie and love, Alan noted this.</p>

<p><em>“In Arthur C. Clarke or Isaac Asimov’s day, it was pretty astonishing they foresaw quite as much as they did. It took several decades before some of the things they foresaw happened. Now, it’s sometimes several days!This play could go from sci-fi in two years to a standard domestic comedy!”</em></p>

<p>Certain plays are so loosely defined they can probably get away with being set today without altering the dialogue – <em><strong><a href="/plays/haunting-julia">Haunting Julia</a></strong></em> (1994) or <em><strong><a href="/plays/snake-in-the-grass">Snake in the Grass</a></strong></em> (2002) perhaps. Interestingly the plays with a fantastic element possibly lend themselves to modern settings. But even then, it’s not uniform. You can’t set <strong><em><a href="http://thegirlnextdoor.alanayckbourn.net">The Girl Next Door</a></em></strong> (2021) in anything but 2020 as it’s meant to reflect life during the Covid lockdowns.</p>

<p>There have been arguments that plays such as <em>The Norman Conquests</em> or <strong><em><a href="/plays/house-and">House &amp; Garden</a></em></strong> (1999) are essentially timeless. But they’re not. When you look at them and some of the situations and attitudes on display, they are not comfortable fits with the modern day. Norman is a man of his time and all the better for it.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn’s plays have always reflected what he sees in the world around him and this makes them, by their very nature, time if not place specific. He writes about what he sees in society and around him today. From the moment they are written, they are period works ideally suited to the year in which they were written.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>The world premiere of Absent Friends (1974)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>To best appreciate them and to do best service to them in production, this should always be appreciated.</p>

<p>Whether this will become a bar to long term posterity for the plays remains to be seen. <em>Relatively Speaking</em> is still performed frequently despite being 60 years old next year and is rarely performed as anything but a piece of the ’60s. But only time will tell whether the larger play canon will achieve that long-term standing and be performed in the decades to come.</p>

<p>But to return to the beginning, there is also the question of why anyone should feel the need to alter a play?</p>

<p>To read an Ayckbourn play – or any other playwright for that matter – and to have understood and appreciated it, it should be obvious if that work is of a certain time and period. Does altering those fundamentals essentially change the nature of the play and the intentions of the playwright?</p>

<p>Why would you want to do that? For then, surely, it is no longer an Ayckbourn work. Just an unsatisfactory hybrid, not as the playwright intended and almost certainly not guaranteed to work.</p>

<p>If the existing play doesn’t reflect what you want to say, better to write or commission it yourself, whilst leaving Alan Ayckbourn’s original works to be produced as they should be. And by doing so, offering a fantastic insight and social document into the state of our lives in the UK from the 1960s to the 2020s.</p>

<p><em>Simon Murgatroyd, April 2024</em></p>
