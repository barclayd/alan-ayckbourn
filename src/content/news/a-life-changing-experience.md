<p>On 14 April 1957, Alan Ayckbourn had inarguably a life-changing experience.</p>

<p>As an 18 year old aspiring <a href="/career/actor">actor and stage manager</a> working at <a href="/career/actor/connaught-theatre">Leatherhead Theatre Club</a>, Alan found himself on a trip to London to see a young Scarborough-based company which was also the UK’s first professional theatre-in-the-round company. </p>

<p>It was an experience that not only opened his eyes to a form of theatre he had never experienced before, but which would shape his entire future as, from that summer forward, his life and career would indelibly be associated with that Scarborough company.</p>

<p>In Ian Watson’s excellent – but, sadly, long-since out of print, work <em><a href="https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/offer-listing/0571151922/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&amp;camp=1634&amp;creative=6738&amp;creativeASIN=0571151922&amp;linkCode=am2&amp;tag=alanaycksoffi-21&amp;linkId=IX2FLYKM4UTPLUDV">Conversations With Ayckbourn</a></em> – Alan talks about his formative experience with theatre-in-the-round.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>The Library Theatre, Scarborough (circa 1956): home to the UK’s first professional theatre-in-the-round company<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p><strong>Conversations With Ayckbourn</strong> <em>by Ian Watson</em></p>

<p><strong>Ian Watson:</strong> Had you been working towards this? Had you been yearning for the acting?</p>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn:</strong> I was looking for the acting, yes. I was a stage manager [at Leatherhead Theatre Club] and waiting, biding my time. And I played out the rest of that year. I did quite a lot of acting… lt was weekly rep again, but I worked with a lot of good people there. Actors are quite<br>generous people, really: they do tend to help. Sometimes it’s terribly misguided help, but<br>they’re always there to say: ‘If you take my tip kid, don’t enter that way, it doesn’t look so<br>good.’ So l was learning, I was still a long way from the technical side, but the Stage Manager<br>there was a man called Rodney Wood; and at the end of that season, Rodney Wood, who<br>thought for some reason that I wasn’t bad at my ASM job, asked me and a part-time<br>carpenter who was there if we’d like to come to Scarborough and form the stage team there,<br>with me as stage manager and him as ASM. And that was the moment that I got to hear of<br><a href="http://thesjt.alanayckbourn.net/styled-49/steohenjoseph.html">Stephen Joseph</a> and the theatre-in-the-round. And one Sunday, we went up to town [London] and we had a look at some of this man’s work, although l wasn’t to meet the man himself for quite a<br>long time. We went to the Mahatma Gandhi Hall <em>[1]</em> and saw a production of <em>Huis Clos</em> <em>[2]</em>, which<br>sticks out still in my mind as one of the most exciting things I’ve ever seen in the theatre.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Rodney Wood &amp; Alan Ayckbourn in 1957 at the Library Theatre, Scarborough<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p><strong>Ian Watson:</strong> Was this the first time you’d encountered theatre-in-the-round?</p>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn:</strong> Yes. It was an absolute knockout. It was a pretty racy play, for its time, you know.<br>And I thought: ‘This is terrific.’ I also liked it because it had no scenery, and that meant less<br>work. So we rehearsed in London that first season: again I was acting because it was a small<br>enough company. The late Clive Goodwin was directing, and there were two actors, two<br>actresses – Clive also acted later, and Rodney then directed. Stephen had very little to do<br>with that season: he didn’t direct. <em>[3]</em> And I stage-managed with John, and there was an<br>Assistant Stage Manager, called Anne Taylor. We did our two weeks, or whatever it was in London, we went up to <a href="http://thesjt.alanayckbourn.net/page1/styled/">Scarborough</a> and we started playing; and there was still no sign of this Stephen Joseph man. That was a great summer. I did the lights, John did the sound – that’s how it started.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Alan Ayckbourn &amp; John Smith stage-managing at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, during 1958<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>And eventually, Stephen turned up, this amazing bloke: he introduced himself, and he<br>was obviously a man much more interested anyway in the technical side of the theatre than<br>in the acting side. So we hit it off immediately: he liked our end much better. We spent a lot of time chatting about sound and how to improve it. It was all rather new, we were in the<br>pioneering days of tape recorders. It all seemed as though it was going to be a rather brief,<br>one-season affair.</p>

<p><em>(Extract from Conversations with Ayckbourn, Faber, 1981 © Ian Watson)</em></p>

<p>Of course, what Alan thought was going to be a one-season affair turned into something that 63 years on is still going strong as Alan stayed returned to the Library Theatre in 1958, began writing for the company in 1959 and directing 1961 – both under the guidance and encouragement of Stephen Joseph. Following a brief hiatus to the <a href="/career/victoria-theatre">Victoria Theatre</a>, Stoke-on-Trent, and the <a href="/career/bbc-career">BBC</a> between 1962 and 1970 (during which time he still worked with the Scarborough company), Alan returned to the company becoming <a href="/career/artistic-director">Artistic Director</a> from 1972 until 2009. Since then he has guest-directed and continues to premiere his work at, what is now, the <a href="http://thesjt.alanayckbourn.net/page1/styled-3/">Stephen Joseph Theatre</a>.</p>

<p><strong>Footnotes</strong></p>

<p>[1] Stephen Joseph never intended to set up a permanent theatre company in Scarborough. He intended to found a theatre-in-the-round company promoting new writing in London, but was unable to find appropriate or affordable premises. By a bizarre turn of events, he instead found himself opening the company in Scarborough (this is explored further <a href="http://thesjt.alanayckbourn.net/page7/styled-33/">here</a>). But this was never intended to be a permanent home, Stephen was constantly seeking ways to move his work to London or to a town which would support a purpose-built theatre-in-the-round venue. As a result of this, Stephen set up the Studio Theatre Club, which would tour on Sundays from 1955 to 1957, to the Mahatma Gandhi Assembly Hall, 41 Fitzroy Square,  London, and perform theatre-in-the-round productions with the aim of drawing attention to his new project. Ultimately, the club failed and Stephen never achieved his ambition of finding a home for theatre-in-the-round in London.</p>

<p>[2] This was actually one of the first performances of Sartre’s famed play <em>Huis Clos</em> (also known as <em>In Camera</em> or <em>Vicious Circle</em> at the time and, more commonly subsequently, as <em>No Exit</em>) which had its London debut in 1946, directed by Peter Brook. The Studio Theatre Club’s performance was directed by David de Kayser and performed at the Mahatma Gandhi Hall on 3 February and 14 April 1957. Stephen Joseph felt it was a play which benefited greatly from theatre-in-the-round production and he would also direct it at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, during the <a href="http://thesjt-plays-50s.alanayckbourn.net/styled/page1/page57/page57.html">winter 1959</a> season.</p>

<p>[3] There’s no recorded reason why Stephen Joseph didn’t direct the summer 1957 at the Library Theatre – he would direct most of that year’s winter season productions though. Alan would be directed by Stephen for the first time during the summer 1958 season when he played Constable Williams in Stephen’s production of Frederick Knott’s <em>Dial M For Murder</em>.</p>
