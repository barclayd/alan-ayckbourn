<p>Today marks the 25th anniversary of the world premiere of Alan Ayckbourn’s acclaimed diptych <em><a href="/plays/house-and"><strong>House &amp; Garden</strong></a></em>.</p>

<p>The plays premiered at the Stephen Joseph Theatre, Scarborough, on 17 June 1999 – a month after Alan’s 60th birthday and intended by the playwright as a present to himself!</p>

<p>The plays famously take place simultaneously in two auditoria with one cast moving between the two spaces. What is off-stage in one auditorium is on-stage in the other. It is a marvellous ‘coup de théâtre’ and – as far as the playwright believes – is the first time anyone had written a work of this nature.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn’s Official Website has various articles and interviews with the playwright about <strong><em><a href="/plays/house-and">House &amp; Garden</a></em></strong>, but to mark the anniversary, the blog has an exclusive piece of never before published correspondence in which Alan offers his thoughts on the plays, how they came to be and the reaction to them.</p>

<p>You can also join the playwright’s archivist, Simon Murgatroyd, in celebrating the play’s anniversary with a talk in the Italian Gardens on Scarborough’s South Cliff on Tuesday 9 July at 2pm. Tickets are free and further details can be found <strong><a href="https://www.eventbrite.co.uk/e/alan-ayckbourns-house-and-garden-at-25-a-talk-with-simon-murgatroyd-tickets-903093465867?aff=oddtdtcreator">here</a></strong>.</p>

<p><strong><em>House &amp; Garden</em></strong> by Sir Alan Ayckbourn</p>

<p>I wrote them simultaneously. I had to. And I had to know, obviously, where people were going. I asked my stage manager, “Would you, one day when you’ve got a moment, walk not-too-fast between The McCarthy (the upstairs theatre) and The Round (the downstairs theatre) and tell me how long it takes.”</p>

<p>And she came back to me and said, “It takes about 37 seconds.” So I said, “And that’s walking slowly?” And she said “Yes,” so I said, “OK, so if I make it a minute,” – I think I made it one minute fifteen – “then an actor leaving the stage in The McCarthy should be able to reach the stage in The Round in time to come on for a different scene in that play.”</p>

<p>So it’s truly simultaneous action, taking place in two auditoria with actors moving between the two quite a lot of the time. It was exciting because we were nervous and I didn’t leave a lot of leeway for mistakes. We had to keep the plays very closely technically linked together.</p>

<p>And, we can say it now, we never had anybody off. Nobody was ever not on. Nobody missed an entrance. Which says something for the actors playing it. They were very precise. We had television monitors in the wings so you could see on the video monitor where the other play was in the other theatre, and you could tell, after a bit, you had to move.</p>

<p>But it had a serious intent. How we are all at some point walk-ons in other people’s lives. Characters that are the main parts in one play became quite small parts in the other.</p>

<p>It was fun to do. It was very important, too, that the plays finished together because the curtain calls had to happen simultaneously in both theatres with the actors – quite a lot them – about thirty in all, including children, moving pretty speedily between the auditoria.</p>

<p>Finally, because it was about an English garden fête with games and selling jams and all sorts of things, we had a fête in the foyer. So when the audience came out they became part of an informal third act. The actors joined them and sold them books and food and goodies, they played games, the audience played with the actors and it became a huge sort of party.</p>

<p>I said to the cast up here one day, “Listen, this will never be done again because this is a special theatre with these two auditoria. Nobody else will be mad enough to do it and besides, it’s crazy.”</p>

<p>But it was fun and the audience thought it was fun. They loved it. They kept coming back. You had to come twice to see it all, anyway.</p>

<p>On the very last day, Trevor Nunn, who was at the time running the National Theatre, came and saw the matinee of <em>House</em> and the evening show of <em>Garden</em>. At the end of <em>Garden</em> he came out into the foyer where all these games were going on, all the fun, and he was completely swept away by it, he loved it. And he let me do it at the National, which was lovely because I had the Lyttelton and the Olivier theatres to play with, which are huge compared with this place [the Stephen Joseph Theatre].</p>

<p>I had an audience everyday of 2000 people. We had a large garden fête in the foyer with much bigger resources. We had a band, and we had games, and it was great. We took the National Theatre over really for month or so in the summer of 2000. And it was packed; it was a terrific success.</p>

<p>It’s my belief very much that theatre sometimes, not all the time, has to be an event. It has to be something special. People stop going, they get bored with it, or they get used to it. Or they think they’ve been going when they haven’t for ages so I like to intrigue them. To make them say, “Hey, that sounds interesting, that sounds fun, I want to go and see that.”</p>

<p><em>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>
