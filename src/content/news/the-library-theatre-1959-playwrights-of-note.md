<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1959 and the emergence of two major playwriting talents.</p>

<p><strong>1959: Playwrights of Note</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>One of the aims of the blog this year is to celebrate the significance of Scarborough Library to the history of theatre in the round in the town itself and UK theatre as a whole.</p>

<p>It’s easy to forget that professional theatre in the round in the UK began on the first floor of Scarborough Library in 1955 and that over the next two decades, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre gained national prominence as one of the UK’s leading provincial, new writing companies.</p>

<p>There are a number of significant theatrical milestones which took place in the library – two of which took place in 1959 and emphasise just why Scarborough Library plays a significant part in 20th century British theatre history.</p>

<p>In that year, two playwrights featured who would go on to have a phenomenal impact on British theatre. One is almost synonymous with Scarborough now, the other’s connection to the town is barely known.</p>

</div>

<p>The playwrights were <strong><a href="/">Alan Ayckbourn</a></strong> and Harold Pinter.</p>

<p>On 3 January 1959, the <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-40/">Studio Theatre Ltd</a></strong> company finished its winter season at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough. It had been a great success and must have been a source of pride to the company’s founder, Stephen Joseph, that the winter seasons – entirely reliant on home audiences rather than the tourists prevalent during the summer – had been so well-supported. As such, they paid back his faith in Scarborough and his belief theatre in the round and new writing could and would be adopted by the local community.</p>

<p>Following this, a short tour was arranged featuring a repertory of plays drawn from the previous winter and summer season as well as a couple of new productions.</p>

<p>The highlight of this was the debut play of a young playwright called Harold Pinter. The play,&nbsp;<em>The Birthday Party</em>, had opened in the West End in 1958 and been a disaster closing after just eight performances.</p>

<p>Stephen Joseph, who had taught Pinter at the Central School Of Speech And Drama, read the play noting it “baffled me, but it also attracted me strongly.” Keen to encourage innovative new work such as this, he contacted Pinter with an invitation to direct the play himself.<br><br>Pinter agreed, despite budget limitations and having to cast within the existing Scarborough company. Stephen scheduled the play as part of the three month tour, to be rehearsed during the company’s short winter season at Scarborough’s Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.<br><br>There, in Scarborough, Pinter came into contact with Alan Ayckbourn, an actor who had joined the company the previous year and who had not yet started writing professionally. He was given the role of Stanley but initially thought Pinter’s script was “absolute gobbledegook”.<br><br>As did the company, but Stephen Joseph encouraged them to bear with Pinter, convinced the playwright had something important to say and that he could show “the actors how to do the action, thus giving even the most baffling parts of the play a conviction and organic logic of their own.”<br><br>Still, there was always that ‘baffling’ aspect and Alan famously attempted to gain some insight into his role. Pinter’s reply was probably one of Alan’s more memorable acting notes: “I remember asking Pinter, what can you tell me about him that will give me more understanding? And Harold just said, ‘Mind your own f***ing business. Concentrate on what’s there.’”<br><br>Pinter resolutely believed everything an actor needed to know was in the script – a path which Alan himself would later follow. The company kept faith with the director and despite Alan’s belief the play “was completely mad, new and weird”, Pinter’s passion won over the actors.<br><br>Once the Scarborough season had finished at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre and rehearsals were complete, the company embarked on its regional winter repertory tour with&nbsp;<em>The Birthday Party</em>&nbsp;opening in Birmingham and later playing in Leicester. The play was a moderate financial success but Stephen Joseph was convinced it was “instrumental in getting television companies interested in the author.”<br><br>The effect on the company and audience was more profound. For Alan it was an eye-opening experience.</p>

<p>“The play made no sense and we didn’t understand it – until we went on stage and we just electrified the audience. We just came off stage and stared at Pinter!”<br><br>Pinter would go on to become a significant inspiration and influence on Alan’s playwriting career – who notably wrote his first play,&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>, during the same tour. Pinter also later said the production had restored his faith in the play and his own abilities. The rest was history.</p>

<p>Whilst rehearsing <em>The Birthday Party</em> at Scarborough Library, another playwriting phenomenon was about to be created.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn was 19 in 1958 and had joined the Scarborough company the previous year as a stage manager with the intention of becoming an actor, but was already uncertain about his future. During the winter season he appeared in a play called&nbsp;<em>Ring Of Roses</em>&nbsp;by David Campton and had neither enjoyed the role nor the experience. He challenged Stephen Joseph why he wasn’t getting better roles.</p>

<p>Stephen, ever the provocateur, turned this on its head and replied that if Alan wanted better roles, he should write them himself. He challenged Alan to write his own play promising he would stage it if it was any good and that he should also write himself the main role; a clever suggestion as who was going to write themselves a poor part?</p>

<p>Alan and his fiancée, the actress Christine Roland, thus went off on the Studio Theatre winter tour with the challenge ringing in Alan’s ears. On this, as noted, he was working with 28 year old Harold Pinter whose work was an inspiration and revelation to the budding writer.</p>

<p>During the course of the tour, Alan and Christine begin writing a play for Stephen Joseph under the pseudonym Roland Allen. It’s been postulated this was to distinguish between Alan’s acting and writing careers; which is unlikely as it was common knowledge from the start that Alan was the author of all the Roland Allen plays.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn &amp; Christine Roland in 1959 (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>The real reason for the pseudonym was far simpler; the play –&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/the-square-cat">The Square Cat</a></strong></em>&nbsp;– was a joint effort between Alan and Christine and the pseudonym reflected that (Christine’s maiden name and a variation on Alan’s christian name). This was no secret as practically every newspaper report on the couple noted how they were both writing their first play together.</p>

<p>Alan had already been trying his hand at writing plays – although none had been produced – and apparently already had a clear nascent gift for dialogue and ideas. Christine was apparently very good at creating structure and she provided the framework for the play, around which Alan would write the dialogue.</p>

<p>Thus&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat&nbsp;</em>was born, credited as Alan Ayckbourn’s first professionally produced play but actually co-written by Alan and – by then – his wife, who he had married after completing the play.</p>

<p>Given the significance of the play in Alan and the Scarborough company’s history, it’s interesting to note its production was not a surefire certainty. Although&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>&nbsp;would prove to be enormously popular and the second most profitable play of the year, when Stephen Joseph told Scarborough’ Library’s Chief Librarian’d Director of Libraries, Museums and Art Gallery – Mervyn Edwards – about the forthcoming summer season in mid-March, there was not a mention of&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>; which probably hadn’t been finished at that point.</p>

<p>It’s only when the posters for the season were printed – approximately a month before the season – that&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>&nbsp;is mentioned.</p>

<p>The play, starring Alan Ayckbourn in the dual role of pop-star Jerry Wattis and his mild- mannered alter-ego Arthur Brummage, was a huge success; although it didn’t feature Christine who was, by this point, pregnant with their first child, Steven.</p>

<p>The play’s success led to it becoming the first play to run for two consecutive weeks at the Library Theatre when Stephen pulled a week of the far less well received&nbsp;<em>Frankenstein</em>&nbsp;adaptation by David Campton. Immediately after the season ended, Stephen also commissioned Alan to write a second play which was premiered during the winter season later that year and was called&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/love-after-all">Love After All</a></strong></em>.</p>

<p>All of which makes 1959 a remarkable year for the company. Here we have two playwrights who will go onto extraordinary success staging their first plays with a little- know provincial theatre company.</p>

<p>Alongside this, there is little doubt that working with Pinter was a huge inspiration to Ayckbourn in both his efforts to write his first professional play, but also later as a fully- fledged playwright who has always credited Pinter’s influence on him in writing dialogue.</p>

<p>And above all this, we have Stephen Joseph, an Artistic Director who saw the potential in both writers. He encouraged Pinter to keep on going after what must have been a traumatic experience in the West End and recognised a nascent talent in Alan Ayckbourn, encouraging him to write and immediately capitalising on the success of his first play.</p>

<p>Arguably, Stephen’s actions also unwittingly had huge long term consequences. By encouraging Alan as playwright and, later, director, he unknowingly laid the seeds for the company’s prolonged success and survival.</p>

<p>And, by nurturing Pinter and Ayckbourn, he played a vital part in the formative years of two of the most successful British playwriting talents of the second half of the 20th century.</p>
