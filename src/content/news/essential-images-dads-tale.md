<p><em>Essential Images is a regular feature which looks at each of Alan Ayckbourn’s plays through a single, significant photograph from its original production.</em></p>

<p><strong>Essential Images: Dad’s Tale&nbsp;</strong>by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>It’s fair to say that one of the great questions to stump even the most ardent Ayckbourn fan is: What is the name of the ballet / play written by Alan Ayckbourn? </p>

<p>In the realms of Ayckbourn obscurity, we’re scraping the deepest of barrels with his 1960 creation, <em><a href="/plays/dads-tale">Dad’s Tale</a></em>. A play which was produced just once before being consigned to the vaults, never to be seen again (unless you happen to visit the <a href="http://biography.alanayckbourn.net/page-12/page-13/">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a> at the University of York, where you can read a copy held in the Ayckbourn Archive).</p>

<p>Coming from a ballet background myself, it’s always been a play I’ve had a fondness for within the Ayckbourn play canon – although I’d never actually want to see it performed! And there are a couple of interesting surviving images from the playwright’s only foray into ballet.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption><sub>Rosalind Dickson as Auntie and Tonya Burcka (TBC) as a dancing Christmas Tree in Dad’s Tale (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>This image sees Rosamund Dickson of the Studio Theatre Ltd company, based in Scarborough, with Tonya Burcka (TBC), of the British Dance Drama Theatre, based in Birmingham. I mention where the companies were based only because that is also where each rehearsed until the dress rehearsal when both met in Scarborough for the first time!</p>

<p>As one of the few shots of A) <em>Dad’s Tale</em> and B) a member of the British Dance Drama Theatre in Dad’s Tale, it’s a fascinating glimpse into a play which really shouldn’t exist (and that the dancer in the photo had a very open fifth position of the feet).</p>

<p>For Alan Ayckbourn thought he was co-writing an adaptation of Mary Norton’s beloved children’s book <em>The Borrowers</em> when commissioned in 1960. Except his co-writer – David Campton – quit the project, Alan had never actually read <em>The Borrowers</em> and Studio Theatre Ltd’s Artistic Director, Stephen Joseph, had forgotten to mention it was a co-production with a ballet company…</p>

<p>Unperturbed (or possibly very perturbed, Alan has never actually clarified his feelings on the matter), the playwright began constructing a play which had to allow for integrated ballet scenes and the fact the lack of budget meant the two companies had to rehearse 162 miles apart until the day before the production opened.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption><sub>Another of the ballet scenes within Dad’s Tale with Robert Horrobin (TBC) sporting the most magnificent side-burns to ever grace an Ayckbourn production (© Haydonning Ltd).</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Alan cleverly worked around this by writing a play about a poor family at Christmas which was punctuated at regular intervals by dream sequences of imagined better times. These were the ballet sections and – aside from a minimal amount of interaction with the character whose dream it was – they were essentially entirely separate and could just be dropped into the action at the relevant point.</p>

<p>Such ingenuity was, sadly, not appreciated by an audience which singularly failed to turn up. This was the Scarborough company’s first attempt at a Christmas play and it opened at the Library Theatre the day after schools broke up for the holiday. As any theatre knows, school parties are essential to your Christmas show’s success. This had neither school parties to visit nor apparently many children full-stop.</p>

<p>After his two early hits of <em><a href="/plays/the-square-cat">The Square Cat</a></em> and <em><a href="/plays/love-after-all">Love After All</a></em>, his third play brought Alan back to Earth with a bang and definitely convinced him there was no place for ballet within his writing. It probably also made him question whether he should just stick to acting at this point.</p>

<p>It’s a play which has very little to contribute to the Ayckbourn canon aside from four notable-ish points: Alan was still acting at this time and still using his plays as a showcase for his talents. In the case of <em>Dad’s Tale</em>, he played several – as he recalls – equally unmemorable roles ; it was directed by Clifford Williams, who would go on to considerably more success with the Royal Shakespeare Company; it marked the debut with the Scarborough company of the Australian actor Stanley Page, who Alan would work frequently with in the future including the world premieres of <em><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></em> and <em><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></em>; it’s the first Ayckbourn play to break the fourth wall (or whatever the in-the-round equivalent is) and, it has been argued, is possibly one of the earliest examples of a play which presents a single event from multiple conflicting viewpoints. </p>

<p>None of these are really enough to warrant further attention of the play though!</p>

<p>However, it’s a play which is always going to have some interest given it marked Alan’s first and only foray into the realms of ballet. For which I – if no-one else – am grateful that we have an essential image of the dancers.</p>
