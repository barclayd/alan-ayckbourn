<p><em>Welcome back to our ongoing exploration of significant events in the history of Alan Ayckbourn’s home theatre, the Stephen Joseph Theatre in Scarborough. We’ve reach 1959 and a turning point not only for there SJT but, arguably, 20th century British Theatre.</em></p>

<p><em>As chronicled previously, Alan Ayckbourn joined the Library Theatre in Scarborough two years previously in 1957 as an aspiring <a href="/career/actor">actor</a>. In 1959, he would take the first steps on the path we are most familiar with, but not before an extraordinary confluence brought him into contact with another extraordinary playwriting talent.</em></p>

<p><strong>1959: Making History</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>There are certain significant years in the history of the Stephen Joseph Theatre: the year the company was founded (1955), <a href="/life/stephen-joseph">Stephen Joseph</a>’s death and the re-launch of the company (1967), the move to the Stephen Joseph Theatre (1996) among others.</p>

<p>But one of the most significant years has to be 1959 and with it a tale of two playwrights who would go on to have a phenomenal impact on British theatre during the 20th century and beyond.</p>

<p>On 3 January 1959, the Studio Theatre company finished the winter season at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, which had been a great success; it must have been a source of pride to the company’s founder, Stephen Joseph, that the winter seasons – entirely reliant on home audiences rather than the tourists prevalent during the summer – had been so well-supported, paying back his faith in Scarborough and the believe that theatre-in-the-round could and would be adopted by the local community.</p>

<p>Following this, a short tour was arranged featuring a repertory of plays drawn from the previous winter and summer season as well as a couple of new productions.</p>

<p>Most interesting of which was the debut play of a young playwright called Harold Pinter. The play,&nbsp;<em><a href="/career/actor/the-birthday-party">The Birthday Party</a></em>, had opened in the West End in 1958 and been a disaster closing after just eight performances.</p>

<p>Stephen Joseph knew of Pinter from his work at the Central School of Speech and Drama and he invited Pinter to come to Scarborough to direct the play himself; the only provisos being he had to draw the cast from the existing company and the production was to be in- the-round. Other than that, he had free rein.</p>

<p><em>The Birthday Party</em>&nbsp;was then performed in Birmingham and Leicester as part of the winter / spring tour. Of note was the production featured Alan Ayckbourn in the role of Stanley; Alan has frequently noted how inspiring working with Pinter was.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>The only known image from Pinter’s production of The Birthday Party with Alan Ayckbourn (left) as Stanley.<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Reactions to the production were very positive and Pinter later said that the experience – marking his professional directing debut – had restored his faith in both the play and his own abilities as a writer. The rest, as they say is history, as Pinter went on to what we might term a modicum of success!</p>

<p>Back in the play itself though was the 19 year old Ayckbourn, who was already uncertain about his future as an actor. During the winter season, he had appeared in a play called&nbsp;<em>Ring Of Roses</em>&nbsp;by David Campton and had not enjoyed the role nor the experience. He challenged Stephen Joseph why he wasn’t getting better roles.</p>

<p>Stephen, ever the provocateur, turned this on its head and replied that if Alan wanted better roles, he should write them himself. He challenged Alan to write his own play promising he would stage it if it was any good and that he should also write himself the main role; a clever suggestion as who was going to write themselves a poor part?</p>

<p>Alan and his fiancée, the actress Christine Roland, thus went off on the winter / spring tour with the challenge ringing in Alan’s ears. And at the same time he was working with the 28 year old Pinter who had written and directed a play which was undoubtedly exciting, pushing boundaries in theme and writing and completely unlike anything Alan nor any of the actors had experienced before.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Alan Ayckbourn &amp; Christine Roland<br>(© Haydonning Ltd)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>It was a huge inspiration to the budding writer.</p>

<p>During the course of the tour, Alan and Christine begin writing a play for Stephen Joseph under the pseudonym Roland Allen. It’s been postulated this was to distinguish between Alan’s acting and writing careers; however, this is unlikely as it was common knowledge from the go that Alan was the author of all the Roland Allen plays.</p>

<p>The real reason for the pseudonym was far simpler; the play –&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/the-square-cat">The Square Cat</a></em>&nbsp;– was a joint effort between Alan and Christine and the pseudonym reflected that (Christine’s maiden name and a variation on Alan’s christian name). This was no secret as practically every newspaper report on the couple noted how they were both writing their first play together.</p>

<p>Alan had already been trying his hand at writing plays – although none had been produced – and apparently already had a clear nascent gift for dialogue and ideas. Christine was apparently very good at creating structure and she provided the framework for the play, around which Alan would write the dialogue.</p>

<p>Thus&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>&nbsp;was born, credited as Alan Ayckbourn’s first professionally produced play but actually co-written by Alan and – by then – his wife, who he had married after completing the play.</p>

<p>Given the significance of the play in Alan and the Scarborough company’s history, it s interesting to note its production was not a sure fire certainty. Although&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>&nbsp;would prove to be enormously popular and the second most profitable play of the year, when Stephen Joseph told Scarborough Library’s Chief Librarian about the forthcoming summer season in mid-March, there was not a mention of&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>; which probably hadn’t even been finished at that point.</p>

<p>It’s only when the posters for the season were printed – approximately a month before the season – that&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>&nbsp;is mentioned; the poster being one of the rarest pieces of Ayckbourn memorabilia. Just two copies are believed to have survived, one held in <a href="http://ayckbourn-archive.alanayckbourn.net/page-5/page-16/">The Bob Watson Archive</a> at the SJT and the other in the Ayckbourn Archive at the <a href="http://ayckbourn-archive.alanayckbourn.net/page-5/page-10/">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a> at the University of York.</p>

<p>The play, starring Alan Ayckbourn in the duel role of pop-star Jerry Watts and his mild-mannered alter-ego Arthur Brummage, was a huge success; although it didn’t feature Christine who was by this point pregnant with their first child, Steven.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Alan Ayckbourn in The Square Cat as Jerry Wattis &amp; Arthur Brummage<br>(© Haydonning Ltd)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>The play’s success led to it becoming the first play to run for two consecutive weeks at the Library Theatre when Stephen pulled a week of the far less well received&nbsp;<em>Frankenstein</em>&nbsp;adaptation by David Campton. Immediately after the season ended, Stephen also commissioned Alan to write a second play, which was premiered during the winter season later that year and was called&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/love-after-all">Love After All</a></em>.</p>

<p>All of which makes 1959 a remarkable year for the Stephen Joseph Theatre. Here we have two play- wrights who will go onto extraordinary success staging their first plays with a little-know provincial theatre company.</p>

<p>Alongside this, there is little doubt that working with Pinter was a huge inspiration to Ayckbourn in both his efforts to write his first professional play, but also later as a fully-fledged playwright who has always credited Pinter’s influence on him in writing dialogue.</p>

<p>And above all this, we have Stephen Joseph, an Artistic Director who saw the potential in both writers. He encouraged Pinter to keep on writing after what must have been a traumatic experience in the West End and recognised a nascent talent in Alan Ayckbourn, encouraging him to write and immediately capitalising on the success of his first play.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Alan Ayckbourn &amp; Harold Pinter<br>(© Tony Bartholomew)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Arguably, Stephen actions also unwittingly had huge long term consequences. By encouraging Alan as playwright and, later, director, he unknowingly laid the seeds for the company’s prolonged success and survival as well as ensuring, though Alan Ayckbourn, his policy of encouraging new writing and writers became embedded as a central tenet of the SJT’s existence.</p>

<p>Also, by nurturing Pinter and Ayckbourn, he played a vital part in the formative years of two of the most successful British playwriting talents of the second half of the 20th century.</p>
