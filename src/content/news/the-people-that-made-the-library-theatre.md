<p>In 1955, <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> opened <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> in Scarborough as the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company.</p>

<p>Throughout the year, the blog is telling the story of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre and here we’re highlighting one of the most important elements. The people.</p>

<p>Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre operated from 1955 to 1976, when the company moved to its second home the <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-188/page-15/">Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round</a></strong>. During that 21 year period, the company employed several hundred people on and off-stage.</p>

<p>As with every organisation, there are some names which deserve to be highlighted for their contribution to the company and all that it achieved. This is my list of the 10 (actually 12, but you’ll see why) people who I believe are the most influential in the company’s early years. It’s not definitive, it’s not really objective, but hopefully it shines a light on some of the significant figures from the past including several who haven’t received their fair share of the spotlight in previous celebrations of the Stephen Joseph Theatre’s History.</p>

<p><strong>The People That made The Library Theatre</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>In no particular order, these ares some of the people who helped make Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre a success.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Stephen Joseph (© Studio Theatre Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>1) Stephen Joseph.</strong> Obviously. Stephen created the company in 1955 and provided the philosophy upon which it was run for the next six decades. Stephen was a theatrical pioneer who wanted to break the system – specifically the predominance of what he viewed as the staid and limiting proscenium arch. He championed ‘new’ theatre forms such as theatre in the round and the opportunities they presented, not least the ability to produce and promote affordable new writing. In 1955, he set up the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough, dedicated to promoting theatre in the round and new writing by new writers. It was revolutionary. Not only did he have faith that theatre in the round could be successful but that new writing was a vital part of British theatre moving forward – it’s worth remembering Stephen opened the UK’s first company dedicated to new writing, a year before the English Stage Company / Royal Court. Without Stephen Joseph, there would be no theatre-in-the-round in Scarborough and it’s impossible to calculate his contribution to British theatre during this period despite the fact, it’s largely forgotten now.</p>

<p>You can find a more detailed look at Stephen Joseph, his life and achievements on the <strong><a href="http://www.a-round-town.com">A Round Town</a></strong> website by clicking <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">here</a></strong>.</p>

<p><strong>2) William Smettem and Mervyn Edwards.</strong> Traditionally, William Smettem and Mervyn Edwards have largely been sidelined from the history of the Stephen Joseph Theatre. Yet, fundamentally, they are vital to the company and, without them, it’s impossible to believe the company would have survived, let alone thrived, at the Library. They were the Directors of Public Libraries, Museums and Art Gallery in Scarborough and were responsible for Scarborough Library. When Stephen Joseph contacted Smettem in 1955 – arguably having all but given up on finding somewhere to found his in-the-round company – the Director was on the verge of retirement and looked favourably upon Stephen’s rather radical proposal. Not only did he give permission for Stephen to hire the library’s Concert Room for the summer but he also negotiated exceptionally favourable hire terms, which made it financially viable for Stephen and favourable to return to in future years. Smettem retired that summer and was succeeded by Mervyn Edwards, who would remain the Director until control of libraries went from local to regional level in 1974. He was even more supportive and enthusiastic about theatre-in-the-round and his correspondence with Stephen, held in archive at Scarborough Library, showed a mutual respect for each other. Mervyn was responsible for arranging grants to help the company financially, supporting Stephen’s unfulfilled ambitions for a civic theatre in the town, helping expand the theatre’s operations into winter seasons and, judging by the correspondence, they had a very warm relationship. It is notable that when Mervyn left his position at the library, everything became very precarious for the company. He was, arguably, one of the company’s greatest cheerleaders and supporters during Stephen Joseph’s years with the company between 1955 and 1965 and, without him, I find it hard to believe the company would have survived past its first five years, let alone 70 years.</p>

<p><strong>3) Ken and Margaret Boden.</strong> Undoubtedly two of the most significant people connected with Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-7/styled-10/">Ken Boden</a></strong> was an insurance agent and leading member of Scarborough’s amateur dramatic community alongside his wife, Margaret. Ken met Stephen in 1955 and Stephen began to tap into Ken’s contacts and quickly established him as the first front of house manager and, later, the theatre manager. Ken brought with him access to the voluntary staff who would help run the front of house – again making the company financially viable to run. Ken was the company’s theatre manager throughout the ’60s to mid ’80s and Margaret was box office manager for many years. Perhaps Ken’s single most important contribution though was saving the company in 1965 after Stephen had decided to close it as a professional concern. Ken was able to organise an amateur theatre-in-the-round season in 1966 before, with Stephen’s blessing, restarting a professional company in 1967 allowing the company to survive whilst a natural successor to Stephen was found to take it forward.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>David Campton (© Studio Theatre Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>4) David Campton.</strong> Trick question, who was Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre’s first resident playwright? Not Alan Ayckbourn as most people assume. It was actually <strong><a href="http://articles.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-75/page-7/styled-16/">David Campton</a></strong>, who was one of the first playwrights to be produced by Stephen in 1955 and had at least one production every year in Scarborough until 1965 when Stephen closed the company. David met Stephen on his playwriting course, who asked him to contribute to his new theatre’s inaugural season. It began a close relationship between the two men with Stephen championing David’s writing whilst David became increasingly central to the running of the company and was its theatre manager for several years. Stephen believed David had the potential to be his break-out writer, far more so than Alan Ayckbourn, and David was favourably compared with the likes of Harold Pinter early in his career. Whilst David’s career did not quite hit the heights of Alan’s, it was nonetheless phenomenally successful over the decades. After Stephen’s death in 1967, David was responsible for his estate and ensured the Stephen Joseph archive went to the University of Manchester. He was the first of many successful writers to be nurtured by the company Stephen created.</p>

<p><strong>5) Rodney Wood.</strong> Rodney was one of Stephen’s protégés and is probably responsible for one of the single most significant decisions in the entire history of the Stephen Joseph Theatre. In 1957, Stephen appointed Rodney as theatre manger in Scarborough; at the time he was working at Leatherhead Theatre Club alongside an enthusiastic 17 year old stage manager with aspirations to be an actor. Rodney asked the young man if he would like to join him in Scarborough as stage manger and he agreed. Enter Alan Ayckbourn who, without Rodney, would likely never have met Stephen Joseph and joined the company with which he has been associated with throughout his life. Rodney was theatre manager between 1957 and 1959 – essentially running the theatre for the frequently absent Stephen Joseph. As a director, Rodney was the third most prolific director at the venue after Stephen Joseph and Alan Ayckbourn. Most notably, following the resumption of professional theatre in 1967, he was appointed the Director of Productions in both 1967 and 1968, essentially righting the boat and steadying its course after a turbulent period including the death of its founder, Stephen Joseph, in 1967.</p>

<p><strong>6) Veronica ‘P.B.’ Pemberton-Billing.</strong> It’s perhaps odd to include Stephen Joseph’s house-keeper in this list, but everyone I’ve spoken to who remembers the Library Theatre years has mentioned P.B. She was intensely devoted to Stephen Joseph and intrinsically involved in the Library Theatre from its opening season. Best known as the catering manager who made the ‘delicious cakes’ advertised in the programmes, she would also fiercely help to protect Stephen’s legacy after his death and had a place on the board of Scarborough Theatre Trust for many years – Stephen even left his home to her. It’s with noting that her son, Robin, also made an extraordinary contribution to British Theatre as founder of Bolton’s Octagon Theatre, which opened in the UK in 1967 and is generally acknowledge as the UK’s first successful adaptable theatre able to function as in-the-round, thrust and open stage.</p>

<p><strong>7) Stephen Garrett.</strong> It’s an interesting question why the director of the Getty Museum in Los Angeles and the Getty Villa&nbsp;in Malibu – which he also designed – is on a list about a small regional in-the-round theatre in Scarborough. But Garrett was instrumental in its foundation. Stephen Joseph knew Garrett from university at Cambridge and tapped him for ideas when he began to create Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in 1955. Garrett drew up architectural plans for theatre in the round (an example of which is still held by Scarborough Library) for Stephen as well as designing an exhibition telling the story of theatre-in-the-round, which was displayed art the library for several years and survives to this day in archive. When interviewed years later, Garrett noted that Stephen never paid him for his work and that he found him both inspirational and irritating in equal parts!</p>

<p><strong>8) Peter Cheeseman.</strong> Peter’s specific contribution to Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre is not of huge significance. But his connection to Stephen Joseph, which begins in Scarborough, is. <strong><a href="http://articles.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-75/page-7/styled-22/">Peter Cheeseman</a></strong> was the theatre manager during the winter 1961 and summer 1962 seasons at the Library Theatre and directed eight productions. What is vital though is, like Alan Ayckbourn, his connection with Stephen Joseph. In the autumn of 1962, Stephen founded the Victoria Theatre in Stoke-on-Trent, the UK’s first professional theatre in the round venue and Peter became its first Artistic Director. He – along with most of the Scarborough company including Alan Ayckbourn – joined Stephen in Stoke-on-Trent in 1962 to set up the theatre. Peter would, famously, stay with the company for the rest of his professional life, transforming it into a hugely successful regional theatre company with deep roots in the community. It also stayed a theatre-in-the-round, now in its second home as the New Vic in Newcastle-under-Lyme. But again, without Peter coming to Scarborough and meeting Stephen Joseph, he arguably would not have become such a vital figure in British regional theatre.</p>

<p><strong>9) Stephen Mallatratt.</strong> Most of the people mentioned in this list are connected with Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre from its earliest days – <strong><a href="http://articles.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-84/page/styled-123/">Stephen Mallatratt</a></strong> is from its latter years. He joined the company as an actor in 1974 and was a prolific performer until 1979. But it is as a playwright he is most famous. Stephen came from the same tradition that found Alan Ayckbourn, a company which encouraged its actors – and other staff – to write. Stephen had his first play, <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-19/page-3/page-38/page53.html">An Englishman’s Home</a></strong></em>, produced in 1975 by Alan Ayckbourn, who described it as an almost ‘perfect’ debut play. Stephen would have three plays produced at the library and another six at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round and was resident playwright at one point. His most famous work, however, was his adaptation of Susan Hill’s novella <strong><a href="http://thewomaninblack.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk"><em>The Woman in Black</em></a></strong>, which premiered in Scarborough in 1987 and became a theatrical sensation and the second longest running play in the West End after <em>The Mousetrap</em>. The play, which premiered in the studio at the theatre as a low budget ‘Christmas stocking filler’ has been seen around the world and all from a writer, who – like Alan Ayckbourn – got his break at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. After his initial writing and acting period at the theatre, he returned as an Associate Director at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round from 1986 – 1987.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>10) Alan Ayckbourn.</strong> If we begin with Stephen Joseph, we have to end with his protégé Alan Ayckbourn. The man who has arguably done more to promote both Stephen Joseph and theatre-in-the-round than anyone else. Alan met Stephen at the age of 18 when he joined the Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in 1957 and was immediately influenced and inspired by the man he would consider a mentor. Stephen saw Alan’s potential and encouraged him as playwright and director as well as forming a close friendship with their shared interest in music, sound design and theatre. After Stephen’s death in 1967, Alan’s was his natural successor and, in 1972, became the Artistic Director of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. He committed himself and the theatre to continuing Stephen’s legacy of championing theatre-in-the-round and new playwriting, taking the company into two new homes and giving it an international profile. Alan’s own achievements are too numerous to count, but he has always made it known that all he achieved would not have been possible if he had not the good fortune to have met Stephen in 1957 at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in a life-changing moment.</p>

<p>You can find out about Alan Ayckbourn’s career, achievements and thoughts on Stephen Joseph on his official website <strong><a href="/">www.alanayckbourn.net.</a></strong></p>

<p>That then is my list of the 10 most significant people to be involved with Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. But it only touches the surface of the hundreds of people who helped to make the company a success over 21 years and established a theatre that would continue to thrive until the present day.</p>

<p>Many of the people of the people involved in the company between 1955 and 1976 can be found listed on the <strong><a href="http://www.a-round-town.com">A Round Town</a></strong> website <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-129/page-185/page22.html">here</a></strong> and <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-129/">here</a></strong>. This was a theatre which relied on voluntary help and people who went above and beyond. Even though we can’t mention them all here, their contribution to the success of the company as it marks its 70th anniversary should not be underestimated and always valued.</p>
