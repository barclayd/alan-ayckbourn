<p>So far in this weekly-ish blog, we’ve asked what is an Archivist? (not, sadly, as someone suggested, a nemesis for Doctor Who) and why Alan Ayckbourn is significant in British Theatre.</p>

<p>But what, you may ask (or not), is the Ayckbourn Archive and what is held in it? I don’t know what you think of – if anything – when you picture an archive, but I tend to gravitate towards vast library stacks in subterranean cellars with automated storage and retrieval systems (but then I need to get out more) or perhaps you think of dusty rooms full of box files (closer to the truth, although hopefully lacking the dust) or perhaps, you have no concept of what an archive might be.</p>

<p>The truth, as far as the Ayckbourn Archive is concerned, is a lot of document wallet boxes, folders, ring binders and file boxes held between the Borthwick Institute of Archives at the University of York (material between 1959 and 2006) and in Scarborough (predominantly material between 2006 to the present day) alongside a couple of iMacs where much of the archive is also stored in a digital format.</p>

<p>The Ayckbourn Archive is, essentially, a historical collection of material pertaining to Alan Ayckbourn and his professional theatre career. It began, informally, in the 1960s from articles and reviews haphazardly collated into scrapbooks and grew exponentially from there.</p>

<p>Over a series of articles, I’ll be looking at different areas of the Archive, illustrating them with some of my favourite pieces. We begin with the foundation of the Archive which is, naturally enough, Alan’s plays.</p>

<div><figure><a href="/plays/the-square-cat"><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/tsc-c2a9-haydonning-ltd-1.jpg" alt="" width="567" height="800" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></a><figcaption><em>The first page of Alan Ayckbourn’s first play, The Square Cat (© Haydonning Ltd)</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>The Ayckbourn Archive – and when we say this, this is the entire Ayckbourn Archive across what is held both by the Borthwick Institute and the playwright in Scarborough – holds an entire collection of play-scripts from his first play, <em>The Square Cat</em> (and several earlier, unproduced plays) through to his latest, <em>Birthdays Past, Birthdays Present</em> (and truth to tell, beyond, as there are three recent full-length plays as of writing which have yet to be produced).</p>

<p>The early manuscripts are obviously the rarest and most valuable with Alan’s second play, <em>Love After All</em>, being the rarest of the scripts; there is believed to be only one extant copy of the manuscript for this and it is held in the British Library with a copy held in the Ayckbourn Archive. <em>The Square Cat</em>, comparably, is surprisingly profligate with originals held by the Ayckbourn Archive, the British Library, Manchester University and personally by the playwright.</p>

<div><figure><a href="/plays/the-sparrow"><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/ts-c2a9-haydonning-ltd.jpg" alt="" width="636" height="800" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></a><figcaption><em>The cover of Alan Ayckbourn’s The Sparrow (© Haydonning Ltd)</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Probably the rarest manuscript in the actual Ayckbourn Archive is his 1967 play <em>The Sparrow</em> for which it is believed there are only two surviving copies (and intriguingly it is not listed within the British Library online catalogue). Given the play has only been performed once for just three weeks, it is a genuine Ayckbourn curiosity not least because the production featured the actors John Nettles and Robert Powell as well as the playwright’s wife, Heather Stoney.</p>

<p>Original manuscripts of all the early withdrawn plays can be found in the Ayckbourn Archive which include: <em>The Square Cat</em> (1959, original), <em>Love After All</em> (1959, copy), <em>Dad’s Tale</em> (1960, original), <em>Standing Room Only</em> (1961, original), <em>Christmas V Mastermind</em> (1962, copy), <em>Mr Whatnot</em> (1963, original) and <em>The Sparrow</em> (1967, original). The Archive also holds the original permutations of <em>Relatively Speaking</em> (originally titled <em>Meet My Father</em> and substantially different to the the play today) and the early permutations of <em>Family Circles</em> produced originally as <em>The Story So Far…</em> and then <em>Me Times Me</em> before eventually becoming <em>Family Circles</em>; an awful lot of effort for a play which Alan later admitted he wished he’d ‘left it alone.’</p>

<div><figure><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular"><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/aps-c2a9-haydonning-ltd.jpg" alt="" width="915" height="800" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></a><figcaption><em>An original Absurd Person Singular manuscript from 1972 (© Haydonning Ltd)</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Original typed manuscripts are held for all the classic plays from the 1970s from <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> through to <em>The Norman Conquests</em> – two of which written and originally produced under the far less catchy titles of <em>Fancy Meeting You</em> (<em>Table Manners</em>) and <em>Make Yourself At Home</em> (<em>Living Together</em>), <em>Bedroom Farce</em> to <em>Taking Steps</em>. In the 1980s, there is the original three-act version of <em>Season’s Greetings</em> featuring Uncle Harvey’s unseen off-stage wife, Shirley, as well as the revised, definitive two-act version in which Shirley was later excised.</p>

<div><figure><a href="/plays/jeeves"><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/bj-c2a9-haydonning-ltd.jpg" alt="" width="1096" height="800" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></a><figcaption><em>The earliest complete draft of the musical Jeeves (© Haydonning Ltd)</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>For fans of flop musicals – and who doesn’t love a flop musical? – the Archive holds several permutations of Alan Ayckbourn and Andrew Lloyd Webber’s notorious 1975 collaboration <em>Jeeves</em>. An in-depth discussion of the development of the scripts can be found <a href="/plays/jeeves/history/creating-jeeves">here</a>, and <a href="/plays/jeeves/songs">here</a>, but in archive there is the original 202 page script <em>An Evening With Bertram Wooster</em> – which is a mind-boggling length. In context, the revised 154 page script apparently ran for five-and-a-half hours during the original run-through!</p>

<div><figure><a href="/plays/seasons-greetings"><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/sg-c2a9-haydonning-ltd.jpg" alt="" width="550" height="800" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></a><figcaption><em>An example of Alan’s hand-written pencil notes, here for Season’s Greetings (© Haydonning Ltd)</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Prior to 1984, Alan hand-wrote all his first drafts before dictating the plays for typing. As a result, a number of hand-written first drafts are held in archive and which provide a fascinating insight into Alan’s playwriting process and his desire to doodle on practically every piece off paper he’s ever been given. They also illustrate his – and I hope he will forgive me for this – torturously difficult to read hand-writing. In archive, there is an original hand-written copy of his seminal play <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> just waiting to be ‘translated’ by someone with a lot of patience and very keen eyesight.</p>

<p>One of my favourite manuscripts is <em>A Chorus Of Disapproval</em>, for which only the first act in handwritten first draft form exists. This was due entirely to Alan coming into possession of his first word-processor midway through the writing process. The first draft of Act 1 was hand-written, Act 2 typed and edited until the final draft. It’s an intriguing idea that we can place the moment so exactly when technology affected a writer’s life so completely. Not widely known now is that Alan dictates all his work now into a computer with voice recognition software (it’s subtly alluded to in his 82nd play, <em>Better Off Dead</em>).</p>

<p> Perhaps surprisingly, it is only in more recent years that we tend to have differing drafts of the plays. But prior to <em>A Chorus Of Disapproval</em>, Alan would hand-write the manuscript and then dictate it for typing and which became the first and, usually, final draft; it’s worth considering that prior to 1987, Alan tended to write his plays to the latest possible deadline (the day before rehearsals) so the turn around of writing to producing manuscripts was frequently little more than a week and didn’t leave much time for second or third drafts. After 1984, Alan tended to edit completely on his word processor, later computer, and only the final draft would tend to be kept.</p>

<div><figure><a href="/plays/living-together"><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/myah-c2a9-haydonning-ltd.jpg" alt="" width="605" height="800" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></a><figcaption><em>An original manuscript for the first production of Make Yourself At Home – later to become famous as Living Together from The Norman Conquests (© Haydonning Ltd)</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Of course, technology has had a huge effect and by the 1990s, a certain magic is lost. The certain magic of opening an aged, typed 1970s script with hand-written notes, sellotaped in inserts and accompanying errors is lost with cleanly bound, word-perfect, Arial font manuscripts. Perhaps that’s just me though!</p>

<p>Alongside all the full-length plays, there are also manuscripts for all the revues, adaptations, one act plays, children’s plays and miscellany alongside the ‘grey’ plays, unused notes and other treasures. But we’ll save those for another blog!</p>
