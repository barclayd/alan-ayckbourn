<p><em>Welcome back to our ongoing exploration of significant events in the history of Alan Ayckbourn’s home theatre, the Stephen Joseph Theatre in Scarborough.</em></p>

<p><em>We’ve reached 1958 now – the fourth year of what was then The Library Theatre. The company has now become firmly established in Scarborough with both a summer and winter season as well as an autumn touring programme. Behind it all is the founder and Artistic Director, Stephen Joseph, a theatre pioneer who regularly clashed with the Establishment throughout his professional. This attitude to break down walls and tradition is typified by the events of 1958.</em></p>

<p><strong>1958: Discordant Anthem</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>One of my favourite stories drawn from the history of the Stephen Joseph Theatre is the furore which surrounded Stephen Joseph’s decision to withdraw the National Anthem from performances during 1958.</p>

<p>In context – for our younger readers! – until the 1960s, the National Anthem was essentially played in the UK at every theatre, concert and cinema performance – even at the end of the night at dance halls. Indeed, the BBC played the National Anthem at the end of the broadcasting day until as recently as 1997; essentially when rolling 24/7 programming began.</p>

<p><a href="/life/stephen-joseph">Stephen Joseph</a> though was an early rebel and in 1958 he decided to stop the performance of the National Anthem following every performance at the Library Theatre; becoming one of the first regional theatres to consistently stop the practise – it had already ceased at some London theatres and Stephen believed, correctly as it transpired, it was a dying and dated tradition.</p>

<p>However, Scarborough was a provincial town during the 1950s and such a change did draw a considerable amount of attention!</p>

<p>This was typified by the first – and finest – of many letters published in the Scarborough Evening News from an aggrieved patron:</p>

<p><em>Sir, My wife, daughter, and two friends went to the theatre in the round at the Public Library. Neither at the start nor the finish of the show was the National Anthem played. We were so seething with rage that we tackled the director of the show, Mr Joseph. His lame excuses were: (1) he did not think patrons wished it, (2) it was not good unless it was played by an orchestra and (3) it would be played at the last performance of the season. I hope that as the show is put on with the idea of making money, and as they are tenants of the corporation, the public will stay away until this is rectified. This is our intention although we have enjoyed the productions. Yours Streater Ellis, 66 Filey Road.</em></p>

<p>Particularly enjoyable is the thought of someone seething with rage at the omission and Stephen’s gentle sarcasm that it needed an orchestra to play well. Mr Ellis’s call to arms did generate a response, but there’s no record of mass boycotting of the venue.</p>

<p>The Scarborough Evening News letters’ pages were subsequently filled with much gnashing of teeth about whether the National Anthem should or should not be played with local figures such as the Mayor of Scarborough drawn in, who at one point publicly declared “75 to 90 per cent of the population of Scarborough wants it.” This ‘fact’ was presumably found on the back of a beer mat or some such place, given the Town Hall later confirmed it had received no correspondence as far as it was aware pertaining to the lack of the National Anthem.</p>

<p>Stephen Joseph, meanwhile, joined in the fun as he was wont to do in such matters. Although he did not officially comment on the issue until the end of the year, he was notorious for writing inflammatory letters under pseudonyms to newspapers often pursuing vigorous arguments against himself under another pseudonym! In this case, it’s almost certain he began a discourse under the names of ‘Republican’ and ‘Roundhead’ which includes the wonderful description of the National Anthem.</p>

<p><em>“I would like to offer my sincere congratulations to the Library Theatre from saving us from the hideous noise, the supreme embarrassment and the maudlin sentimentalism occasioned by the playing of that barbaric and jarring thing (I refuse to call it a tune). The sooner all theatres do this the better.”&nbsp;</em></p>

<p>Although the argument raged throughout the summer, Stephen did not comment ‘officially’ publicly until the end of the year in which he continued to hold his stance – with the rather cheeky proviso that the anthem would be played in the unlikely event royalty did visit the Library Theatre.</p>

<p><em>“We feel that the playing of the anthem at every performance does not contribute to any feeling of respect. We would, of course, play it if royalty were to visit us. I feel that those people who objected in the summer were a bit eccentric, or they were people who did not often visit theatre or just happened to be feeling cross.”</em></p>

<p>Whilst that should have signalled the end of the issue, the National Anthem issue ran on for the better part of a decade and kicked into an even higher gear the following year. In the press cuttings held in <a href="http://ayckbourn-archive.alanayckbourn.net/page-5/page-16/">The Bob Watson Archive</a> at the SJT, the majority of articles from the first six months of the year are concerned with the National Anthem and whether it would be restored. </p>

<p>This reached its apex when Maurice Plows, the treasurer of Scarborough Theatre Guild, resigned in principle over the issue. As Scarborough Theatre Guild was associated with the Library Theatre, Mr Plows made the somewhat tenuous leap that people would assume the theatre guild was also not playing the anthem and people would stay away in droves. Mr Plows would later become a Town Councillor and if you’re an Ayckbourn fan, you may be aware of him as he was the inspiration behind Councillor Donald Evans in the play <em><a href="/plays/ten-times-table">Ten Times Table</a></em>. In an interview, Mr Plows let vent.</p>

<p><em>“I have been approached by a well-known figure in Scarborough who asked me if I could do something about it [the playing of the National Anthem at each performance]. I saw Mr Joseph who said that the National Anthem was not played at every performance in London. But it is different here. If there is sufficient local demand – and there is – for the National Anthem, then they should play it – or otherwise lose patrons…. If they [the patrons] are going to stay away because they think we are not playing the National Anthem, then we are going to lose money.”</em></p>

<p>Quite who – if anyone at all – approached Mr Plows is never explained nor resolved, nor at any point in the coming months is any actual evidence presented about by anyone about the true depth of feeling in the community about this issue. Despite all the warnings, there is no evidence the issue affected ticket sales in any note-worthy way.</p>

<p>By this point, the argument was being regularly covered in The Stage newspaper as well as the local press. This led to Rodney Wood, who was managing the company’s current tour, wrote to The Stage noting the company had no associations with Scarborough Theatre Guild so the resingation would have no effect on any decisions. He also pointed out the company itself had received only four letters regarding the issue – two from people who had never even visited the theatre! – and only five people had been recorded making any comment in person the previous year.</p>

<p>Which just goes to show that what we think of as a modern issue of the ‘vocal minority’ dominating the conversation is an age-old issue. Different media, same result. The letters’ page of the local newspaper was platform enough for those who intended to shout loudest to set the agenda, when there’s no evidence at all the average theatre-goer gave a fig about the furore.</p>

<p>Come March – three months before the Library Theatre was to re-open for the year – and Mr Plows was on a roll. Standing his ground more firmly than ever, he asserted the number of people now not attending shows ran into “three figures.” Quite how he deduced this figure is hard to evaluate. The company hadn’t performed in the town since early January!</p>

<p>The wider lack of interest was demonstrated conclusively when the theatre announced that – coinciding with the launch of the summer season – a ballot of patrons visiting the Library Theatre from 22 – 26 June would ascertain whether there was a demand for the anthem to be restored. According to internal correspondence held by The Bob Watson Archive, the response was apparently so lacklustre it was decided to not even advertise the result in any meaningful way.</p>

<p>Astonishingly, the issue of the National Anthem kept popping its head over the parapet over the next decade until the issue was finally put to rest in 1969. By this point, Stephen Joseph had died two years previously and the anthem had not been played except at the opening and close of the season for ten years. </p>

<p>At Scarborough Theatre Trust’s AGM, who else but Maurice Plows should once more query the decision.</p>

<p>“Secretary reported that he had received a request for some decision regarding the playing of the National Anthem at the Library Theatre. After some discussion it was proposed Miss Clarke and seconded Mr Plows:- ‘That the National Anthem be played.’</p>

<p>“On a vote being taken it was defeated three votes for and four against. It was however agreed that the National Anthem be played at the beginning and end of the season performances and when there was a civic night. This was carried.”</p>

<p>That was that. Or as Shakespeare put it, much ado about nothing. </p>

<p>And for those wondering what the legal position at the time was, there was absolutely no requirement to play the anthem as the Lord Chamberlain’s Office rather curtly pointed out to the Yorkshire Evening Post.</p>

<p><em>“An official of the Lord Chamberlain’s office said today: ‘There is no regulation on this. We are completely indifferent to it, and would never contemplate issuing any order or advice.’”</em></p>

<p>Complete indifference. It’s a wonder the tradition lasted so long as it did!</p>
