<p>Forty years ago today, one of Alan Ayckbourn’s most popular and famed works had its world premiere.</p>

<p><em><a href="/plays/a-chorus-of-disapproval"><strong>A Chorus of Disapproval</strong></a></em> opened at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round, Scarborough, on 2 May 1984 and would become one of the playwright’s most successful and performed works.</p>

<p>The following year, it would open at the National Theatre before transferring into the West End. This production would win an Olivier Award for Best Comedy as well as the Evening Standard and DRAMA Awards for Best Comedy.</p>

<p>But the play we know and love today was not the one initially conceived by the playwright. His original plan was on a far grander, fourth-wall breaking(if that’s possible in-the-round!) scale and – in all likelihood – would have been impossible to stage for the Scarborough company at that point.</p>

<p>In an exclusive extract from his book <strong><em><a href="http://www.alanayckbourn.net/styled-13/page28.html">Unseen Ayckbourn</a></em></strong>, Alan Ayckbourn Ayckbourn’s Archivist Simon Murgatroyd tells the story of the <em>A Chorus of Disapproval</em> which never came to be…</p>

<p><strong>A Chorus of Disapproval</strong></p>

<p><em>A Chorus Of Disapproval</em> marked the 25th anniversary of Alan Ayckbourn’s career as a playwright in 1984 and was his 31st full-length play. From conception it was always highly ambitious in its desire to portray an amateur drama company and its attempt to stage a musical.</p>

<p>Alan was initially inspired by Rudolf Friml’s operetta <em>The Vagabond King</em>, whose ludicrously detailed stage directions seemed like a recipe for disaster for any amateur company tackling the piece.</p>

<p>This concept was then developed to incorporate approximately 85 local amateur singers in the chorus (in Alan Ayckbourn’s biography <em>Grinning At The Edge</em>, his biographer Paul Allen writes it was 20 people but Alan himself states his grander intentions of 85 people in Ian Watson’s <em>Conversations With Ayckbourn</em>), who would be scattered around the auditorium. Once the performance began, this clandestine chorus would have started singing 25 minutes into the production causing “the person next to them to look absolutely alarmed.”</p>

</div>

<p>Subsequently on 9 March, 1984, the Scarborough Evening News ran an editorial article advertising auditions.</p>

<p><em>“Alan Ayckbourn is writing a new play for the forthcoming professional Summer season centred around a (Fictional!) Amateur Operatic Society. His is anxious to meet AMATEUR SINGERS who would be interested in take part in this venture.”</em></p>

<p>The auditions were actually held with Alan teaming up with a female director from one of the local amateur companies to assist. It was an enlightening experience for Alan and possibly quite traumatic for those auditioning.</p>

<p><em> “It was quite an eye-opener. Although you hear nightmare stories occasionally, most professional auditions are pretty director, who I suppose became a prototype for Dafydd. She was absolutely brutal. A woman started singing and the director said almost at once, ‘Don’t go on. You have no voice. You’re out of tune and it’s a horrible noise’. As the singer ran out on the verge of tears, I said to the woman director, ‘That was a big blunt,’ and she said ‘Well, you’ve got to tell ’em or they just come back again’.”</em></p>

<p>A number of obstacles swiftly put paid to Alan’s initial plans. The actors’ union, Equity, absolutely opposed the use of amateur actors in a professional show; Alan’s audition experiences with the local amateur operatic societies proved to be less than fruitful with singers apparently expecting nothing less than the lead roles; and the Rudolf Friml Estate, fearing Alan was intending to send up <em>The Vagabond King</em>, denied him permission to use the play, claiming a major tour was imminent, but which never took place.</p>

<p><em>A Chorus Of Disapproval</em> was planned from the outset to be produced at the National Theatre, where the Artistic Director Peter Hall had been aware of Alan’s original intentions. Now Alan contacted him to notify him of the changes to the play.</p>

<p><em>“Needless to say, the play is vastly different from the one I described to you on the phone a few weeks ago. No chorus of amateurs. Just a few good singers. (Equity intervened there). I had to go back to John Gay as his agent was the only one who didn’t raise an objection.”</em></p>

<p>The playwright would turn instead to John Gay’s <em>The Beggar’s Opera</em> for inspiration; a musical not only out of copyright, but which mirrored Alan’s themes for his play. He also dropped the plans for the amateur chorus – although rumours persist that several characters in<em> A Chorus Of Disapproval</em> were inspired by some of the members of amateur dramatic societies Alan met during auditions.</p>

<p><em>Extract from Unseen Ayckbourn by Simon Murgatroyd</em></p>

<p>It’s also interesting to note that when <em>A Chorus of Disapproval</em> opened, it was with a production unlike any which followed. Alan Ayckbourn, obviously, writes the vast majority of his work with theatre in the round in mind as its preferred staging. His work is generally conceived and premiered in the round.</p>

<p>When <em>A Chorus of Disapproval</em> opened in 1984 in Scarborough, it was not only performed in the round but written for performance on a double stage revolve, which allowed the quick transition between multiple locations. The stage manager’s plans and notes for the revolve and its positions are still held in archive today.</p>

<p>But this was the only time the play was performed as it was originally conceived. There have been revivals in the round – including by the playwright himself – but none have utilised the double revolve staging it was conceived for.</p>
