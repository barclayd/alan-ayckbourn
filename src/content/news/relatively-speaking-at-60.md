<p>Alan Ayckbourn’s <strong><em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></em></strong> celebrates its 60th anniversary this month and for this final celebration piece on the blog, we’re going to back to 1965.</p>

<p>It’s not widely known that the original production of <em>Relatively Speaking</em> was considerably different ton the play we know today.</p>

<p>Firstly, it had a completely different title – <em>Meet My Father</em>. It was changed in 1967 as the play’s West End producer, Peter Bridge, wanted a title that sounded “less provincial”. One of the characters also had a different name, Ginny was Gina. The play was also substantially cut by <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong>.</p>

<p>Stephen was a ruthless editor of the plays he staged in Scarborough. He had to be. Performances at <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> began at 8pm and there was an informal agreement, the venue had to be clear by 10pm.</p>

<p>So that was just two hours for the play and the chance to talk to the audiences afterwards, which Stephen encouraged. To achieve this, he eliminated intervals and frequently cut all the plays to approximately 90 minutes.</p>

<p>In the case of <em>Meet My Father</em>, an original manuscript with Stephen’s edits reveals about a third of the play was cut with vast swathes of Stephen’s marker pen lines!</p>

<p>So, the play we know today is nothing like the very trimmed down play of 1965. But there’s more…</p>

<p>When Peter Bridge optioned the play for the West End, Alan sent him the original uncut manuscript – instantly restoring a third of the play’s dialogue. To this, Peter then asked for substantial changes to the first scene and for the ending to be altered.</p>

<p>Alan rewrote the first scene for the West End and then, after its London run had finished, altered the opening scene yet again so that the scene as it was performed in both the world and the West End premieres has never been seen subsequently!</p>

<p>But the most significant alteration was the climax. Alan was never happy with how the play originally ended and throughout 1966 and the early months of 1967, he kept altering it in the hope of achieving a more satisfactory resolution.</p>

<p>It was only when the play was on its pre-West End tour that Peter Bridge’s assistant, later Alan’s agent, Tom Erhardt suggested bringing the play back round to the slippers which began the play, that Alan was able to find the satisfactory ending he needed.</p>

<p>That final scene – as originally performed – has not been seen since 1965. But the blog is delighted to be able to present both the original and the revised scene to show just how big a difference several lines can make to the play.</p>

<p>Here’s the original script as performed at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in 1965.</p>

<p><strong>Meet My Father</strong>&nbsp;(act II, scene 2) <em>A garden<br></em><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;’Bye.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Goodbye. <em>(Pause)<br></em><strong>Sheila:&nbsp;</strong>Oh they are a sweet couple. I‘m quite taken with them. Aren’t you ?<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Entranced.<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;Quite wrong for each other of course. Absolutely and utterly. It’s almost certain to be a disastrous marriage but it will be very nice for them while it lasts.&nbsp;<em>(Philip sits down)&nbsp;</em>Are you all right?<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Fine.<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;You look tired.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Really? I wonder why that is?<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;<em>(picking up bedroom slippers)</em>&nbsp;Look what turned up again – your slippers. Guess where they were? Under the bed all the time. Funny how things have a habit of turning up… Do you want to put them on – rest your feet? <em>(She puts slippers down beside him. Philip stares at them)</em><br><strong>Philip:&nbsp;</strong>Those aren’t mine.<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;Oh dear.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;What?<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;I was just wondering how that poor boy was going to make out.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;That’s his problem.&nbsp;<em>(Pause)&nbsp;</em>I gather – that you gathered – from all that – the general – gist of things?<br><strong>Sheila:&nbsp;</strong>Just a little gist – yes.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Yes.<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;I’ll make a nice cup of tea, shall I?<br><em>(Sheila moves to the house. Pauses and turns)</em><br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;I’ll tell you one thing though, I’m awfully glad we haven’t got a daughter. She’d be a terrible handful, wouldn’t she?<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Yes. You’re right there. She would.<br><strong>END</strong></p>

<p>Not terribly satisfying really and a bit of a cop-out if one is being very honest. Compare it with the play we know now in which the loose end of the mysterious slippers is picked up and takes the play into a whole different realm giving Sheila a line to bring the curtain down!</p>

<p><strong>Relatively Speaking</strong>&nbsp;(act II, scene 2) <em>A garden</em><br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;‘Bye.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Goodbye. <em>(Pause)</em><br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;Quite wrong for each other of course. It’ll be a disastrous marriage but great fun for them while it lasts.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;I gather you’ve gathered the general gist of things.<br><strong>Sheila:&nbsp;</strong>Just a little gist – yes. I don’t think there’s any need to go to the Coopers now, is there?<br><strong>Philip:&nbsp;</strong>No.&nbsp;<em>(He goes through the gate)</em><br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;No.&nbsp;<em>(She starts putting plates and glasses on the tray)</em>&nbsp;We’ll have tea here, then. I’ll tell you one thing, though. I’m awfully glad we haven’t got a daughter, she’d be a terrible handful, wouldn’t she?<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Yes, you’re right there. She would.&nbsp;<em>(He catches sight of the slippers in the urn. He picks them out of the urn and comes back through the gate)</em>&nbsp;Sheila! Sheila! What are these doing here?<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;I don’t know, darling. You really ought to look after your things more carefully.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;My things? They aren’t mine.<br><strong>Sheila:&nbsp;</strong>Of course they’re yours. I bought them for you.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;No, they’re not. They look a bit like them-same tartan, same sort of shape and texture.<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;Well then – they’re yours.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;No, they’re not – the lining’s different.<br><strong>Sheila:&nbsp;</strong>Uh?&nbsp;<em>(She takes a slipper from him)</em><br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Mine were red. <em>(Sheila examines the lining of the slipper)<br></em><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;So they were. Oh dear.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;What?<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;I was just wondering how that poor boy was going to make out.<br><strong>Philip:</strong>&nbsp;Never mind about that.&nbsp;<em>(He takes a slipper from her)</em>&nbsp;Whose are these?<br><strong>Sheila:</strong>&nbsp;Wouldn’t you like to know?<br><em>(Sheila claps her hands delightedly and moves on to the terrace, to go out through the DS door, leaving Philip gazing at the slippers, dumbfounded)</em><br><strong>END</strong></p>

<p>With just a bit of editing the line order and the addition of several more, we end up with the classic ending of the play. Given how quickly Alan wrote his plays back then (no more than 10 days for <em>Relatively Speaking</em>), he probably spent more time working on these final lines than the rest of the play combined!</p>

<p>That ends the blog’s celebration of sixty years of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>. You can find out more about the play at Alan Ayckbourn’s Official Website <strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">here</a></strong>. In the meantime, here’s hoping <em>Relatively Speaking</em> will still be a hit and performed in 2085!</p>
