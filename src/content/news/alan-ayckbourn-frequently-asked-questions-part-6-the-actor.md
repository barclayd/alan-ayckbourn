<p><em><a href="/">Alan Ayckbourn’s Official Website</a> constantly receives questions about Sir Alan Ayckbourn and his plays – many of which recur quite frequently.</em></p>

<p><em>As a result of this, there’s a substantial <a href="/life/ayckbourn-faqs">FAQs section</a> on the website looking to answer many of the questions. For the next few weeks, the blog will be reproducing – and occasionally expanding – the FAQs hopefully answering a few questions and offering a bit of insight into Alan Ayckbourn.</em></p>

<p><em>In part 6, we’re looking at questions regarding Alan Ayckbourn’s first professional career in theatre, when he was an actor.</em></p>

<p><strong>Frequently Asked Questions: Alan Ayckbourn – The Actor</strong></p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption>Alan Ayckbourn in The Rainbow Machine (1962) © Haydonning Ltd</figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>1) What inspired Alan Ayckbourn to become an actor?<br></strong>It would be fair to say that Alan Ayckbourn never had a burning desire from youth to be an&nbsp;<a href="/career/actor">actor</a>. As he readily admits, like so much of his career, he was in the right place at the right time. Even by the time he left school at Haileybury, he admits he was “tinkering with a journalism career.”</p>

<p>What tipped the balance was a tutor&nbsp;<a href="/life/influences/haileybury">Edgar Matthews</a>, who encouraged Alan’s interest in acting. After taking his O Levels, Alan decided he did not want to stay on for further education as the school hoped: “Actually one was leaving because one didn’t want to stay, rather than because one wanted to do anything.” Having made the decision to leave school, he decided to try and find a route into theatre and approached Edgar Matthews, who gave him a letter of introduction to&nbsp;<a href="/career/actor/donald-wolfit">Sir Donald Wolfit</a>&nbsp;and contacted the famed impresario.</p>

<p>Wolfit, about to embark on a short tour, employed Alan on the Monday after he left school on the previous Friday. After the three week tour to the Edinburgh International Festival, where Wolfit suggested Alan go to drama school, Alan took an unpaid job as a student acting stage manager at the&nbsp;<a href="/career/actor/connaught-theatre">Connaught Theatre</a>, Worthing (1956 – 1957), from there he went straight into a job at&nbsp;<a href="/career/actor/connaught-theatre">Leatherhead Theatre Club</a>&nbsp;Theatre Club (spring 1957)and to the&nbsp;<a href="/career/ayckbourn-and-the-sjt/sjt-venues/theatre-in-the-round-at-the-library-theatre">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a>, Scarborough (summer 1957). He also briefly spent the 1957/58 winter season at the&nbsp;<a href="/career/actor/connaught-theatre">Oxford Playhouse</a>, </p>

<p>There was never a definite plan of action, he was just largely a victim of fortunate circumstance: “Entirely due to circumstances and possibly because I never did take any action. I could look back on my life and say I planned it that way, but I didn’t plan to be an actor, nor a director, nor a writer.”</p>

<p>Full details of Alan Ayckbourn’s career as an actor can be found&nbsp;<a href="http://careers.alanayckbourn.net/styled-3/index.html">here</a>.</p>

<p><strong>2) What is Alan Ayckbourn’s connection to Sir Donald Wolfit?<br></strong>After Alan left Haileybury, a tutor – Edgar Matthews – arranged for Alan to join&nbsp;Sir Donald Wolfit’s company for three weeks during the Edinburgh Festival. Alan was an acting assistant stage manager (an ASM with occasional acting roles) and appeared in the play&nbsp;<em><a href="/career/actor/the-strong-are-lonely">The Strong Are Lonely</a></em>. He played a foot-soldier apparently employed largely because the person who previously played the role kept fainting on stage due to the long periods of standing to attention. Alan – having been a member of the Cadet Force at Haileybury – was apparently able to cope with such ‘acting’ pressures.</p>

<p><strong>3) What was Alan’s first professional acting role following this?<br></strong>Alan’s first professional acting role was in Emlyn William’s&nbsp;<em><a href="/career/actor/the-corn-is-green">The Corn Is Green</a></em>&nbsp;at the&nbsp;Connaught Theatre, Worthing. Sadly, no other details are currently known about this production.</p>

<p><strong>4) How did Alan Ayckbourn come to work at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough?<br></strong>Whilst working at Leatherhead Theatre Company in the spring of 1957, Alan was asked by the stage manager – Rodney Wood  – if he wanted to come down to London to see Stephen Joseph’s Studio Theatre Ltd company performing Sartre’s <em>Huis Clois</em>. Alan did, saw his first production in-the-round and was astounded by it.</p>

<p>Soon afterwards, Rodney accepted the role of Theatre / Company Manager at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre for the 1957 season and asked Alan to accompany him. Alan agreed and took on the role of Acting Stage Manager – a stage manager with understudying and acting roles. His first season in Scarborough was the 1957 summer season.</p>

<p><strong>5) What was Alan’s first acting role at the Stephen Joseph Theatre (then Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre)?<br></strong>Alan’s first role at Theatre in the Round at&nbsp;the Library Theatre, Scarborough, was Eric Birling in J.B. Priestley’s&nbsp;<em><a href="/career/actor/an-inspector-calls">An Inspector Calls</a></em>. It was directed by Clive Goodwin – who would later become a prominent director at the Royal Shakespeare Company.</p>

<p><strong>6) How long did Alan Ayckbourn act professionally for?<br></strong>Alan Ayckbourn acted professionally on stage (see note below for his later off-stage career) from August 1956 to September 1964.</p>

<p><strong>6) How many productions did Alan Ayckbourn perform in?<br></strong>Alan Ayckbourn acted in approximately 80 productions between 1956 and 1964. These included appearing in the world premieres of four of his own plays as well as the second production of Pinter’s <em><a href="/career/actor/the-birthday-party">The Birthday Party</a></em> (see Question 9)- which also marked Pinter’s professional directorial debut. He played the lead role in approximately half of these 80 productions</p>

<p><strong>7) What was Alan Ayckbourn’s final stage acting role?<br></strong>Alan’s final stage role was as Jerry Ryan in William Gibson’s&nbsp;<em><a href="/career/actor/two-for-the-seesaw-2">Two For The Seesaw</a></em>, performed at the Civic Theatre, Rotherham. He played opposite Heather Stoney, whom he would marry in 1997. He and Heather only took the roles as the theatre made an appeal in The Stage newspaper for actors familiar with the roles after the two actors intended for the production pulled out. They had no other connection – or experience – with Rotherham Civic Theatre.</p>

<p>It’s also worth noting that Alan Ayckbourn also took to the stage in 2023 when he performed in a rehearsed reading of his previously unseen work, <em><a href="/plays/truth-will-out">Truth Will Out</a></em>. This was a table read and Alan played the role of Jim Tungsten.</p>

<p><strong>8) Are there any other acting roles Alan performed after 1964?<br></strong>Yes. Most significantly,  Alan Ayckbourn briefly stepped out of retirement during 2020 with the world premiere of his play&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/anno-domino">Anno Domino</a></em>. This was premiered as an audio stream as a result of the Covid-19 pandemic lockdown with Alan recording the entire play at his own home with all eight roles divided between himself and his wife, Heather Stoney (formerly a professional actor too). He would follow this up later in the same year by taking all the roles in his own audio adaptation of his play&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/haunting-julia">Haunting Julia</a></em>.</p>

<p>Of lesser note, Alan did appear on the 1964 Living Shakespeare recording of <em>Much Ado About Nothing</em>. Directed by Michael Mellinger, Alan played a number of smaller roles. The recording is still available today through most major streaming services.</p>

<p>Further to that, on the Sunday after&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves">How The Other Half Loves</a></em>&nbsp;had its world premiere in 1969 at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, the actor Jeremy Franklin slipped a disc. As a result Alan stepped in to play the role of Frank Foster for that week’s four remaining performances on the book; notably losing the final page of his script on the first performance and improvising the play’s climax.</p>

<p>Alan also took to the stage briefly in 2005 during the Stephen Joseph Theatre’s 50th anniversary programme <em>50 Years New</em>, when he played Dennis in a scene from&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/just-between-ourselves">Just Between Ourselves</a></em>. Also Alan Ayckbourn has frequently played the off-stage pre-recorded roles in many of his world premieres, most famously the foul-mouthed Arthur in&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/private-fears-in-public-places">Private Fears In Public Places</a></em>.</p>

<p><strong>9) What is Alan Ayckbourn’s connection to Harold Pinter? Is it true Alan Ayckbourn appeared in a Harold Pinter directed play?<br></strong>Alan Ayckbourn appeared as Stanley in only the second production of&nbsp;<a href="/life/influences/harold-pinter">Harold Pinter</a>’s play&nbsp;<em><a href="/career/actor/the-birthday-party">The Birthday Party</a></em>, directed by Harold Pinter himself for a three month rep tour from January 1959.<br>Intriguingly, this production by&nbsp;<a href="http://biography.alanayckbourn.net/styled-36/styled-37/">Stephen Joseph</a>’s Studio Theatre company is rarely mentioned in conjunction with the history of the play or the playwright, but is notable for several reasons:</p>

<p>○&nbsp;It was the first professional production of Pinter’s own work to be directed by Pinter himself.<br>○&nbsp;Pinter himself has said the production restored his confidence in both the play and his abilities as a playwright.<br>○&nbsp;It is demonstrably the first professional production of&nbsp;<em>The Birthday Party</em>&nbsp;following its critical panning in London in May 1958; this is in contrast to the Tower Theatre’s production in London in May 1959 which is frequently cited as the second production of the play.<br>○&nbsp;Alan Ayckbourn’s experiences with the play undoubtedly influenced him in the very early stages of his professional playwriting career (Alan was commissioned by Stephen Joseph to write his first play at approximately the same time as rehearsing the Pinter play).</p>
