<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1963 and an ultimatum to Scarborough Library regarding the future of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p><strong>1963: An Ultimatum</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>In 1963, Stephen Joseph began the process which would eventually see him close Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre little more than a decade after he had created it.</p>

<p>Stephen had founded the company in 1955 on the first floor of Scarborough Library and never intended this to be a permanent home. The venue had many shortcomings, but Stephen initially saw it as a means to an end with the hope he might either find more suitable premises in the town or his efforts to persuade another town council to fund a purpose-built theatre would be successful.</p>

<p>Stephen, it must be emphasised, had no ties to Scarborough nor any long-term commitment to either the town or Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. It was very much a test bed for his ideas about theatre-in-the-round and promoting new writing and, although he had grown fond of the town, he was well aware of the limitations the company faced.</p>

<p>This all came to a head in 1962 when Stephen finally had the opportunity to open a theatre-in-the-round venue with the <strong><a href="/career/victoria-theatre">Victoria Theatre</a></strong> in Stoke-on-Trent. His company, <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-40/">Studio Theatre Ltd</a></strong>, transferred to the Potteries and, in autumn 1962, most of the Scarborough acting company moved to Stoke for the inaugural season.</p>

<p>Arguably, there was nothing left in Scarborough. A situation only exacerbated when an application for funding to the Arts Council by Studio Theatre Ltd resulted in a grant less than half that which had been requested for just the Victoria Theatre.</p>

<p>Faced with a difficult choice, the board of Studio Theatre Ltd decided to cut loose Scarborough to concentrate all its finances and resources on the precarious new theatre in Stoke-on-Trent. There  was neither funding nor a company in Scarborough and, it appeared, that  Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was to close after eight seasons.</p>

<p>However, Stephen decided – with or without Arts Council help – he didn’t want the Scarborough project to end and arranged to run the 1963 season himself. He formed a new company – <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-40/">Theatre in the Round Ltd</a></strong> – with co-director David Campton in the hope that, albeit on a much reduced basis, the theatre could survive for at least another year.</p>

<p>Mervyn Edwards, Director of Public Libraries, Museums and Art gallery in Scarborough and a keen supporter of Stephen and his work, summarised the situation in a letter to the Libraries Committee in spring 1963, explaining the dire situation the company now found itself in.</p>

<p>“In October 1962, Studio Theatre Ltd, which has been responsible for productions in the Library Theatre for the last eight years, acquired its own theatre in Stoke-on-Trent. This year, the company’s arts council grant is less than half the amount asked for in respect of the new theatre alone. Faced with the alternative of closing the Victoria Theatre or abandoning the Scarborough season, the directors decided the latter course. As matters stood earlier this year, there would have been no Scarborough theatre-in-the-round this summer.</p>

<p>“However, Mr Stephen Joseph is very reluctant to throw away the result of eight years’ work and has taken over the goodwill of the Scarborough season from Studio Theatre Ltd. As a result, a smaller company, operating on a shoe-string and entirely without grant, will commence a repertory of plays on Tuesday, 11 June 1963. I understand that neither Mr Joseph nor the manager, Mr David Campton, will be drawing any salary.”</p>

<p>The piece ends with the hope the committee may be able to re-consider and increase the financial help that can be given to the theatre.</p>

<p>This situation also brought other matters to a head for Stephen. Whilst facilities had always been basic at the library, there had at least been a budget to compensate for some of the shortcomings. Now there was barely any budget to cover the staging of a season and all the problems with the venue that had been at the periphery were now writ large.</p>

<p>He and David decided that further action was needed if the company was to have any legitimate chance of a future in Scarborough. The conundrum was they couldn’t direct it at the library itself. Mervyn Edwards had been a staunch supporter of the project since 1955 and had frequently arranged for help from various groups including the Libraries Committee, which had not only been supportive of Stephen’s endeavours but had also been financially generous over the years.</p>

<p>Indeed, in one letter from 1963, Mervyn wrote the committee had been stalwart in their support as “I think they feel, with me, that your perseverance is something to be wondered at and supported.”</p>

<p>Stephen had to go above the library which, on Mervyn’s advice, meant writing to Scarborough Town Council directly as the library, at this time, was run on a local rather than a regional level. Only there might he find the larger financial support needed to keep the company running and the means to improve the facilities at the library.</p>

<p>To this end, Stephen wrote a strongly worded, four page ultimatum to the council, which is held in archive in Scarborough Library. Issued in April 1963, it broadly highlights the issues Stephen felt faced the company if it was to survive in Scarborough. It also began with the declaration , the summer 1963 was ‘likely to be the last’ without help.</p>

<p>This document is important as not only does it chronicle a key moment in the company’s history, but it also offers a snapshot into what conditions the company worked under; sadly, there is next to no photographic documentation of what the facilities at the library were like during the 1960s.</p>

<p>It also emphasised what Stephen had and hoped to achieve through his work with Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre and how far he felt the company had progressed since 1955.</p>

<p>What also shines through is how little money the company had had historically and how it was essentially being run on good-will. Actors were paid less than they should, volunteers were running front of house and back-stage, equipment was loaned rather than being bought or hired, plus there was no ability to make improvements in any meaningful way.</p>

<p>Despite committing to the 1963 season at undoubtedly his own personal expense, it becomes clear throughout the document that practically every aspect of the venue needed improvement in some way. Not surprising really, given it had always been a low-budget conversion of the Library’s existing Concert Room which had never been intended to house a theatre company.</p>

<p>This was essentially the nub of the problem, the Concert Room was not designed for this purpose and led to Stephen raising the issues he felt were essential for the company to continue. Broadly speaking these were: on-site storage for the seating rostra; permanent provision of lighting facilities (i.e. a grid); an improved fascia outside the theatre; new box office facilities; improved dressing rooms; kitchen facilities for catering; office space for administration; better financial support; that the council rather than the library take on the responsibility for improvements and funding.</p>

<p>Stephen’s ultimatum deliberately did not pull its punches whilst making clear that for the past eight years, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre had only worked due to dedication, ingenuity and making the most of a group of dedicated volunteers. They had carried the company so far, but it was obvious substantial money and support were needed if real change and longevity were to be encouraged.</p>

<p>If all this sounds as though it’s essentially saying the library is unfit for purpose and what we really need is a permanent purpose-built home, that’s probably fairly accurate. As, ultimately, that was still Stephen’s ambition, despite the fact the Town Council had voted on the building of a permanent theatre-in-the-round the previous year with the motion being narrowly defeated.</p>

<p>The ultimatum went to Scarborough Town Council and received little more than an acknowledgment of its receipt. There was no consideration from the council of the issues raised and no help was forthcoming between 1963 and 1965. Perhaps Stephen over-played his hand. From one perspective, he had had everything taken from him the previous year and was still able to run the company in 1963. Perhaps the council reasoned it was all a bluff and that Stephen had no intention of closing the company down without further assistance.</p>

<p>Whatever the reason, the company did indeed struggle on until 1965. At which point, at the end of the summer season, Stephen announced Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was to permanently close. It wasn’t a threat or a bargaining tool, it was – to Stephen’s mind – quite conclusively, the end of the Scarborough experiment.</p>

<p>It’s interesting to note that when the theatre did re-open – without Stephen Joseph on board due to terminal illness – in 1967, it was funded by both Scarborough Council and the Arts Council. Perhaps if Stephen had been good on his threat in 1963 and closed at the end of that season, it would have forced the town council to evaluate what might be lost earlier in the process and put in the support and facilities Stephen had asked for while he was still active with the company. If so, the Library Theatre might have taken a different path.</p>

<p>Who knows? The reality was, aside from the limited support of audiences and the library itself, Stephen felt he was unsupported in Scarborough which would reflect on his decisions regarding the company as life became increasingly difficult for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>
