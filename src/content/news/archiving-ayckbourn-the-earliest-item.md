<p><em>Archiving Ayckbourn is a regular feature in which Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist, Simon Murgatroyd, delves into the archives to highlight items of interest relating to the playwright and theatre in the round.</em></p>

<p><strong>The Earliest Item?</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>I’m frequently asked, as Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist, what’s the oldest item in archive?</p>

<p>I suppose it depends on how you define the oldest item. There’s a photo of Alan as a baby and, eventually, his birth certificate will no doubt become part of the Ayckbourn Archive. Those are undoubtedly the earliest or oldest items in the archive.</p>

<p>But I tend to view these questions through the prism of Alan’s theatrical career – as that is essentially what most people are interested in with regard to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p>So my natural inclination is to reframe the question, what’s the oldest item relating to Alan’s life and career in theatre held in archive?</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn (right) as Peter in Romeo &amp; Juliet (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>We can be quite definitive about this as it’s a photograph taken circa 1955 and reproduced here. This is a photograph of a school production of Shakespeare’s <em><strong><a href="/career/actor/romeo-and-juliet">Romeo and Juliet</a></strong></em> with Alan playing the role of Peter.</p>

<p>As fas as we know – and Alan’s recollections support this – this is the first time of note that Alan appeared on stage in front of an audience. It was a production staged by Haileybury College when Alan would have been – probably – 15 and it was directed by the French Master, <strong><a href="/life/influences/haileybury">Edgar Matthews</a></strong>, who was a huge early influence on Alan and who we can thank for not only introducing Alan to theatre but also providing him with the contacts which led to his first professional employment as an actor.</p>

<p>In the book <em>Conversations With Ayckbourn</em> by Ian Watson (Faber, 1988), Alan sheds some more light on this early foray onto the boards.</p>

<p>“There was a master at [Haileybury] school called&nbsp;Edgar Matthews, who had been a friend of&nbsp;<a href="/career/actor/donald-wolfit"><strong>Donald Wolfit</strong></a>‘s and indeed whose daughter had been Wolfit’s secretary. He was getting near to retirement age and was teaching French at the school, and once a year he organised a Shakespeare tour during the school holidays, taking a party of boys off to the Continent; to France and Germany and all over the place.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Details from picture showing Alan Ayckbourn (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>“He [Matthews] was a wizard of organisation. He also directed the plays and his wife and daughters acted in them. You weren’t allowed to go in for them the first year or two, but I auditioned when I was fifteen, I think, and I got into the first one I could. I got a small part, as Peter, in&nbsp;<em>Romeo &amp; Juliet</em>.</p>

<p>“And off we went to Holland and we toured around there. And that was magic. That was the first real ‘theatre’ theatre I’d experienced, which was just terrific – you know, all that spirit gum and greasepaint.</p>

<p>“We played very odd places: some were quite good theatres, other places were quite primitive. I don’t think our Shakespeare was earth-shattering, but it was fairly good. I was just acting. This was tremendous. And I think Edgar must have thought I had a bit of talent.”</p>

<p>Here then lay the foundations of Alan’s interest in theatre. It’s worth emphasising that when Alan left Haileybury in 1956 for a career in theatre, his aim was to become an actor. There’s no indication at all he was interested in becoming a playwright or director, he was fixated on becoming an actor and that can be seen in his career path between 1956 and 1959.</p>

<p>The idea of becoming a writer and a director only comes later with the encouragement and at the suggestion of his most important influence, Stephen Joseph, after Alan joined Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in Scarborough in 1957. Stephen also – more importantly – provided the opportunities for Alan to write and direct.</p>

<p>Returning to Haileybury, Alan would have had limited opportunities to indulge his interest in theatre. His only encounter with plays would have been through his English Literature lessons as there were no classes or courses in drama or theatre. It was only the enthusiasm of Edgar Matthews and his annual productions that gave students the chance to experience acting and stage-craft at the college.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan &amp; the Haileybury company on the Queen Mary (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>The year afterwards, 1956, Alan was again chosen for the Shakespeare tour, this time to America. Matthews was retiring from teaching and decided to go all out with a four week tour of North America with <strong><a href="/career/actor/macbeth">Macbeth</a></strong>, leaving on the Queen Mary and returning on the Queen Elizabeth liners.</p>

<p>The idea that Matthews thought Alan had “a bit of talent” confirmed by the fact he was chosen to play Macduff in the production; a huge leap from a minor role to a major one in just the course of the year.</p>

<p>Sadly, we don’t have a photograph of Alan as Macduff, but we do have a photograph of the company on board the Queen Mary – reproduced here – as well as Alan’s memories which he, again, recorded in <em>Conversations With Ayckbourn</em>.</p>

<p>“He’d [Haileybury teacher&nbsp;Edgar Matthews] marked me down as a comedy actor. They were doing&nbsp;<em>Macbeth</em>, and I didn’t want to play that boring old porter, so I plunged in hoping to play Macbeth. He really didn’t think I was up to that – I didn’t really have the weight, I was a six-stone weakling – but he did cast me as Macduff.</p>

<p>“And this year he decided to really go to town. He was going to take us round America. We went over on the Queen Mary and we came back on the Queen Elizabeth – the first, not the second – and that in itself was worth all the money in the world: a group of public schoolboys, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, getting themselves absolutely slewed all the way over – it was wonderful.</p>

<p>“And this was touring gone mad, because we had all the joy of touring with none of the professional responsibility. We didn’t actually care if the show never went on. We arrived and we went up the East Coast: we played in Maine, at the university there, and then we went up into Canada and played in Ottawa. And we played in Quebec, then we went down to Niagara and we played there; then we came through to Pittsburgh – and along the way we also took in Boston, Montreal, Peterborough and Washington DC.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Detail of Alan on the Queen Mary (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>“It was a very strange tour, but we did see a hell of a lot of America by Greyhound coach. I don’t think the whole thing was more than about four weeks.”</p>

<p>There are two extant reviews of the tour in archive and Alan comes off from well from both with one describing him giving “the most satisfying acting” of the company and the other saying he “dominated strongly”.</p>

<p>So these two photos of Alan in <em>Romeo and Juliet</em> and preparing to tour <em>Macbeth</em> are the earliest items in archive relating to Alan Ayckbourn’s theatrical career. Alan would professionally act from 1956 to 1964 before pursuing the careers he is most well known for, actor and director.</p>

<p>It’s perhaps interesting to note that Alan’s earliest steps into theatre were in Shakespeare’s plays, but that Shakespeare then rarely features in his professional career of almost seven decades. He played Claudius in <em><strong><a href="/career/actor/hamlet">Hamlet</a></strong></em> in January 1962 (Stephen Joseph’s intolerance of intervals pushed to the absolute limit with a four hour production with no breaks!) and he can also be heard on a vinyl recording of <em><strong><a href="/career/actor/much-ado-about-nothing">Much Ado About Nothing</a></strong></em> released in 1964. Other than that, his only other foray into Shakespeare was directing a production of <em><strong><a href="/career/directing-career/othello">Othello</a></strong></em> in 1990 at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round.</p>
