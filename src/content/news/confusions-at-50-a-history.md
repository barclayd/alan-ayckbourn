<p><em>2024 marks the 50th anniversary of Alan Ayckbourn’s much loved work <strong><a href="/plays/confusions">Confusions</a></strong>.</em></p>

<p><em>To mark the anniversary, the Ayckbourn Blog is looking at the play in several articles. Here we take a detailed look at the creation and history of this work.</em></p>

<p><strong>Confusions at 50 – A History</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>1974 was an exceptional year for Alan Ayckbourn. It saw not only the world premiere of two of his classic works, <em><strong><a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a></strong></em> and <em>Confusions</em>, but also the West End debut of his famed trilogy <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></strong></em>.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/1974-confusions-1-w300.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="300" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p>Yet although we would now classify all of these works as classic Ayckbourn plays, it is worth emphasising that <em>Confusions</em> was a play written purely out of necessity and to solve a practical problem. It evolved out of two existing unsuccessful works and Alan Ayckbourn wrote it almost specifically for the company he was working with at the time and would not have believed it would go to to such a long and successful life.</p>

<p>In 1974, Alan had been the Artistic Director at <strong><a href="/career/ayckbourn-and-the-sjt/sjt-venues/theatre-in-the-round-at-the-library-theatre">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre,</a></strong> Scarborough, for two years. Comfortably in situ, his ambition was to expand the company’s programme. Since 1961, the company had been confined to just a summer season of approximately thirteen weeks. Alan found this unsatisfactory, not least in the difficulty it presented in keeping a repertory company of actors together.</p>

<p>However, Alan’s ambitions were being obstructed by the increasingly less than amicable relationship with North Yorkshire Libraries Committee, which had run Scarborough Library – where the company was based – since 1972. The committee was keen to regain the permanent use of the Concert Room and adjoining spaces, which were hired and used by Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre during the summer months. Rather than promoting expansion, they favoured even less of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre despite its success and its increasing prominence in the national theatre conversation.</p>

<p>In 1974, Alan proposed to expand to a forty week season, essentially extending the summer season through the autumn and winter into the spring. In response, the library offered him a slight expansion to the summer season and then the use of a smaller room, rather than the Concert Room, for two days a week during the autumn and winter.</p>

<p>Alan’s solution was to relaunch touring – again there had been no tours by the company since 1961 – working around the limited use of Scarborough Library in order to keep the company together and to move it forward.</p>

<p>Alan realised that it would be desirable to have one of his own plays produced during this autumn / winter season, but therein lay a problem. Alan had premiered <em>Absent Friends</em> earlier in the year and the obvious solution would have been to revive this summer hit.</p>

<p>However, Alan had already sold the rights to the West End producer Michael Codron, whose intention was to open it in early 1975. As a result, Alan was unable to extend the Scarborough run into the winter season and needed a new solution.</p>

<p>His solution was to write a work consisting of five one act plays with minimal propping and which would throw the spotlight on the actors themselves, showing off their skills as each took on a variety of very different roles in the ensemble piece.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Janet Dale, Christopher Godwin and Eileen O’Brien in Drinking Companion (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>He also did not wish to write another full-length play so soon after <em>Absent Friends</em> and was restricted to the five actors he knew he had confirmed for the winter season: Janet Dale, Christopher Godwin, Stephen Mallatratt, Eileen O’Brien and Stanley Page. It also had to be a piece which could easily tour and work in a variety of stage spaces.</p>

<p>Alan’s experience of writing one act plays was very limited though – professionally he had had just one of note produced, <em><strong><a href="/plays/countdown">Countdown</a></strong></em>, as part of the multi-author anthology, <em>Mixed Doubles</em>. Prior to that, he had only written one acts for amateur production early in his professional career during the late 1950s and early 1960s.</p>

<p>Alan decided he would begin with an existing work, <em>Mother Figure</em>, which had been written the previous year for an entertainment entitled <em>Mixed Blessings</em>; a sequel to <em>Mixed Doubles</em> with various writers submitting short plays themed to parents and children. <em>Mixed Blessings</em> was performed for a week in Horsham, Sussex, apparently on a pre-West End tour, but was never seen again. Alan decided to reclaim <em>Mother Figure</em> and constructed <em>Confusions</em> around it.</p>

<p>Surviving hand-written notes held in the Ayckbourn Archive at the <strong><a href="http://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a></strong> at the University of York show originally intended <em>Confusions</em> to consist of six one act plays. His initial idea being they would all be loosely themed to marriage, from wedding to parenthood to break-up.</p>

<p>With the first play in place, Alan began work on the other pieces, quickly reducing them to five plays rather than six and dropping the over-arching theme. Alan would later say the over-riding impetus became to create an ensemble piece specifically for this company, which would not only show off their talents but also be an interesting challenge for them to perform.</p>

<p>With <em>Mother Figure</em> in place, Alan turned to another work from earlier in the year which had made little impact and had barely been seen. On 20 May 1974, the BBC showed for the first – and last – time, Alan’s only produced screenplay, <strong><em><a href="/plays/service-not-included">Service Not Included</a></em></strong>. This half-hour drama was set in a hotel at an end-of-conference party and followed in the footsteps of a waiter, Jace, seeing and hearing only what he does.</p>

<p>Given the broadcast had, Alan joked, only been seen by “five people” he decided to take the idea behind it and repurpose it for the stage. In <em>Between Mouthfuls</em>, he created a technically challenging piece in which a waiter in a restaurant moves between two couples at two tables. We hear only what he hears as conversations fade in and out as he moves between tables, whose patrons have more in connection than we initially realise.</p>

<p><em>Drinking Companion</em> was meanwhile inspired by his observations whilst staying at the Royal Hotel in Scarborough with his partner whilst renovations were taken place at his home in the old town. Whilst – arguably – the most popular piece, <em>Gosforth’s Fête</em> was inspired by the playwright’s experiences of civic events and their penchant for going wrong!</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Stanley Page &amp; Stephen Mallatratt in A Talk in the Park (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>“I love things when they’re set up and go wrong; there’s something very funny about human dignity. Civic occasions are wonderful in small towns, too, because they don’t quite have the Lord Chancellor to organise them. So vases of flowers fall over. Every summer in Scarborough I always go to the Mayor’s tent. It always rains, and the Mayor and Mayoress sit there and nobody turns up. There’s a great pile of sandwiches, the band’s playing, the cricketers are cursing, and everything’s a washout.”</p>

<p>To end the play, Alan chose to write a dying fall with <em>A Talk in the Park</em>: a series of five monologues. Alan’s intention being after seeing these actors in an ensemble playing 20 very different roles  to then see them revert to individuals and for the audience to ask, as the playwright noted, “Is that all there were of them?”</p>

<p>This structure would later prove to be contentious. The comic high and near farce of <em>Gosforth’s Fête</em> would quickly become a favourite with directors and audiences frequently leading to the decision to end the night on a comic high by dropping the more introspective <em>A Talk in the Park</em>. To say this misunderstands the playwright’s original intent and does a great dis-service to both the playwright, the play and the actors is something of an understatement and has disappointed Alan throughout the decades.</p>

<p><em>Confusions</em> opened on 30 September 1974 in the large lecture room at The Library Theatre. The size of the room – as opposed to the Concert Room where productions were normally staged – meant that productions could not be staged in-the-round and <em>Confusions</em> has to be performed three-sided. But this was a play which had flexibility baked into its DNA, which considering what lay ahead was a necessity.</p>

<p>The play ran, initially, until 9 October in Scarborough before embarking on a tour which visited venues in the likes of Hull, Workington and Kendal.</p>

<p>The company then returned to Scarborough and launched a local tour which has become the stuff of legend within the history of the Scarborough company. A repertory programme of three plays was programmed consisting of <em>Confusions</em>, <em>On Approval</em> by Frederick Lonsdale and <em>But Fred, Freud is Dead</em> by Peter Terson. These would tour to the Sun Lounge at Filey, Whitby Spa Theatre and Scarborough’s Library Theatre.</p>

<p>However, each venue had different staging requirements which meant every week between 12 November and 21 December, <em>Confusions</em> and the other plays would visit Filey on Tuesdays to be performed in-the-round; on Wednesdays and Thursdays they would visit Whitby to be performed in the proscenium arch. On Fridays and Saturdays, the company would return to Scarborough and perform in a three-sided configuration.</p>

<p>One imagines it was quite a confusing couple of months for everyone concerned!</p>

<p>Despite the torturous touring programme, the season was a success and <em>Confusions</em> was revived for the 1975 Scarborough summer season, now performed in-the-round in the Concert Room.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sup><sub>Between Mouthfuls in the world premiere production of Confusions (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></sup></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Typically, Alan’s plays would be optioned for the West End once they had opened in Scarborough and been put through their paces. However, Alan sold <em>Confusions</em> to his London producer, Michael Codron, before it even had its first performance. Codron read the play and was very enthusiastic about it. Alan was quoted as saying that he had never sold a play to London so quickly and expected it to open in the West End in early 1975, barely weeks after it had finished its autumn / winter tour.</p>

<p>Codron’s only request apparently was to make the connection between the plays more clear. Contrary to popular belief, every play is connected, albeit it’s quite an obscure connection at times! In <em>Mother Figure</em>, Lucy is married to Harry, who we see trying to call her. Harry is the businessman who appears in <em>Drinking Companion</em> and who is served drinks by a waiter. This is the same waiter who appears in <em>Between Mouthfuls</em> (at presumably the same hotel), who serves Mrs Pearce and her husband. Mrs Pearce appears in <em>Gosforth’s Fête</em> and mention is also made of her husband. In the final play, <em>A Talk In The Park</em>, the most subtle and easily missed connection is that Doreen is the former wife of Gosforth, who Milly mentions in <em>Gosforth’s Fête</em>. Doreen talks about her former husband being a landlord, which is a subtle reference to Gosforth.</p>

<p><em>Confusions</em> was also a favourite of Alan’s agent, the famed literary agent Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay. Having read it, she immediately sent Alan a telegram declaring it contained some of his best writing.</p>

<p>With a London production fast-tracked for 1975, Codron had high hopes that Alan’s frequent West End collaborator, the director Eric Thompson, would return to direct <em>Confusions</em> having had enormous success with <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em> and <em>The Norman Conquests</em>. However, Eric’s packed schedule already included directing the West End premieres of <em>Absent Friends</em> and Alan and Andrew Lloyd Webber’s musical <em><strong><a href="/plays/jeeves">Jeeves</a></strong></em> as well as the North American premiere of <em>The Norman Conquests</em>.</p>

<p>In his place, Codron appointed Alan Strachan as director marking the first time he would work with Alan. This began a long and productive relationship which led to Strachan subsequently directing the London premiere of <strong><em><a href="/plays/just-between-ourselves">Just Between Ourselves</a></em></strong> and overseeing the recast of several subsequent London Ayckbourn productions as well as working at Alan’s home theatre in Scarborough.</p>

<p>The anticipated 1975 opening of <em>Confusions</em> was delayed however by a year due to difficulties in casting the play. Much to Alan’s chagrin – and a frequent feature of the West End productions – Codron required star casting to sell the play, despite the fact it was intended as an ensemble piece with no actor taking prominence over the others.</p>

<p>Eventually, the husband and wife team of John Alderton and Pauline Collins were cast and a decision made which would have long-term ramifications for the play. As has been previously stated, each of the plays gave each of the five actors a chance to shine and no single figure featured more than the others. For the London production, a decision was made that John Alderton would be seen as the star of the plays and would play Harry in <em>Drinking Companion</em>, the waiter in <em>Between Mouthfuls</em> and Gosforth in <em>Gosforth’s Fête</em> – turning what was always an ensemble piece into a star-driven piece. It’s not known who took this decision – likely either Codron or Alderton – but it severely altered the nature of the play and had repercussions for it in the future.</p>

<p>Alan has always had an issue with the way the West End distorts his plays, particularly in the frequent need to attach star names to London productions for what are, essentially, ensemble plays. While there is no doubting John Alderton gave a well-received performance in <em>Confusions</em>, there is also no doubt the London production became a vehicle for both him and Pauline Collins. The production not only went against the grain of the playwright’s intentions but also lost the subtle linking thread between the plays; somewhat ironic given the tenuous links had only been added at the request of Codron.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Sheila Gish, John Alderton &amp; Pauline Collins in the West End premiere of Confusions (© John Haynes)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>By ignoring the author’s intentions, this had an unforeseen impact given the abnormal precedence normally given to West End productions over the original productions – which Alan generally considers to be the definitive productions. Several major publications on Alan Ayckbourn – wrongly taking the London production as definitive when it was not even directed by Alan – incorrectly state it is not the same Waiter in the two acts of the play and, as a result, cannot with certainty say what links <em>Drinking Companion</em> and <em>Between Mouthfuls</em>. To clarify the author’s intention, the Waiter is the same person in both plays and one actor should not play all the featured male roles. It is an ensemble work where every actor is given the chance to shine.</p>

<p>Matters were not helped when Alderton broke his ankle and performed one performance in a wheelchair and a number of others with his leg in a cast; one can only imagine that this sucked even more attention away from the rest of the company.</p>

<p>Alan ironically recalls Alderton later telling him that <em>Confusions</em> has been a very difficult piece and very tiring as an actor; Alan did not correct him by pointing out it was only exhausting as he had taken the lion’s share of the roles which were intended to be spread over the entire company!</p>

<p>Indeed, the production seemed to be cursed with bad luck. The actress Sheila Gish fell ill during the pre-West End tour and in a run of bad luck was also involved in a car accident which led to her missing six weeks from the run, whilst Derek Fowlds had to take a break during the London run due to contracting mumps. Once the play was settled in London, Pauline Collins also became pregnant which precluded an extended run for the production and led to extensive and frequent modifications of Collins’ costumes to accommodate the pregnancy.</p>

<p>Alan subsequently noted that it was not necessarily Collin’s pregnancy which precluded an extended run of the production, rather the fact they could not find someone as good in the role(s) to replace her. He recalls being impressed by Collins’ performance in the play and that made it difficult to find someone to take over a part which involved playing four very different characters. This is backed up by a letter from Peggy Ramsay in the Ayckbourn Archive which states “The producer tried to recast, but could not get the people who [sic] wanted, so is not going to continue with an inferior cast, or one which won’t draw.”</p>

<p><em>Confusions</em> opened at the Apollo Theatre on 19 May 1976 and had an eight month run, receiving some very positive reviews. The production was a success, but not to the extent of the recent clutch of Ayckbourn plays which had taken the West End by storm. The reason for this was probably due to the nature of it being a series of one-act plays, rather than any particular fault with the evening itself.</p>

<p><em>Confusions</em> closed in London in January 1977 and was immediately in demand from regional repertory theatres, proving to be a popular and successful play to the present day. It is also the only Ayckbourn play to have an illegal production on record!</p>

<p>In November 1978, a newspaper article came to Alan’s attention noting the success of Ashford Theatre Workshop in the Spelthorne drama festival with a production of <em>Mother Figure</em>, despite the fact that – although published – it had not yet been released for amateur production. This led to an article in Amateur Stage magazine in which Samuel French Ltd warned amateur companies against producing restricted or unreleased plays and that legal action was being considered relating to the unauthorised production of <em>Mother Figure</em>.</p>

<p>The popularity of <em>Confusions</em> over the decades was confirmed by the publishers Samuel French in 2014, when it was named as the most performed Ayckbourn play by amateur companies between 1996 and 2013.</p>

<p>Notably, In 1985, <em>Confusions</em> became the first Ayckbourn play to be included as part of the National Curriculum in the United Kingdom as part of the ‘O’ Level syllabus and subsequently became a set text for GCSE examinations. This has led to it being published by several companies and is the only Ayckbourn play for which there has been a student edition in print since 1983.</p>

<p>Notably, in 2015, Alan Ayckbourn directed the play for the first time since its 1974 world premiere at the Library Theatre with a revival as part of the Stephen Joseph Theatre’s 60th anniversary. The opening night of the play coinciding with the opening of <strong><a href="http://www.a-round-town.com">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> on 14 July 1955. This production also marked the play’s New York premiere with it touring to the <em>Brits Off Broadway</em> festival at the 59E59 Theaters in 2016.</p>

<p><em>Confusions</em> has remained a perennial Ayckbourn favourite ever since the 1980s. It has proved particularly popular with amateur and school companies where the different acts are often performed in drama festivals or in various combinations.</p>

<p>Fifty years on, it stands as one of his most popular, revived and successful works. Not bad for a play which was written as a solution to a problem and for which the author had no long term ambitions.</p>
