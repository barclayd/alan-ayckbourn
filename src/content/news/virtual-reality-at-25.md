<p>Twenty five years ago today a new Ayckbourn play premiered at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in Scarborough.</p>

<p>Which is, pretty much, par for the course every year.</p>

<p>Except, this specific play – <em>Virtual Reality</em> – was, shortly afterwards, withdrawn by the playwright and has never been seen again. </p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Virtual Reality poster (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>It’s probably been forgotten now even by the most ardent Ayckbourn fans. So what was <em>Virtual Reality</em>? And why, although still considered part of the playwright’s official canon, has it never been seen again?</p>

<p><em>Virtual Reality</em> was the first new Ayckbourn play of the new millennium. It premiered at the <strong><a href="/career/ayckbourn-and-the-sjt/sjt-venues/stephen-joseph-theatre">Stephen Joseph Theatre</a></strong>, Scarborough, on 3 February 2000 and was, unusually, a play written specifically for the end-stage rather than in-the-round.</p>

<p>It came off the back of a run of considerable successes. As with any career of more than six decades, Alan’s career has had its fair share of highs and lows. At the time, Alan was cresting a peak with notable successes such as&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/things-we-do-for-love">Things We Do For Love</a></em></strong> (1997),&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/comic-potential">Comic Potential</a></em></strong>&nbsp;(1998) and&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/house-and">House &amp; Garden</a></em></strong> (1999). All had performed extremely well in Scarborough and gone on to acclaimed and popular runs in the West End. As such, his follow-up to the extraordinary&nbsp;<em>House &amp; Garden</em>&nbsp;– which was also due to open at the National Theatre during the summer of 2000 – was much anticipated.</p>

<p>In hindsight, there is something of <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></strong></em> here. Having written one of the ambitious and epic works of his career in 1973 with the acclaimed trilogy, he drew himself in for his next work creating the single set, real-time drama <em><strong><a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a></strong></em>.</p>

<p>Likewise in 1999, having completed the epic <em>House &amp; Garden</em> – two plays running simultaneously in two auditorium with a single cast – Alan decided to go in the completely opposite direction for his follow-up.</p>

<p><em>Virtual Reality</em> was an intimate, small cast, relationship-based play written specifically for the Stephen Joseph Theatre’s smaller, end-stage McCarthy Theatre. It was inspired by Alan’s desire to investigate the devastating potential of relationships based on sexual attraction – a theme he had previously tackled in&nbsp;<em>Things We Do For Love</em>&nbsp;– and an incident in a restaurant, where he observed everyone at one table was talking into their mobiles rather than talking to each other – sadly, something unusual then but all too common now!</p>

<p>This wasn’t a particularly new theme for Alan. He had dealt with people’s inability to communicate with each other frequently before, but here he delved into what he felt was a growing phenomenon. How, despite having the technology to communicate more easily and with more people than ever before, we seem to be losing the ability to truly communicate with each other.</p>

<p>It is a theme he would return to with more success in the&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/damsels-in-distress">Damsels In Distress</a></em></strong>&nbsp;plays and, most notably,&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/private-fears-in-public-places">Private Fears In Public Places</a></em></strong>.</p>

<p>It also dealt with the idea of how our relationships are all interconnected and how one person’s actions may have unforeseen knock-on effects. In the play, the protagonist, Alex, has an affair with a young waitress, Cassie. It not only destroys his own marriage but also that of his closest friends, whose own relationship is in the balance and draws from that of Alex and his wife, Penny. Ultimately, Alex’s actions alienate him and affect all the relationships around him, leaving him alone.</p>

<p>Interestingly, Alan would return ton this theme with considerably more success with <em><strong><a href="/plays/anno-domino">Anno Domino</a></strong></em> (2020), his play which premiered during lockdown as an audio work and which charts the effects a divorce has on three generations of the same family.</p>

<p> The pervasive nature of technology – again, another favourite theme of the playwright’s – is also featured in that Alex and his business partner, Barney, are the creators of Viewdows (essentially, holographic windows offering views where there are none) which, during the course of the play, start malfunctioning.</p>

</div>

<p>The play is also unusual in that it is also set specifically in London, beginning a cycle of four consecutive plays set in the capital. In contemporary interviews, Alan felt this was due to the increased amount of time he had spent in the past few years in the city. </p>

<p>The play opened at the Stephen Joseph Theatre before embarking on a short UK tour. It was met with lukewarm or negative reviews and the audience response was generally poor. Alan himself recognised there were issues with the play and he later admitted that he had begun to realise the play was not what he intended as early as the rehearsal process.</p>

<p>So what went wrong?</p>

<p>Unusually for Alan, the main criticism was the characters were largely unsympathetic. It’s a fair criticism, to be honest, and given the playwright has often said audiences need to empathise to some extent with all the characters, it’s an uphill task for a play when there’s a struggle to find even one character to empathise with.</p>

<p>All of them are largely selfish and self-obsessed with little wider regard or recognition of what damage their actions are causing. In fact, <em>Virtual Reality</em> is something of an oddity. As not only are the characters largely unsympathetic, but the one character who has some self recognition is Alex, who – quite unusually for an Ayckbourn play – is given to speaking monologues about his situation.</p>

<p>That is atypical for Alan Ayckbourn. The traditional Ayckbourn man is not one for introspection nor self-recongition and monologues – there are probably more of those in this play than the rest of his 91 plays combined! As Alan has said, people only tend to talk in monologues to fill the space when someone else isn’t talking (a good example being the second act of <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em> when Geoffrey is talking to the silent, suicidal Eva). But generally, not out loud to ourselves as though we’re characters in a Shakespearean play. It served actually only to make Alex seem an even more distant and remote figure.</p>

<p>All this was fatal for a play about relationships falling apart and people getting hurt. If we don’t care for those people to begin with, we don’t care about their relationships, thus rendering the play rather pointless.</p>

<p>One of the other unexpected criticisms was also the language, which generated substantive debate in the Scarborough Evening News’ letters pages. Most of the swearing comes from a hard-headed, alcoholic agent and her use of ‘offensive’ language was both realistic and warranted; the playwright has even suggested the character was inspired by his own famed literary agent Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay.</p>

<p>In a play about younger couples centred on technology, it’s perhaps worth saying that – certainly at this point – Alan’s core audience in Scarborough was older and there had been complaints about his use of ‘strong’ language previously, although not to this extent.</p>

<p>This irked the playwright as he felt it was something of a myster what had triggered people with this specific play, giving he had been using language of this nature consistently in his plays since 1974! He also argued strongly that it is a writer’s job to reflect the language of the period they are writing in and to suggest that what was once seen as ‘strong’ language is now anything but an accepted and commonplace part of the English vernacular is naive.&nbsp;Two years later, he would wonder why the far stronger and more prolific language in his production of Tim Firth’s popular play <em><strong><a href="/career/directing-career/the-safari-party">The Safari Party</a></strong></em> generated next to no complaints or notice at the same theatre!</p>

<p>But there is also the fact that none of<em> Virtual Reality</em>‘s  individual themes seem to come together or mesh as they do in all Alan’s best work. The sub-theme of the malfunctioning and sabotaged Viewdows doesn’t ever seem to successfully relate to the play’s other themes, even when it takes centre stage at the climax of the play.</p>

<figure><figcaption><sub>Celia Nelson, Andrew Havill &amp; Susie Blake in Virtual Reality (<a href="http://www.bartpics.co.uk">© Tony Bartholomew</a>)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>And this was also a problematic end. Alex, alone and lost, ends the play looking at the malfunctioning Viewdow and its view of a garden where the virtual gardener has apparently run off. In one of his few pro-active and decisive moments, he apparently steps into the machine and takes on the role of the gardener.</p>

<p>Whilst it is undoubtedly a striking climatic action and image for the play, given the mechanics of the machine have been explained earlier it is unclear whether this is real (in which case, Alex would presumably be electrocuted instantly), imagined, metaphorical or a sort of deus ex machina. The lack of clarity and logic arguably makes the climax confusing and unclear. There doesn’t seem to have been a journey for the protagonist.</p>

<p>That is not to say endings have to be literal, obviously. In his 2004 work, <em><strong><a href="/plays/drowning-on-dry-land">Drowning on Dry Land</a></strong></em>, the protagonist, Charlie, finds himself somehow at the top of a folly which it’s been repeatedly noted there is no way to the top. But in a play about achievement and celebrity, it caps off the journey of Charles. He finally succeeds and achieves something that no-one else does. But it is pyrrhic, it’s a meaningless achievement and unseen to all but him. No more valid than the vacuous and precarious fame as a celebrity he had previously experienced in his life.</p>

<p>Taken together, all these were faults that <em>Virtual Reality</em> could not escape. Previously, Alan has returned and refined works that haven’t been as successful as he hoped when initially written (for example <em><strong><a href="/plays/body-language">Body Language</a></strong></em> or the musical <strong><em><a href="/plays/jeeves">By Jeeves</a></em></strong>). But he has never talked about or considered going back to <em>Virtual Reality</em>.</p>

<p>Instead, soon after the tour was finished, the play was quietly withdrawn and prohibited from ever being produced. It has never been published and the only way to access it is through the Ayckbourn Archive at the <strong><a href="http://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a></strong> at the University of York or at the British Library manuscript collection.</p>

<p>And in 2020, he confirmed it would never be allowed to be produced again whilst his copyright applies to it; that’s 70 years after his death.</p>

<p>Since 1959, there have only been nine withdrawn Ayckbourn plays. The majority – <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-square-cat">The Square Cat</a></strong></em>, <strong><em><a href="/plays/love-after-all">Love After All</a></em></strong>, <strong><em><a href="/plays/dads-tale">Dad’s Tale</a></em></strong>, <em><strong><a href="/plays/standing-room-only">Standing Room Only</a></strong></em>, <strong><em><a href="/plays/christmas-v-mastermind">Christmas V Mastermind</a></em></strong> and <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-sparrow">The Sparrow</a></strong></em> – were all written during the earliest years of his writing career.</p>

<p>Of the rest, only <strong><em><a href="/plays/jeeves">Jeeves</a></em></strong> (1975) and <strong><em><a href="/plays/the-musical-jigsaw-play">The Musical Jigsaw Play</a></em></strong> (1994) have all been withdrawn – although <em>Jeeves</em> was successfully revised and rewritten as <em>By Jeeves</em> (1996). <em>Virtual Reality</em> is the most recent of his withdrawn works and his only play – as opposed to musical – which has been withdrawn since <em>The Sparrow</em> in 1967.</p>

<p>So whilst not successful in itself, perhaps it is notable for being a rare failure that, even in the playwright’s mind, is not redeemable.</p>

<p>Regardless of what the playwright thinks of the play, if you saw it in 2000, you witnessed a very rare piece of Ayckbourn history that none of us will see again during our lifetimes.</p>

<p>Which also goes to show, that – even after a writing career of – then – 41 years, you still aren’t guaranteed to always get it right.</p>
