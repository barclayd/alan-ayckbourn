<p>As we’ve previously noted, Alan Ayckbourn’s <em><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves">How the Other Half Loves</a></em> celebrates its 50th anniversary this year. In this column, we’re going to look at several items held in Archive pertaining to the classic play.</p>

<p><em>How the Other Half Loves</em> premiered at the <a href="http://thesjt.alanayckbourn.net/page1/styled/">Library Theatre</a>, Scarborough, on 31 July 1969 and was Alan Ayckbourn seventh play for the venue (and his ninth play in total). Alan had been appointed <a href="http://careers.alanayckbourn.net/styled-6/index.html">Director of Productions</a> for the season and directed the majority of plays that year – this is arguably the first of Alan’s many ‘rep’ companies in Scarborough.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption><em>How the Other Half Loves world premiere production (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>The photograph is a a relatively rare image from the world premiere production, directed by Alan. It features the entire cast including Elisabeth Sladen (far left) who would go onto fame in the BBC series <em>Doctor Who</em> as Sarah Jane Smith as well as Stephanie Turner (third from left), who would have great success as actor and director on stage as well as on television – some readers may recall her as the original Juliet Bravo.</p>

<p>The photo offers one of the clearest views of Alan’s composite set design and the infamous dinner sequence in which two dinner parties play out in two flats on two different nights. It’s a favourite image from Alan’s early plays in that it’s a very rare case insight into the layout of an Ayckbourn in-the-round production. Very little survives regarding how Alan staged his plays in-the-round during the 1950s and 1960s, so images like this are always important for anyone researching Ayckbourn during this period.</p>

<p>The second item is a piece of correspondence between Alan’s agent, <a href="/life/influences/ramsay-margaret">Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay</a> and Alan, discussing the same scene. Within it, she highlights how difficult she perceives the scene may be to stage and whether it will confuse the audience: time has proven that audiences have no difficulty appreciating the scene and it is regarded as one of the most famous scenes in Alan Ayckbourn’s play canon. The letter also refers to the ’60s theatre impressario Peter Bridge, who produced <em><a href="http://relaitvelyspeaking.alanayckbourn.net">Relatively Speaking</a></em> and <em>How the Other Half Loves</em> in the West End.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/1969-how-the-other-half-loves-correspondence.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="454" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><em>Correspondence between Margaret Ramsay &amp; Alan Ayckbourn (© Haydonning Ltd)</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Bridge was responsible for the casting of the famed Robert Morley in the West End premiere of <em>How the Other Half Loves</em> and, thus, must shoulder<a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/?p=280"> </a>a lot of the blame for what was to follow. Morley – as the previous article on the <a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/?p=280">blog</a> explores – was a huge stage and screen star, but who was wont to take enormous liberties with the source material to better suit what he felt was funny. In the case of <em>How the Other Half Loves</em>, it completely unbalanced what was written as an ensemble piece and led to the play being regarded as little more than a frivolous farce for many years; fortunately its reputation was salvaged and its qualities better appreciated subsequently.</p>

<p>Morley’s appeal – particularly as a huge box office draw – can be seen in the next two pieces. A flyer for the West End production, in which it can be safely assumed that Morley is the sole reason anyone would want to see the play! At least, Alan is credited although it is in association with <em>Relatively Speaking</em> just to emphasise he’s not a complete unknown. Given Morley’s role of Frank Foster has no more lines than anyone else in the actual play, one can imagine why Alan recalls being physically sick during rehearsals in response to how Morley deformed the play. If you look closely, you can see the credits include Brian Miller – Elisabeth Sladen’s husband. He appeared in the world premiere production of <em>How the Other Half Loves</em> at the Library Theatre and is the first actor to transfer in an Ayckbourn play from Alan’s home theatre into the West End.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/1970-how-the-other-half-loves-flyer-02.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="611" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><em>Flyer for the West End premiere of How the Other Half Loves held in The Ayckbourn Archive</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>The second related piece is the – extremely pink – cover of the programme for the first Australian production of an Ayckbourn play. Following the closure of <em>How the Other Half Loves</em> in the West End in 1962 after 869 performances, Morley persuaded the producers to take the play on tour to Canada and Australia – despite neither country having been exposed to Ayckbourn in any significant way. But then this wasn’t about Alan as the programme cover from the Australian leg of the tour demonstrates. It makes it very clear what the sole reason for seeing this play is as can be seen from the many pictures of Mr Robert Morley and not even a passing mention of the playwright!</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/1973-how-the-other-half-loves-australia-cover.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="517" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><em>A Robert Morley-centric Australian programme held in The Ayckbourn Archive.</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Which does lead us to a rare example of one of my least favourite items in the Ayckbourn Archive! Back in the 1970s, it was obviously possible not to credit the playwright on the cover of the programme. Not something which would be approved today. However, it can happen and in 2009 Alan Ayckbourn revived <em>How the Other Half Loves</em> for its fortieth anniversary at his home theatre, the Stephen Joseph Theatre, Scarborough. Somehow the programme managed to omit Alan’s writing credit not only on the programme cover, but also the title page and the credits page – quite an achievement! Only two-thirds of the way through does Alan get a writing credit in his biography for one of his most famous works. Naturally, it’s the one part of the programme he wrote himself.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/2009-how-the-other-half-loves-cover.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="561" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><em>The programme cover for the 40th anniversary revival of How the Other Half Loves. The playwright is Alan Ayckbourn… </em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>As can be imagined, Alan was not terribly happy with the Press &amp; Marketing Manager – and were it any other theatre, one presumes such a blatant breach of the license would have had more severe repercussions! Which just goes to show how essential it is to proof-read if you don’t want to face the ire of playwrights and Archivists in the decades to come.</p>
