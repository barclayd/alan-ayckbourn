<p><em>Although not as famous as the plays which immediately followed it, Alan Ayckbourn’s <a href="/plays/time-and-time-again">Time &amp; Time Again</a> is a crucial play in his development as a writer.</em></p>

<p><em>Premiered at the <a href="/career/ayckbourn-and-the-sjt/sjt-venues/theatre-in-the-round-at-the-library-theatre">Library Theatre</a>, Scarborough, on 8 July 1971, it is regarded as the first of his tragi-comics work epitomised so successfully by his next play, <a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a>.</em></p>

<p><em>It is also the first play to feature what was later dubbed the ‘Ayckbourn man’ and in its lead character, Leonard, Time &amp; Time Again laid the foundation for a whole host of classic Ayckbourn characters such as Norman in <a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a>, Colin in <a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a>, Dennis in <a href="/plays/just-between-ourselves">Just Between Ourselves</a> and many more.</em></p>

<p><em>Here Alan Ayckbourn talks about creating Time &amp; Time Again.</em></p>

<p><strong>Creating Time &amp; Time Again</strong> by Alan Ayckbourn</p>

<p>I wrote <em>Time &amp; Time Again</em> in 1971. It came  immediately before <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> and immediately after a manic piece of theatre that never got anywhere called <em><a href="/plays/family-circles">Family Circles</a></em>.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2021/time-and-time-again-square-2-w400.jpeg" alt="" width="400" height="400" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Time &amp; TIme Again – detail from West End premiere poster</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>I principally remember <em>Time &amp; Time Again</em> as the play in which I used water for the first time onstage. It wasn’t, of course, the last.</p>

<p>It was first produced in Scarborough when the in-the-round company were still based at the Library Theatre in amongst the outsize books on the first floor. During the night, after a very happy opening performance, our modest pond leaked slowly through the stage into the Reading Room below, fusing the lights and wrecking the latest copies of <em>Gardeners’ Weekly</em> and <em>Bellringers’ World</em>.</p>

<p>It was also memorable because a well-meaning, hygienically minded stage manager, to prevent a typhoid or malaria epidemic breaking out during the action, added a none too mild solution of disinfectant to the murky water causing Leonard and Joan to leap from the pond like demented dolphins one evening.</p>

<p>I always think of the piece as a love story, though admittedly with a protagonist like Leonard, it’s rather an untypical one. And for me, who spends a great deal of time tinkering with the shape and traditional form of comedy, it is really quite a straightforward play.</p>

<p>It was the play, some people observed, when the so-called ‘darker’ side of my writing started to emerge. Personally, I prefer to regard it as the play when I first began to attend to people as well as plot.</p>

<p>It’s also a play that up-ends one of the golden rules of play-writing. Deliberately. The principle unconventional ingredient is the character of Leonard. It’s usual in comedy to construct a central figure who through his actions drives along the story to some sort of conclusion. Leonard does the same but through total inertia. We have at the centre a hero with no purpose or willpower whatsoever. A floating, non-voting cross bencher of a man to whom things just sort of happen.</p>

<p>Despite his appalling behaviour I have a special affection for him. Anyone who can escape the distress of the rat race and yet avoid the discomfort of dropping out deserves a certain amount of admiration.</p>
