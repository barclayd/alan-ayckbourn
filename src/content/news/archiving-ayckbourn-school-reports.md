<p><em>Archiving Ayckbourn is a regular feature in which Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist, Simon Murgatroyd, delves into the archives to highlight items of interest relating to the playwright and theatre in the round.</em></p>

<p><strong>School Reports</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>Whilst rummaging through random boxes in the archive recently, I came across Alan Ayckbourn’s school reports and was, naturally, intrigued to see whether his literary talents had been recognised early.</p>

<p>Unsurprisingly – as I’m sure can be applied to so many of us – Alan’s particular talents were not readily recognised whilst he was at school. Indeed, Alan himself recalled his house master ridiculed his ambition to leave school at 17 and to go into theatre rather than continue his studies.</p>

<div>

</div>

<p>“Well, if you’re happy to make a howling ass of yourself, my friend, then there is nothing I can do. Goodbye!” Alan recalls was the measured response.</p>

<p>Alan attended Haileybury &amp; Imperial Service College near Hertford – now just known as Haileybury – between 1952 and 1956. It was a public school and Alan was only accepted because his mother had married a manager at Barclays Bank, when Alan was 8. He, later, managed to arrange a scholarship for Alan to attend Haileybury.</p>

<p>It was, Alan recalls, “a very tough school” and Paul Allen’s biography <strong><em><a href="https://www.amazon.co.uk/Grinning-Edge-Biography-Ayckbourn-Autobiography/dp/0413771369/ref=sr_1_1?crid=2CC7YDT88NEV1&amp;dib=eyJ2IjoiMSJ9.c9tHthk78vpTopn_cQnKc5fl4I272wZ2Cp2uue_DRCfd_XsKjk7svyJpuYgHUyftE5fML2qpyX62JyujUhQw5QHTNWUoFCFhqqGMAGgwJ0I.qnSLKf-rOQr6Z3EnVG19CXnkrAeKpqI8IcDuSx-YRmc&amp;dib_tag=se&amp;keywords=grinning+at+the+edge&amp;qid=1712152601&amp;sprefix=grinning+at+the+edge%2Caps%2C164&amp;sr=8-1">Grinning At The Edge</a></em></strong> paints a very vivid picture of life at a public school during this period – cold baths, canings, cadet force et al. There was little in the way of practical theatre on offer at Haileybury with the exception of annual productions staged by the French master, <strong><a href="/life/influences/haileybury">Edgar Matthews</a></strong>, who would be a huge influence on Alan (for more details of Alan’s involvement in these productions, refer to the earlier Archiving Ayckbourn article <strong><a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/2024/03/07/archiving-ayckbourn-the-earliest-item/">here</a></strong>).</p>

<p>So with no drama lessons or theatre studies, Alan’s predominant route to creativity would be through his English lessons and this is what we’re having a look at here. How did Alan do in his English lessons whilst at school? And did they portend a hugely successful career in theatre?</p>

<p>Let’s take a look.</p>

<p><strong>Easter 1952:</strong> “His essays are always good, at times he writes really well – much promise.”</p>

<p><strong>Easter 1953:</strong> “Good intelligent work. He has a pleasant style and a wide vocabulary.”</p>

<p><strong>Summer 1953:</strong> “His essay style is bright, but he has a weakness in grammar.”</p>

<p><strong>Winter 1953:</strong> An “inexplicably bad examination.”</p>

<p><strong>Summer 1954:</strong> “Rather Disappointing.”</p>

<p><strong>Winter 1954:</strong> “He has done good work, although not always consistent.”</p>

<p><strong>Easter 1955:</strong> “A methodical worker, lacking inspiration.”</p>

<p><strong>Easter 1956:</strong> “He has not done the work necessary to ensure a pass at ‘A’ level. He does not lack ability.”</p>

<p>That is the final school report we have and it ends with the House Master’s report, who predicts dire things ahead… “It appears to me that he is in some danger, at this rate, of complete failure at ‘A’ Level.”</p>

<p>As can be seen, it really doesn’t offer any indication that Alan will become one of the major English playwrights of the 20th century. Certainly the criticism that he lacks inspiration seems somewhat wide of the mark for a man who has written 90 full length plays and more than 50 other works. That’s quite a lot of inspiration.</p>

<p>At any rate, Alan didn’t fail his ‘A” Levels. He passed both his specialisations, English and History, before surprising everyone by announcing he intended to leave school at 17, without going to university, in order to pursue a career in acting.</p>

<p>Despite opposition and disappointment from his masters and the offer of another scholarship to continue his studies, Alan was resolute. He asked Edgar Matthews for advice and the day after leaving Haileybury, Alan joined the famed theatre impresario <strong><a href="/career/actor/donald-wolfit">Donald Wolfit</a></strong> and his company as it prepared to go to the Edinburgh Festival.</p>

<p>From that point to the present day, Alan has never been out of work. He initially moved consistently from one theatre to the next before alighting on <strong><a href="http://www.a-round-town.com">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> in Scarborough in 1957 where he met <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong>. The rest, as they say, is history.</p>

<p>Contrary to making a “howling ass” of himself, Alan would go on to become of the UK’s most successful and significant playwrights of the latter half of the twentieth century.</p>

<p>And what of Haileybury, did it ever recognise the talent it had nurtured? Indeed it did. In 1988, Alan was delighted to open the Alan Ayckbourn Theatre at Haileybury, converted from the gym where Alan is recognised in his reports to have one genuine success.</p>

<p>Apparently he was a very competent boxer. Perhaps he missed his vocation… or maybe not!</p>
