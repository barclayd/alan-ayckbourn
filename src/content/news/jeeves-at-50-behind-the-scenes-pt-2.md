<p><em>This month is the 50th anniversary of the world premiere of Alan Ayckbourn and Andrew. Lloyd Webber’s musical <strong><a href="/plays/jeeves">Jeeves</a></strong>.</em></p>

<p><em>To mark the anniversary of what is considered one of the West End’s biggest flops, Alan Ayckbourn’s archivist Simon Murgatroyd has searched through the archives to present a behind-the-scenes look at the creation of the musical drawing on contemporary correspondence, interviews and articles held in the Ayckbourn Archive at the Borthwick Institute for Archives at the University of York.</em></p>

<p><em>In the concluding part of the article (for Part 1, click <strong><a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/2025/03/18/jeeves-at-50-behind-the-scenes-pt-1/">here</a></strong>), we reach 1975 and the three months leading to the premiere of the show as well as its ill-fated West End run.</em></p>

<p><strong>Jeeves at 50 – Behind the Scenes, pt.2</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>As 1974 drew to a close, Alan Ayckbourn and Andrew Lloyd Webber’s musical <em>Jeeves</em> was barreling towards its opening. Yet, realistically, it was in no shape to do so.</p>

<p>As the new year opened, there was a book and lyrics – written by Alan Ayckbourn – which were far too long. There was music – composed by Andrew Lloyd Webber – for a, then, staggering 19 songs. The director, Eric Thompson, had never directed a musical before and was making production decisions with an avant-garde designer, Voytek, which were in direct opposition to where Alan realised the script needed to go to become manageable. Meanwhile, the producer Robert Stigwood had not yet crossed the Atlantic to consult with the creatives leaving the decisions to an assistant, Bob Swash.</p>

<p>The show was scheduled to open at Bristol Hippodrome on 20 March 1975 before transferring to Her Majesty’s Theatre on 22 April. On 1 January 1975, no-one had been cast, there was no choreographer and the script was causing great concern to both Alan and Lloyd Webber.</p>

<p>No reason to be worried…</p>

<p>There was at least some good news – if rather peripheral to the musical – in that P.G. Wodehouse, author of the Jeeves and Wooster novels, had been knighted in the Queen’s New Year’s Honours List. Perhaps this was the good portent everyone was looking for.</p>

<p>Meanwhile, the director Eric Thompson was preparing for auditions to take place later in January at London’s Palace Theatre. Given the escalating buzz behind the musical and the involvement of two such major talents in Alan and Lloyd Webber (<em><strong><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></strong></em>&nbsp;having now transferred to the West End and been a hit of unimagined scale for Alan), the demand for roles was high with a great deal of noteworthy talent auditioning for the coveted roles of Jeeves and Wooster.</p>

</div>

<p>Given this, it was perhaps all the more surprising when the Evening Standard printed an exclusive report in mid-January.</p>

<p><em>“When the casting is announced probably next week, it is certain that film star David Hemmings and actor Michael Aldridge will be named for the star parts. Hemmings, making his West End debut, will play Wooster and Michael Aldridge will play Jeeves. The announcement of the casting will come as a disappointment to a number of London’s top stage stars who had eagerly sought the roles. A spokesman for producer Robert Stigwood said: ‘No contracts have been signed for these roles yet and I cannot confirm or deny anything until they have.’”</em></p>

<p>The news was unexpected and would not be confirmed for another month; Hemmings was a bona fide star having made his name in the film&nbsp;<em>Blowup</em>&nbsp;in 1966, but had no experience of the West End stage and apparently his singing abilities were not strong. Lloyd Webber was not convinced by his leading man, who it appears was pushed onto&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;by the producers.</p>

<p><em>“[Hemmings] was an extraordinary individual, quite self-destructive. He would never go to bed, and during the previews you’d find him at three in the morning in the hotel bar, even though he’d have rehearsal early next day.”<br>Andrew Lloyd Webber</em></p>

<p>Even Hemmings seems surprised to have landed the role and had given little thought as to what it would require of him.</p>

<p><em>“How did the role come up? I was feeling a bit restless at the time and I was shown Alan Ayckbourn’s script, which I found hilarious. Then I went to Andrew Lloyd Webber’s home to listen to the music. Many people were being considered for it. I went for an audition at the Palace Theatre and sang ‘Code Of The Woosters’, from the show, just like that. It was a very frightening moment. Then the producers asked me.”<br>David Hemmings</em></p>

<p>By the time the choreographer Christopher Bruce (who would go on to become one of the UK’s most pre-eminent choreographers) was appointed in February, it was a company of firsts: Neither Alan, Lloyd Webber, Thompson, Hemmings, Bruce nor the designer Voytek had any experience of West End musicals.</p>

<p><em>“It was the blind leading the blind. Though we were all experienced in our own fields, neither myself, the director, the leading actor, the choreographer nor Andrew had worked on a live theatre musical before. You can only afford so many firsts in a show.”<br>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p>On 12 February 1975,&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;was officially announced to the public with a press conference featuring Hemmings, Aldridge and the actress Gabrielle Drake – again not known for her musical experience. The noted actress Betty Marsden was also announced to play Aunt Dahlia in the £100,000 musical. Although the papers noted the opening was two months away – which seems a remarkably short amount of time to bring a massive cast musical to the stage – they failed to note, it was actually going to open in just over a month in Bristol.</p>

<p>Judging by the headlines though, the announcement of a major West End show was secondary to Hemmings’ private life. His divorce from Gayle Hunnicutt was raised during the press call for the launch of the musical and Hemmings’ forthright thoughts on the subject led to some sensationalist stories and lurid headlines.</p>

<p><strong>David Hemmings accuses ex-wife Gayle</strong></p>

<p><strong>Gayle and me – by David Hemmings</strong></p>

<p><strong>Aristocrats? It’s better than being married to a bum actor says David Hemmings</strong></p>

<p>Not the most auspicious start. To make matters worse, three days later the musical made headlines again for the wrong reasons when it was announced P.G. Wodehouse had died at his home in New York on 14 February at the age of 93.</p>

<p>Rehearsals for&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;began in the last week of February, just four weeks before the production opened for its two week pre-West End run at the Bristol Hippodrome. The musical had gone through at least two more drafts. The first was 202 pages long and featured 18 songs, which become 158 pages and 19 songs for the original rehearsal draft.</p>

<p>It is this draft which opened the show on 20 March at Bristol, running at 4½ hours and accompanied by a notorious incident with the orchestra.</p>

<p><em>“[We] had this disastrous experience in Bristol: on the first night, the band stopped playing 15 minutes before the end because the producers wouldn’t pay overtime. So these poor, non-singer actors were left completely adrift, without musical accompaniment. I remember walking back to the hotel with my son, who was then very young, and he broke a long silence by saying ‘I’m sure it’ll get better’. But it didn’t.”<br>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p>The regional critics attended on 22 April and were not kind, but offered little indication of the bloodbath that was soon to follow. It was also patently obvious to both Alan and Lloyd Webber that there were major problems: with the latter noting&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;had opened in Bristol “taking water at every level.”&nbsp;</p>

<p>Neither had sufficient influence though to bring the production to a halt, they were at the mercy of the producers and the momentum of a major West End bound musical. In a candid contemporary interview, Lloyd Webber noted: “There is still a lot of work to be done on&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>. It will have to be reduced in length, and there are one or two bits I’m not happy with.”</p>

<p>Alan’s agent – <strong><a href="/life/influences/ramsay-margaret">Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay</a></strong> – had seen the first night too but had largely enjoyed it, even though she questioned whether it was possible “to snip a little bit here and there throughout all the scenes?” She was confident enough though to suggest to Lloyd Webber’s agent they needed to clarify foreign and other rights: “There is no doubt that the moment the London press carry the notices we shall be inundated with inquiries.”&nbsp;</p>

<p>Performances continued in Bristol, but with the running time still over four hours, the first of several drastic decisions was taken. A frequent criticism of the amount of sub-plots led to the role of Aunt Dahlia, played by Betty Marsden, being completely cut; Alan’s description of Marsden’s reaction memorably recorded by Paul Allen in his Ayckbourn biography, <em>Grinning At The Edge</em>.</p>

<p><strong>Betty:</strong>&nbsp;Hello darlings!<br><strong>Eric:</strong>&nbsp;Erm, Betty…<br><strong>Betty:</strong>&nbsp;Don’t tell me, you’ve cut the whole fucking role!<br><strong>Eric:</strong>&nbsp;Yes.<br><strong>Betty:</strong>&nbsp;I don’t believe it. I do not believe it. I do not f—<br><strong>Eric:</strong>&nbsp;Well, you see –<br><strong>Betty:</strong>&nbsp;Well, that’s it. ‘Bye!</p>

<p>The play transferred to London for previews from 11 April, without Marsden and with a reduced running time of 3½ hours, and the producer Robert Stigwood finally appeared to appraise his investment. Less than happy he made his presence felt immediately by removing Thompson as director on 18 April.&nbsp;Four days before the critics were due to come to the opening night.</p>

<p>Alan agreed to take his place as director, which caused some friction between him and Thompson. However, Alan had no real desire to take charge of a sinking ship and accepted only to protect what had been achieved.  When later asked why Thompson had been fired, Alan replied: “They felt they had to sack someone.” He was the scapegoat for a problem that, ultimately, ran all the way to Stigwood at the top of the production heap.</p>

<p><em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;opened on 22 April as planned at Her Majesty’s Theatre and by this point had, thanks to the work of Alan – and Thompson prior to being fired – been reduced to a running time of 2¾ hours. A substantial pruning from the day it opened but still not short by anyone’s standards.</p>

<p>The running time was the least of the problems though as the critics arrived and left little unbloodied. Barely anyone came out well with Hemmings foremost amongst those castigated for being out of their depth, which he took personally.</p>

<p>“<em>We went to see David Hemmings, who was playing Bertie Wooster, who we heard was still in his dressing room. He was crying and sitting in the shower so I stepped in to get to him and told him it was all our fault. I was soaking wet, it was like a farce.’”<br>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p>Hemmings was not the only one to feel the weight of the critics’ opinion though as both Lloyd Webber and Alan received their fair share of criticism too. Alan later succinctly summed up the experience: “It was one of the least successful West End musicals and probably distinguished Andrew’s career as the biggest failure he’s had. It really did get a critical drubbing the first time.”</p>

<p>Worryingly, it wasn’t just the critics who were dissatisfied as Lloyd Webber reported: “there was booing on the opening night.” Just three days after it opened, Bob Swash wrote asking the creative talent to waive their percentages to give the show a chance of a run; the producers having already waived the management fee and negotiated a rent reduction with the theatre.</p>

<p>But there was little hope for&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>. The reviews had had an immediate and devastating effect on the box office. During previews, the show had been generating an average of £1,726 per performance; that figure dropped to an average of £960 for the five performances after the reviews were published.</p>

<p>The waivers were agreed and the next day Alan wrote to Stigwood: “Sorry we weren’t able to get off in a bigger blaze of glory. I think we were within a hair’s breadth of slightly kinder reviews but, alas, once critics decide they don’t like something, they really do go too far.”</p>

<p><em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;limped on to decreasing box office as furious cuts and amendments continued during the next two weeks. But nothing could halt the slide as, week by week, the average take per performance dropped from £738 to £617 to £522. On 12 May, Stigwood threw the towel in and it was announced&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;would close on 24 May just 38 performances after it opened.</p>

<p>That same week that Stigwood announced its closure, the musical hit its nadir with a matinee which took just £164 and the lowest evening run of just £506. Over the course of its run at Bristol and in London, the box office generated £47,577 against an estimated production budget of £100,000, before advertising, wages, rents and other costs.</p>

<p>Despite all the time, effort and money that had gone into the production, its only lasting legacy was to be forever remembered as one of the greatest flops to have been staged in the West End.</p>

<p>Ironically, the effect of the closure was far less traumatic for Alan than might have been expected. Granted, there was a certain inevitability to the early closure and the reception of the play, but even so, Alan was sanguine about the whole experience:</p>

<p><em>“Jeeves left me very unaffected. Well, it took a lot of energy out of me – but the whole episode was so ridiculous and remote in a way, that any sadness I felt was for Andrew (whose career it was at stake) and not for me.”<br>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p>The experience was also tempered somewhat by the fact that as far as Alan was concerned, it was old news. He had finished writing his new play – commissioned by the National Theatre and soon to be tremendous hit –&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></strong></em>&nbsp;on the very day&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;closed in London.</p>

<p>With the production over and Alan and Lloyd Webber adamant that&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;had no future in its current form, there was nothing left but to chalk it up to experience and reminisce.</p>

<p><em>“I don’t think I could ever collaborate with another writer, but I did find collaborating with a musician very stimulating. The book, however, was eventually running for four hours. I could have cut it in about a day in Scarborough circumstances. But with a vehicle like that, by the time one got round to cutting, it was like treading on eggs. Actors all had their favourite bits, and so on. The producer of Jeeves I met twice as he spent most of his time in Los Angeles. We were all left to our own devices, which would have been alright if any of us had done this sort of show before. We badly needed Hal Prince, or someone like him. I did, however, develop a close working relationship with Lloyd Webber, which I hope might lead to something else, something conducted on our own terms.”<br>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p><em>“Frankly, Jeeves should never had made it to London. No one on the team had a clue what they were doing, Stigwood never saw the show out of town and I really only had practical experience of musical theatre via Joseph and Jesus Christ Superstar, both of which had been heard on record before.</em></p>

<p><em>“We came into London to a deserved critical bloodbath. Alan and I couldn’t even watch the second act on opening night, we just buried our heads in far too much wine at the old Petit Club Francois.</em></p>

<p><em>“[We] were shattered but we knew we had it coming. We felt in our bones that we had done some good things and we still felt a musical on the Jeeves stories could work. We also knew we’d created an outsized turkey. But two things happened during the fiasco. First, I think I learned as much the hard way about musicals in eight weeks as I’ve done in the past two decades. Secondly, Alan and I became close friends.’”<br>Andrew Lloyd Webber</em></p>

<p>Aside from a single heavily abridged student production at Dulwich College in 1981 to mark P.G. Wodehouse’s centennial,&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em> was never again seen in the form it took in the West End. It was never forgotten though and over the next two decades both Alan and Lloyd Webber would talk about revising and reviving it.</p>

<p>Convinced there was a good musical in there, they would eventually get the chance to prove it on the 21st anniversary of&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;when&nbsp;<em>By Jeeves</em>&nbsp;was chosen to open the Stephen Joseph Theatre, Scarborough, in 1996.</p>

<p>The result? Everything&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;wasn’t. Not least an award-winning hit. Almost unrecognisable from the original musical, Alan and Lloyd Webber’s clever and small scale reinvention of the musical proved&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;always had potential, it just needed to be approached in the right way.</p>

<p>It’s just a shame that, fifty-years ago, the original <em>Jeeves</em> approached it all in the completely wrong way. Although, it has admittedly provided a lot of entertaining stories ever since and it’s unlikely it will ever be forgotten for its notorious place in West End history.</p>
