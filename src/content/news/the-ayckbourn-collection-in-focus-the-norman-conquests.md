<p><em>In August 2023, The Ayckbourn Collection was donated to Scarborough Museums and Galleries by Sir Alan and Lady Ayckbourn and the playwright’s Archivist, Simon Murgatroyd.</em></p>

<p><em>It is a collection of approximately 60 significant items drawn from Sir Alan’s Archive and Simon’s personal collection exploring the contribution of Alan Ayckbourn, Stephen Joseph and theatre in the round to Scarborough’s cultural heritage and history.</em></p>

<p><em>Currently, items from the exhibition are on display at Scarborough Art Gallery. To mark the exhibition, Simon Murgatroyd – who curated the Collection and facilitated its donation – is taking a weekly look at some of the individual items within the display, their significance and why they were chosen for the Collection.</em></p>

<p><strong><em>The Norman Conquests</em> Manuscripts</strong></p>

<p>2023 marks the 50th anniversary of one of Alan Ayckbourn’s most famous creations, <em><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></em> trilogy. It premiered in Scarborough at Theatre in the Round at Library Theatre in 1973 before opening in the West End in 1974, on Broadway on 1975 and then on television in 1977.</p>

<p>It is regarded as one of the most popular and significant of Alan’s works and was named as one of the 100 most significant works of the20th Century by the National Theatre. </p>

<p>So it’s only fitting that its place in Scarborough and Alan Ayckbourn’s history is marked by The Ayckbourn Collection at Scarborough Museums and Galleries, which holds a complete set of three manuscripts used during rehearsals for its world premiere in 1973 in the town.</p>

<p>It wasn’t quite how we know it today though. First of all, it wasn’t called <em>The Norman Conquests</em>. It was just dubbed ‘The Trilogy’ and wasn’t even advertised as such. The plays were promoted as being inter-connected and you could see one, two or all three plays.</p>

<p>But given ‘The Trilogy’ premiered during the summer season at Scarborough and Alan wasn’t convinced he could persuade holiday-makers to give up three nights of their vacation, he didn’t emphasise the need to see all three works to have an enjoyable experience.</p>

<p>Two of the plays also had completely different titles. Original the plays were <em>Fancy Meeting You</em> (later retitled <em>Table Manners</em>), <em>Make Yourself at Home</em> (later retitled <em>Living Together</em>) and <em>Round and Round The Garden</em>.</p>

<p>The Ayckbourn Collection holds two manuscripts used by the actor Janet Dale, who played Ruth (<em>Fancy Meeting You</em> and <em>Make Yourself at Home</em>) and one used by Ronald Herman, who played Tom (<em>Round and Round the Garden</em>).</p>

<p>Currently on display at Scarborough Art Gallery is the <em>Round and Round the Garden</em> manuscript complete with Ronald Herdman’s notes. The collection’s copy of <em>Make Yourself at Home</em> also had a mystery which was unsolved until earlier this year.</p>

<p>The cover of the manuscript has the name ‘Lucy’ drawn in block capitals and the significance of this was not understood; there is no character called Lucy nor did anyone with that name apparently work on the production. Then, sadly, earlier this year Janet Dale died and it transpired that she was known by the name Lucy to her family and close friends and that was why the manuscript had been labelled as such.</p>

<p>Complete sets of original manuscripts are quite rare with other sets held in the Ayckbourn Archive at the Borthwick Institute for Archives at the University of York and the British Library with two complete sets also known to be held in private collections. The only original set of manuscripts held in Scarborough, where the trilogy premiered, is in The Ayckbourn Collection at Scarborough Museums and Galleries.</p>

<p><em>The Norman Conquests</em> is pivotal in Alan’s progress as a writer, not only for its success, but because it is regarded as the first of his event works. <em>The Norman Conquests</em> cleverly tells the story of one weekend from three different perspectives – the dining room, the lounge and the garden. The plays interweave and the more of them the audience sees, the more fulfiling the theatrical experience as the whole weekend and the character’s relationships and intentions come into clearer view.</p>

<p>As a note, although the manuscripts mentioned here are very rare, the holy grail of <em>The Norman Conquests</em> manuscripts is the unique, complete set of Alan Ayckbourn’s hand-written drafts for the trilogy. These were presented to the late Michael Gambon following the success of the West End production of the plays in 1974. What happened to them after that or whether they still survive is unknown.</p>

<p>Fifty years on, you can now see a small slice of British theatre history with<em> The Norman Conquests</em> manuscript on display at Scarborough Art Gallery. As Norman might say to an Ayckbourn fan… “We just wanted to make you happy.”</p>
