<p>One of the joys of being Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist is not just focusing on the playwright himself, but also the people who inspired him.</p>

<p>Not least  Stephen Joseph. The theatre pioneer who championed theatre in the round and adaptable theatres and had a profound influence on theatre design in the 1960s and 1970s which can still be seen to this day.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Stephen Joseph (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Stephen is a fascinating character, a true theatre polymath in what he knew and taught and – obviously – the single most significant influence in Alan Ayckbourn’s professional career.</p>

<p>Stephen was, as described by Alan, an anarchist who wanted to break down the established theatre as he believed it had long since fallen into a rut. He butted up against the theatre establishment and was very provocative – and often quite ingenious – in how he presented his arguments and views.</p>

<p>This leads to one of the facts about Stephen which has given me a great deal of joy over the years, particularly as a former journalist. Stephen needed publicity for his work, but generally had little money for that. So he was quite inventive, he wrote articles for magazines, held courses and frequently attended symposiums and debates.</p>

<p>But the one thing that I love most? His arguments in newspaper letter pages. Stephen liked to generate a controversy and frequently did so creating a back and forth between… well, between himself. Using several pseudonyms, Stephen would take both sides of an argument to generate a controversy or debate in various publications.</p>

<p>Occasionally, he would also rope in colleagues to support or make their own points. But once you know what you’re looking for, you can spot these confected arguments and the fun Stephen was having.</p>

<p>Stephen’s most frequent nom de plume was Heath Block (also a name he used for some of his acting roles) and I’ve recently discovered what must be my favourite letter from Heath Block, which I’d like to share in today’s blog.</p>

<p>On 18 January 1962, an E G Bottle from Kempsey wrote to The Stage about a recent letter by Stephen in which he had debated both children’s theatre and, what Stephen viewed as, the appalling proposals for the Prince Charles Theatre in Leicester Place, London.</p>

<p>The most important part of Bottle’s letter reads: “Mr Joseph’s letter on the other hand seems to decry the proscenium arch type of theatre as old fashioned and something which the theatre in general would do well without.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/headline-w350.png" alt="" width="350" height="153" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Headline from The Stage newspaper</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>“One must recall that our theatre in its present form has taken over 300 years to evolve from the Elizabethan Playhouse and theatre in the round that Mr Joseph seems to advocate. Surely to suggest that because the proscenium stage cannot put over certain forms of drama as well as in the round, it must be labelled old fashioned and not something worth keeping, is uttering disastrous nonsense. One might as well stipulate that because the Round cannot stage the excitement and illusion of pantomime and other types of entertainment, it must be regarded as useless. </p>

<p>“The most sensible solution is to have both forms working in their own sphere side by side and not against one another.”</p>

<p>Stephen’s response was typically over-the-top – arguably verging on the sarcastic – but, nonetheless, extremely entertaining with it leading The Stage’s letters page with the headline “Vote For Bottle!” Of the page’s three other letters, two are by Stephen’s associates – Rodney Wood and Ian Watson – also joining the argument.</p>

<p>Here’s Stephen’s response to E G Bottle in full: “Sir, – Vote for Bottle! Of course we should have all forms of theatre side by side, and I cannot for the life of me see why Stephen Joseph should object to this situation. The fact that there is no theatre-in-the-round in this country simply proves that it is no good. This is a free country, and the monopoly of the open stage must be resisted.</p>

<p>“I deeply resent Mr Joseph’s tone in attacking the Prince Charles Theatre. As if two foot of wing space really mattered! Why, it is possible to act without any wing space at all. No, the truth is that Mr Joseph is making a dastardly attack upon the Royal Family. Remember that it is his company that recently shocked the Midlands by playing no National Anthem in the theatre, and by presenting disgusting plays by John Osborne and Strindberg. The theatre must resist these Communistic moves – and thank goodness we can rally behind the Bottle Party!</p>

<p>“Yours very sincerely, R. Heath Block.”</p>

<p>Sincere indeed. But a splendid response I hope you’ll agree!</p>

<p>Stephen’s reference to the lack of National Anthem relates to the fact that Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in Scarborough and on its subsequent tours was the first regional company to stop the playing of the National Anthem at every performance.</p>

<p>It produced a mild breeze in a tea cup from a minority of people which fizzled out when a poll of local people found very little interest whatsoever in the issue and the Lord Chamberlain’s Office, when quizzed about the illegality of Stephen’s actions, made clear there was no legal responsibility for any theatre to play the anthem.</p>

<p>Meanwhile the Prince Charles Theatre is a bit of an obscurity – although it still stands today as the Prince Charles Cinema, a much loved independent cinema just off Leicester Square.</p>

<p>However, it was originally designed as an intimate 420 seat proscenium theatre situated beneath an office block and which opened on Boxing Day 1962. It was renowned for its small size and challenges – hence the talk of the essentially non-existent theatre wings.</p>

<p>Stephen Joseph was proved right on this occasion as the theatre was a disaster. Barely two years after it opened, it was redesigned and re-named as Fielding’s Music Hall and after a brief return to its original name, it was sold in 1965 to a cinema chain which rebuilt the space and opened it as the Prince Charles Cinema in January 1969.</p>

<p>It still runs today and is apparently the last independent cinema running in London’s West End. Obviously, it far better suited to film than theatre. A point which even Heath Block would probably have agreed with!</p>
