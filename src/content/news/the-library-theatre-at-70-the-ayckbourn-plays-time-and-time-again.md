<p>Here on the Ayckbourn Blog, we’re celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in July 1955.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn was a vital and significant presence at <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> and these articles celebrate the plays he wrote for the company between 1959 and 1976. They offer an in-depth look at the creation of and background to some of the playwright’s most famous – and most obscure – works.</p>

<p>In the previous article, we looked at his tenth play <em><strong><a href="/plays/family-circles">Family Circles</a></strong></em> (1970) and now reach a milestone in his writing with his first tragicomedy, <strong><em><a href="/plays/time-and-time-again">Time And Time Again</a></em></strong> (1971). The majority of photos accompanying this article have never been published before and have only recently been rediscovered in archive.</p>

<p><strong>Time And Time Again </strong>(1971) by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p><em>Time And Time Again</em>&nbsp;is a pivotal moment in Alan Ayckbourn’s playwriting career. When first performed, critics saw it as a move away from light-comedy into something darker and more substantial.</p>

<p>While the West End successes of&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></em></strong>&nbsp;in 1967 and&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves">How The Other Half Loves</a></em></strong>&nbsp;in 1970 gave Alan widespread recognition, it was&nbsp;<em>Time And Time Again</em>&nbsp;and the plays which immediately followed it which confirmed that Alan was much more than just the farceur some critics had already (and incorrectly) labelled him.</p>

<p>The play was – in part – a reaction to his previous work, <em>The Story So Far…</em> (later retitled <em>Family Circles</em>), which had not been altogether successful in the playwright’s eyes. Technically and structurally complex, it marked the last of his plays sublimated to structure rather than plot or character development.</p>

<p>In the wake of this, he saw a production of Chekhov’s <em>Uncle Vanya</em>&nbsp;which made him aspire to write a play whose drama came completely from the characters without the need for any plot contrivances. He also wanted to write a play that broke a golden rule of the well-made play by having a hero who was totally unmotivated, had no goals and achieved nothing. </p>

<p>For this, he created his first truly memorable character in Leonard, a man who causes destruction around him by sole virtue of his apathy and ennui. He sets the template for the many inadvertently destructive men that follow such as Norman in&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></em></strong>&nbsp;and Colin in&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a></em></strong>.</p>

<p>However, that Alan would write a new play for the summer 1971 season at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was not certain. Since re-opening as a professional theatre in 1967, the company had only opened for summer seasons with an annually appointed Director of Productions. Alan had held this position in 1969 and 1970 and, judging by Scarborough Theatre Trust minutes, it was assumed he would continue in this role in 1971.</p>

<p>On 13 December 1970, the board of Scarborough Theatre Trust was informed that Alan would not be able to take on the role for 1971. He had committed himself to an extended trip to North America as a result of the first Broadway transfer of his work with <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> starring Phil Silvers. In his place, he suggested <strong><a href="http://articles.theatre-in-the-round.com/offsite-75/page-7/styled-14/">Caroline Smith</a></strong> for the role – who would become the company’s first and only female Artistic Director.</p>

<p>Scarborough Theatre Trust was not even completely confident of a new Ayckbourn play for 1971 as Caroline reported on 21 March that although Alan had been commissioned to write a play, she knew no details of it but believed the “playwright would no doubt write the play in time.”</p>

<p>A title was delivered to Caroline advertising purposes by May, although the Ayckbourn Archive indicates <em>Time And Time Again</em> was not his initial choice of title. Others considered include <em>The Game’s The Thing</em>,&nbsp;<em>Plays And Players</em>,&nbsp;<em>The Garden Pact</em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em>The Sporting Gnome</em>.</p>

<p>As usual, there were not details of the play in advertising as Alan was not even close to putting pencil to paper. It was advertised only as “The latest new comedy, from the author of <em>Relatively Speaking</em> and last year’s highly successful <em>The Story So Far…</em>.”</p>

<p>Alan wrote the play over six days during mid-June 1971, finishing the play the day before rehearsals were due to begin. </p>

<p>To Alan’s mind, this play was the one where he began to dispense with jokes or funny-lines, which he said he was “never very good at”. Instead, he began to concentrate on characters with a central character whose passivity would affect all those around him.</p>

<p>“The interesting thing about&nbsp;<em>Time &amp; Time Again</em>&nbsp;is that I have upset the balance. The central character should be the driving force. I wanted to write a total vacuum, a central character who took no decisions, did nothing, everything was done for him and by simply taking no decisions he affects the whole course of the play. Doing nothing, he upsets about five lives. He comes through it in the most extraordinary way; everybody else ends up miserable. Like certain characters in life, he attracts people who have an irresistible impulse to push him in one direction, but he slides out of the push. Some people get angered by this type, others get concerned.”</p>

<p>Rehearsals began during the final week of June with the play scheduled to open on 8 July – The Stage newspaper reporting that he was rehearsing the play until half-an-hour before the first performance! It was Alan’s only contribution to the 1971 summer season with Caroline directing the other four productions.</p>

<p>The cast consisted of Roger Kemp, Eve Shickle, Gil Osborne and Tim Meats and was most notable for the inclusion of Christopher Godwin, making his first appearance in an Ayckbourn play in Scarborough.</p>

<p>Alan had met Christopher in 1964 prior to Alan’s first West End play&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/mr-whatnot">Mr Whatnot</a></em></strong>&nbsp;and they had struck up a friendship. Christopher played the pivotal role of Leonard and would become a significant part of the Scarborough company until 1977. Not only was he the first actor to perform the role of Leonard, he was also the first person to play Norman in&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>, Colin in&nbsp;<em>Absent Friends</em>&nbsp;and Dennis in&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/just-between-ourselves">Just Between Ourselves</a></em></strong>&nbsp;amongst others. As such he created what we now typically consider the ‘Ayckbourn man.’</p>

<p>Notably, <em>Time And Time Again</em> was also the first of many Ayckbourn plays to integrate water into the set with an ornamental pond, which let to two memorable incidents.</p>

<p>Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was based on the first floor of Scarborough Library and, shortly after the first performance, the pond sprung a leak overnight. Water leaked through the parquet flooring into the reading room below – fusing the lights and ruining a number of periodical magazines.</p>

<p>Potentially more disruptive was a second incident, recalled by Alan, in which “a hygienically minded stage manager, to prevent a typhoid or malaria epidemic breaking out during the action, added a none too mild solution of disinfectant to the murky water causing Leonard and Joan to leap from the pond like demented dolphins.”</p>

<p>Other than this, the production was a great success for the company and was the most successful play of the season. </p>

<p>The critics were also practically unanimous in their praise for the play and, more importantly, noted that with its darker edge and concentration on character, Alan was not just a writer of light comedy as he had been previously categorised (with the exception of&nbsp;The Stage newspaper, whose review was bizarrely just a synopsis of the play). It was the first step into the tragi-comedy genre that defines so much of the playwright’s output and for which he has become renowned. Interestingly, Alan feels audiences have never found&nbsp;<em>Time And Time Again&nbsp;</em>quite so dark and he once said that “compared with the plays that followed, it’s positively frivolous.”</p>

<p>The success of the production led to immediate interest in a West End production and, arguably, the start of a true phenomenon. With a new producer in the shape of Michael Codron, <em>Time And Time Again</em> would open at the Comedy Theatre in 1972 and cement an astonishing run where for every year between 1970 and 2000, there would be between one and five Ayckbourn productions taking place in the West End. An unrivalled achievement.</p>

<p>And Alan, inspired by the success of what came to be regarded as this first tragi-comedy, began thinking about his next work – a play that would change everything for him.</p>
