<p>There’s an odd piece of theatre in the round history which took places fifty years ago in Scarborough.</p>

<p>It saw two theatre projects in the town facing off against each other to the extent that it eventually made national news with the unexpected sight of Alan Ayckbourn publicly threatening to quit Scarborough and move his company elsewhere.</p>

<p>This is the story of a 1974 theatrical conflict.</p>

<p>Two years after Alan Ayckbourn became <strong><a href="/career/artistic-director">Artistic Director</a></strong> at <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong>, the company’s fortunes seemed to be on the ascendent. The 1974 summer season had frequently seen the ‘House Full’ board posted outside Scarborough Library on Vernon Road and, having only operated during the summer months since 1961, both a winter and touring season were set to be launched.</p>

</div>

<p>However, an unlikely series of events unexpectedly threatened to derail Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre potentially putting its entire future into doubt.</p>

<p>Between its opening in 1955 and 1973, Scarborough Town Council licensed the company annually to use the facilities on the first floor of the Scarborough Library. During the summer months, the company would take over the Concert Room and the small and large lecture rooms; essentially the majority of the first floor facilities.</p>

<p>In 1974, responsibility for Scarborough Library moved to a county council level. In theory, this should not have caused many issues. Astonishingly though, this administrative move almost led to a county council sub-committee being responsible for the loss of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong>’s and Alan Ayckbourn’s legacy in Scarborough.</p>

<p>What makes the story even more interesting is the unlikely confluence with a high-profile campaign to restore Scarborough’s Opera House theatre. The Opera House had been closed in 1971 due to structural issues. Then owned by York Citizens’ Theatre Trust, the venue had been put up for sale and, in 1972, it appeared likely Scarborough Town Council would buy the site for demolition and development.</p>

<p>Unfortunately, late in the negotiations, the theatre was listed as a building of “historical and special architectural interest.” As a result, the council pulled out of negotiations to buy the site after failing to get the building de-listed.</p>

<p>By late 1972, plans were being submitted to save the theatre with the formation of the Opera House Preservation Society [from hereon the Society] which offered to buy the theatre – an effort which failed as it did not have the finances in place. Soon afterwards, local businessman Don Robinson bought the building for £22,500 and offered the Society the chance to lease it for four years before buying it for £30,000.</p>

<p>Concurrent to this, Scarborough Theatre Trust [from hereon the Trust] – which ran Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre – was looking to raise money to move into a new purpose-built home. The Trust was hoping to raise £80,000 for the move. Meanwhile, the Society was looking to raise £30,000 by January 1975 to obtain the Opera House’s lease.</p>

<p>By August 1973, the Society was complaining that both efforts were essentially pursuing the same sources of financial aid and grants and they should unite behind the Opera House. Key to this was the chair of the Society, County Councillor Erkki Lahteela – a key figure in the conflict to come.</p>

<p>By June 1974, the tension was simmering. The local newspaper – the Scarborough Evening News – had a full-page report on the two projects and how they were likely to seek the same funding sources and were both appealing to the Scarborough public for financial support.</p>

<p>Both sides denied any rivalry when quoted within the report.</p>

<p>However, in November 1974, the Trust applied for a 40 week license to continue performing at the library during 1975. Previously this annual application had largely been a formality and little in the way of opposition was expected.</p>

<p>On 23 November, the Scarborough Evening News reported North Yorkshire County Libraries Committee had turned down the application more than two to one. The reason? A certain County Councillor, Erkki Lahteela, “spearheaded opposition to the Library Theatre proposal” suggesting the theatre’s presence would “take facilities away from organisations” in the town.</p>

<p>This was the same Councillor Lahteela who was chair of the Society and had previously stated he saw no rivalry between the projects and that the Opera House would find it “far easier” to get grants and financial aid than the Trust for the theatre in the round project.</p>

<p>Obviously not that much easier.</p>

<p>The county council’s decision was unexpected and put both the Trust and Scarborough Town Council into a difficult position. The Town Council viewed theatre in the round as an asset to Scarborough, welcoming both the publicity and the money it generated for the town. Conversely, the Opera House was closed and not only needed to raise £30,000 to buy the lease but an estimated £120,000 to repair and make usable as a theatre again.</p>

<p>However, there was another issue in that the Town Council was well aware the Trust needed a new home for theatre in the round  but had made little progress in helping to secure a much needed base for the company away from the library. Which was somewhat problematic considering the company was now threatened with homelessness.</p>

<p>On 25 November, Alan Ayckbourn was interviewed by the Scarborough Evening News with a prominent story proclaiming “Ayckbourn says he will quit if Library Theatre is refused a longer season.”</p>

<p>There the playwright regretfully noted how, if the County Council’s decision was not overturned, it would leave Scarborough without a repertory company and also lead to his “own departure from Scarborough.”</p>

<p>Carefully rebutting all of the committee’s raised objections to the license, he also addressed the Opera House issue for the first time publicly, questioning the veracity of the Society’s plans and suggesting the money would be better spent knocking the building down and constructing a more suitable Scarborough theatre – with an in-the-round space – in its place.</p>

<p>It was a provocative statement, never likely to be accepted.</p>

<p>Just to add fuel to the fire, Hull Arts Centre made an offer to house Alan and the ’round’ company – which had unilaterally voted to join Alan in his threat to leave Scarborough; interestingly the Arts Centre would later become the Spring Street Theatre, home of Hull Truck Theatre.</p>

<p>The argument received extensive television and newspaper coverage with both parties going on the record in extensive interviews, which saw Alan at one point note: “I feel hurt, naturally, that our application for a 40-week season was rejected on such flimsy pretexts, and after 17 years I would have thought the town would be more supportive for theatre-in-the-round in Scarborough.”</p>

<p>The following day, the Society offered a 50-50 administrative split with the Trust if it immediately invested £15,000 to help obtain the Opera House’s lease. The society notably shooting itself immediately in the foot – or displaying an unbelievable amount of ignorance – by perversely suggesting there was no necessity for theatre-in-the-round as part of the Opera House development as “Alan Ayckbourn’s plays are performed in the West End in proscenium arch-type theatre not as theatre-in-the-round.”</p>

<p>In retrospect, it was probably a desperate play for money by the Society, which had not been altogether honest about its financial situation.</p>

<p>As the arguments rolled into December, Scarborough Councillors publicly pushed for a special meeting of the Libraries Committee, which the County Council agreed to.</p>

<p>With his dual positions as Chair of the Society and on the Libraries Committee being portrayed in a less then positive light, Councillor Lahteela resigned as chairman of the Society and went on to make a public statement which was reported nationally due to a rather extraordinary declaration.</p>

<p>He said that neither he nor any other county councillors had realised “how famous Alan Ayckbourn was.” It is not recorded what his fellow councillors thought of this sweeping statement about them, but it was perhaps not the wisest of comments.</p>

<p>In context, in 1974 alone Alan had equalled the record for the most plays simultaneously playing in the West End, had had his first Broadway success, had been featured in a self-titled documentary on BBC2, been a guest on BBC Radio’s phenomenally popular <em>Desert Island Discs</em> and was a constant presence in the region’s newspapers as well as national publications. During that year alone he had major interviews in Cosmopolitan magazine, the Sunday Times, the Evening Standard, the Daily Mail and the New York Times.</p>

<p>It’s inconceivable Yorkshire’s county councillors were not aware how famous Alan was by that point. And it seems highly unlikely they would have approved of Councillor Lahteela’s statement apparently on their behalf.</p>

<p>On 3 January 1975, the day before Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre’s winter season closed, a special meeting of the Libraries Committee was held and its decision reversed. The company was given permission to run a season from May until January 1976.</p>

<p>Theatre in the round was safe for another year whilst the hunt for a new home went on.</p>

<p>Unfortunately, all this publicity had not been positive for the Opera House campaign and there was much attention focused on the Society as the deadline to obtain the lease arrived. Come January 1975, the Society admitted it could not purchase the £30,000 lease as over the past two years it has raised less than… £500.</p>

<p>The Preservation Society essentially vanished but was made redundant anyway as Don Robinson decided to take matters into his own hand and restored the Opera House over the next 18 months, re-opening it as a theatre in 1976.</p>

<p>It had been a very public and, at times, unedifying spat but all was seemingly resolved.  It was not all good news hower as, in a additional statement on 3 January, the Libraries Committee declared this would be the theatre in the round company’s final season at the library and it would not be extended.</p>

<p>The clock was running. Alan Ayckbourn and theatre in the round needed a new home in Scarborough and had just a year to find it….</p>
