<p><em>On 10 August 1988, one of Alan Ayckbourn’s most significant plays of the decade premiered at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round, Scarborough.</em></p>

<p><em>During the ’80s, the playwright had begun to explore morality in the modern world – even the nature of good and evil. It arguably reached its zenith in <a href="/plays/man-of-the-moment">Man Of The Moment</a> in which Alan fixed his eye on the media and it ability to distort and manipulate perception and facts.</em></p>

<p><em>To mark the anniversary, this is an introduction to the play originally written for Alan Ayckbourn’s revival of the play in 2009 at the Royal &amp; Derngate Theatre, Northampton, to mark the playwright’s 70th birthday.</em></p>

<p><strong>Man of the Moment</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>In 1986, Alan Ayckbourn left his adopted home town of Scarborough to take a sabbatical at the <a href="/career/national-theatre">National Theatre</a>. The next two years was a period of extraordinary success and acclaim as both playwright and director.</p>

<p>Despite unsubstantiated speculation he would stay in London, Alan never had any intention but to return to Scarborough in 1988 and, reinvigorated by his break, he wrote one of his most ambitious plays&nbsp;<em>Man Of The Moment</em>; a darkly comic piece confronting morality in the modern world and the amorality of the media</p>

<p>The play was partly inspired by Alan travelling to work whilst in London and passing the flower stall of Buster Edwards, one of the Great Train Robbers, whose life was soon to be made into the film&nbsp;<em>Buster</em>. Alan thought it ironic the villains had become celebrities whilst the unfortunate train driver, who died of his injuries, had been all but forgotten.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/1988a-man-of-the-moment-w400.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="538" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Poster for the world premiere of Man Of The Moment (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>It was also conceived with – surprisingly – Shakespeare’s <em><a href="/plays/man-of-the-moment/articles/othello">Othello</a></em> in mind as, originally, the plan had been to run <em>Man Of The Momen</em>t in repertory with <em>Othello</em> in the West End. It never happened but it does explain why <em>Man Of The Moment</em> has such a large cast which is broadly similar in size to <em>Othello</em>.</p>

<p>It is the first and so far only Ayckbourn play to be set in a foreign country (excepting the fantastical locations of the family plays) and Alan felt it broke new territory for his writing.</p>

<p><em>“It does deal with an issue, violence and our response to it. I’m usually know for writing about a lady having an unhappy life in Reading, or someone having a nervous breakdown in a garage. I’ve come out of the domestic arena. I’ve begun to formulate a few opinions. Until recently I had no opinions whatsoever.”</em></p>

<p>The play opened at the <a href="http://careers.alanayckbourn.net/styled-6/styled-9/">Stephen Joseph Theatre In The Round</a>, Scarborough on 10 August 1988 and was well-received by the critics, who believed Alan’s plays were getting darker. Something Alan disagreed with – as, presumably, did anyone who had been consistently watching his plays in Scarborough since the 1970s.</p>

<p><em> “The comedy is decidedly black, but, you know, I’ve always written in that vein. People simply didn’t recognise it. Perhaps they didn’t want to.” </em></p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/man-of-the-moment-1990-01-w400.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="488" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Poster for the West End premiere of Man Of The Moment (© To be confirmed)</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>As plays such as&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/just-between-ourselves">Just Between Ourselves</a></em>&nbsp;clearly demonstrate, the darkness was there long before&nbsp;<em>Man Of The Moment</em>&nbsp;came along and can inarguably be traced back to at least <em><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></em>, if not earlier..</p>

<p>The play’s most famous physical feature was its swimming pool, which posed little problem in Scarborough – which had already been completely submerged during 1982 for <em><a href="/plays/way-upstream">Way Upstream</a></em> – but promised a challenge for its transfer to London in 1990. The set at the Globe Theatre had a pool filled with 5,000 gallons of water and there is a sense the press was eagerly waiting for a repeat of the incident which had seen Alan’s play&nbsp;<em>Way Upstream&nbsp;</em>flood the National Theatre in 1982. Alan played along, but never doubted this design was made of far sterner stuff.</p>

<p><em>“We could be tempting fate. This one has 36 tons of water in it. It’s one thing to flood the stalls at the National but if this tank goes in Shaftesbury Avenue we could close the Northern Line.”</em></p>

<p>This production of&nbsp;<em>Man Of The Moment&nbsp;</em>starred Peter Bowles and <a href="/career/advocates/alan-ayckbourn-advocates/michael-gambon">Michael Gambon</a>, not as might be expected in the larger than life character of Vic Parks but as the unassuming and content Douglas Beechey. Unanimously acclaimed in the role, it intriguingly saw the actor’s physical transformation compared to a penguin and, most memorably, as: “his whole body mass seems to redistribute itself like a giant sea anemone”. He won several awards for the performance, confirming his place as the pre-eminent Ayckbourn actor; Gambon is the most prolific Ayckbourn actor in West End productions of the playwright’s work.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Michael Gambon as Douglas Beechey (© John Haynes)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>The play’s reception was notable in that, coincidentally like&nbsp;<em>Just Between Ourselves</em>, many critics were keen to push the comparison between Ayckbourn’s writing and Chekhov’s plays; something which must have pleased the playwright who has always cited Chekhov as an inspiration.</p>

<p>The play was nominated for an Olivier and won the Evening Standard Award for Best Comedy. Gambon would win his second Olivier in three years in an Ayckbourn-directed production, having also been recognised in Ayckbourn’s acclaimed production of Arthur Miller’s&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/miller-arthur">A View From The Bridge</a></em>.</p>

<p>Given the nature of the play, it is not surprising that there have been many proposed plans to adapt&nbsp;<em>Man Of The Moment&nbsp;</em>for television or film. Although none of these has ever come to fruition, it has been adapted twice for radio. The first adaptation for the BBC was broadcast in 1992 and a second adaptation was broadcast on the playwright’s 70th&nbsp;birthday weekend featuring Tim Piggott-Smith as Vic and the frequent Ayckbourn collaborator, Janie Dee, as Trudy.</p>

<p>More than three decades on, <em>Man Of The Moment</em> is a play which is still incredibly relevant today and which stands as one of his piercing and significant works of the decade. The late ’80s was a particularly fertile and acclaimed period for the playwright and there’s no doubt, like his play, he was the man of the moment.</p>
