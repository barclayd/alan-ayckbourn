<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1958 when an aspiring actor called Alan Ayckbourn joined the company…</p>

<p><strong>1958: An Anthem of Disapproval</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>One of my favourite stories drawn from the history of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre is the furore which surrounded Stephen Joseph’s decision to withdraw the national anthem from performances in 1958.</p>

<p>In context, until the 1960s, the national anthem was essentially played at every theatre, concert and cinema performance – even in dance halls at the end of the night. Indeed, BBC1 played the national anthem at the end of the broadcasting day until as recently as 1997; essentially when rolling 24/7 programming began.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Stephen Joseph (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Stephen Joseph though was something of an anti-establishment rebel and, in 1958, he decided to stop the performance of the national anthem following every performance at the Library Theatre; becoming one of the first regional theatres to consistently stop the practise. It had already ceased at some London theatres and Stephen believed – correctly – it was a dying tradition.</p>

<p>Stephen’s decision today seems relatively innocuous – although one cannot help but think that he had more than an inkling that it would raise a few eye-brows and garner some publicity for the company. However, in context, this was quite a radical move – certainly for a provincial town during the 1950s. But it’s doubtful even Stephen could have imagined the roar of indignation which followed.</p>

<p>This was typified by the first – and finest – of many letters published in the Scarborough Evening News on 23 January 1958 from an aggrieved patron:</p>

<p>“<em>Sir, My wife, daughter, and two friends went to the theatre in the round at the Public Library. Neither at the start nor the finish of the show was the national anthem played. We were so seething with rage that we tackled the director of the show, Mr Joseph. His lame excuses were: (1) he did not think patrons wished it, (2) it was not good unless it was played by an orchestra and (3) it would be played at the last performance of the season. I hope that as the show is put on with the idea of making money, and as they are tenants of the corporation, the public will stay away until this is rectified. This is our intention although we have enjoyed the productions. Yours Streater Ellis, 66 Filey Road.”</em></p>

<p>The Scarborough Evening News letters’ pages were subsequently filled with much gnashing of teeth about whether the national anthem should or should not be played. At one point, the newspaper even reported the Mayor of Scarborough had been drawn into the argument.</p>

<p><em>“Speaking at a dinner on Saturday, the Mayor said: ‘I have received many letters asking me if I could persuade them to play the national anthem.’ The Mayor added: ‘It just seems ridiculous to me, considering that 75-90 per cent of the population of Scarborough want it.</em>‘”</p>

<p>This ‘fact’ was presumably found on the back of a beer mat or some such place (the famous joke, ”nine out of ten statistics are made up on the spot” comes to mind…), given the Town Hall later confirmed it had received no correspondence, as far as it was aware, pertaining to the lack of the national anthem.</p>

<p>Stephen Joseph, meanwhile, joined in the fun – as he was wont to do in such matters. Although he did not officially comment on the issue until the end of the year, he was notorious for writing inflammatory letters under pseudonyms to newspapers often pursuing vigorous arguments against himself under another pseudonym!</p>

</div>

<p>In this case, it’s almost certain he began a discourse under the names of ‘Republican’ and ‘Roundhead’ which includes this rather wonderful description of the national anthem.</p>

<p><em>“I would like to offer my sincere congratulations to Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre from saving us from the hideous noise, the supreme embarrassment and the maudlin sentimentalism occasioned by the playing of that barbaric and jarring thing (I refuse to call it a tune). The sooner all theatres do this the better.”</em></p>

<p>Although the argument raged throughout the summer, Stephen did not officially publicly comment until the end of the year in which he continued to hold his stance – with the rather cheeky comment the anthem would be played in the unlikely event royalty did visit.</p>

<p><em>“We feel that the playing of the anthem at every performance does not contribute to any feeling of respect. We would, of course, play it if royalty were to visit us. I feel that those people who objected in the summer were a bit eccentric, or they were people who did not often visit theatre or just happened to be feeling cross.”</em></p>

<p>It would be nice to think that Stephen’s comments closed a relative non-issue. Naturally, it didn’t</p>

<p>Every good narrative needs a nemesis and ours enters on 28 January 1959 in the shape of Maurice Plows; treasurer of the Scarborough Theatre Guild. Now to be fair to Maurice, he was a keen and long-term supporter of the theatre in the round. So much so that Alan Ayckbourn allegedly based one of the characters in <strong><em><a href="/plays/ten-times-table">Ten Times Table</a></em></strong> on him! But he was also someone who made his voice heard.</p>

<p>Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was, at this time, still working closely with the local amateur organisations, whose members were manning the front of house and doing work, and Maurice was a significant part of that scene.</p>

<p>Hence there was a quite a furore when Maurice announced he was resigning as treasurer of the Guild solely because of Stephen Joseph’s decision, as reported by the Scarborough Evening News.</p>

<p><em>“I have been approached by a well-known figure in Scarborough who asked me if I could do something about it [the playing of the national anthem at each performance]. I saw Mr Joseph who said that the national anthem was not played at every performance in London. But it is different here. If there is sufficient local demand – and there is – for the national anthem, then they should play it – or otherwise lose patrons…. If they [the patrons] are going to stay away because they think we are not playing the National Anthem, then we are going to lose money.”</em></p>

<p>Quite who the well-known figure who approached Maurice about this is never revealed, nor at any point in the coming months is any evidence presented about the depth of feeling in the community about this issue. Despite all that was said, there is no evidence it affected ticket sales or attendance at the Library Theatre in any note-worthy way.</p>

<p>Had sanity prevailed, that really should have been the end of the argument, but it was obviously a slow news month because the Scarborough Evening News took every opportunity to make something of the story in its letters pages, which even led to The Stage newspaper picking up the story.</p>

<p>Maurice, meanwhile, was standing his ground and banging the drum more loudly than ever, asserting in March that the number of people now not attending shows ran into “three figures.”</p>

<p>Quite how he deduced this is hard to evaluate. The company was – at that time – on tour from the town, visiting venues they had never visited before who were likely completely unaware of the controversy.  There were no shows for audiences in Scarborough to attend!</p>

<p>The Scarborough Mercury wryly noted that it had only received two letters in support of the national anthem. Perhaps all the rest were going directly to Maurice or Scarborough’s mayor…</p>

<p>Eventually, in a bid to close the issue down, a pro-active move was put forward by the theatre manager Rodney Wood, who announced a referendum would be taken from 22 – 26 June at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. If there was a substantive response in favour, the national anthem would be restored.</p>

<p>The argument seemed to lose wind by the end of March, after the announcement of the referendum and a particularly literate letter of support in the Scarborough Evening News.</p>

<p><em>“The matter is of little importance, quite divorced from the question of attendance at the theatre, and that the national anthem is incongruous in such a place…. If, as Mr Plow foretells, we all stay away rather than go and record our vote next summer, then perhaps it would be better if the theatre went elsewhere, rather than depend upon a public which puts petty trifles before art.”</em></p>

<p>Of the referendum, there was no further news, but needless to say, the playing of the national anthem was never restored at the theatre.</p>

<p>As a footnote, the issues still simmered 10 years later. By 1969, Stephen Joseph had died and the theatre had gone a decade without playing the national anthem at daily performances. The issue had not been raised in the press nor in committee minutes. Yet during the AGM of Scarborough Theatre Trust, the following was minuted:</p>

<p><em>“Secretary reported that he had received a request for some decision regarding the playing of the national anthem at the Library Theatre. After some discussion it was proposed Miss Clarke and seconded Mr Plows:- ‘That the national anthem be played.’ On a vote being taken it was defeated three votes for and four against. It was however agreed that the national anthem be played at the beginning and end of the season performances and when there was a civic night. This was carried.”</em></p>

<p>And so the issue of the national anthem at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was, finally, laid to rest.</p>

<p>Of course, the question regularly pops up when this story is told, what was the legal requirement at the time? Was Stephen breaking the law in not playing the national anthem?</p>

<p>The problem was the playing of the national anthem was only a tradition, not a requirement – although it had probably become the latter in some people’s minds. Stephen was well within his rights to stop playing the anthem.</p>

<p>A point confirmed when the Yorkshire Evening Post contacted the Lord Chamberlain at the height of the controversy about the supposed outrage and received this rather curt response.</p>

<p><em>“An official of the Lord Chamberlain’s office said today: ‘There is no regulation on this. We are completely indifferent to it, and would never contemplate issuing any order or advice.'”</em></p>

<p>Complete indifference. It’s a wonder the tradition lasted so long as it did. And it’s safe to say Stephen Joseph shared the same sentiment!</p>
