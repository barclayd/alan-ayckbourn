<p>With Steven Spielberg’s <em>The Fabelmans </em>currently garnering high praise at the cinemas, it seemed appropriate to turn the spotlight on one of its stars, his association with Alan Ayckbourn and his unlikely time performing in a Yorkshire seaside resort.</p>

<p>In 1994, Alan Ayckbourn directed the European premiere of Herb Gardener’s award-winning play, <em>Conversations With My Father</em>, at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round, Scarborough, from which it would later transfer to the Old Vic in London.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2023/1994-conversations-with-my-father-cover-c2a9-scarborough-theatre-trust-w300.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="426" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Poster for the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round production of Conversations With My Father (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Leading the revival was the acclaimed American actor, Judd Hirsch – recently nominated for an Oscar for his role of in Spielberg’s <em>The Fabelmans</em>. Hirsch had starred in the original production of <em>Conversations With My Father</em> in Seattle and then the Broadway production of the play which ran from 1992 and won him a Tony Award for Best Actor.</p>

<p>Which then led to reprising the role of Eddie Goldberg in the European premiere of the play at, where else, but Scarborough…</p>

<p>For British audiences at the time, Hirsch was still best known for the role of Alex Rieger in the television drama <em>Taxi</em>, although two years after he left Scarborough, he would find even wider fame in the blockbuster <em>Independence Day</em> as the father of Jeff Goldblum’s character.</p>

<p>But how on earth did he end up repeating his Tony award-winning role in a small regional theatre on the east coast?</p>

<p>According to Alan, the play came to him via Herb Gardner himself after Alan had previously directed Gardner’s play <em>A Thousand Clowns</em> in Scarborough in 1984.</p>

<p>“Herb sent me the play and I wrote back and said following our production of <em>A Thousand Clowns</em> I’d like to do it. &nbsp;He wrote back and said would I like Judd in it it because he thought Hirsch would be interested in doing it again. I said why not, if he he’d come to Scarborough! He came.”</p>

<p>And the rest, as they say, was history.</p>

<p>The production ran from 19 October to 26 November 1994 and was directed by Alan with an otherwise British cast. Hirsch came over to Scarborough with his wife Bonnie and their then six-month old daughter. In a series of published interviews drawn from an open press call at the theatre, we can get a sense of his feelings at being in the town.</p>

<p>Prior to arriving in Scarborough, Hirsch had never met Alan nor apparently even talked to him as both had a deep dislike of phones and communicated by fax! Still, Hirsch knew of Alan and thought highly of him.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Cutting from the Yorkshire Post showing Judd Hirsch in Scarborough (© Yorkshire Post, photographer not named)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>“I had heard about Alan and all the plays he had written and directed. I heard he was good and very well respected in the business,” said Hirsch. “Alan is a delight, wonderful. He really knows why this play is a comedy. I would say he’s the perfect director for it,”</p>

<p>As for Scarborough, he also had no idea what to expect there either.</p>

<p>“When they asked me if I wanted to do the play here I said, ‘where is it?’ Then I said, ‘Ah, it doesn’t matter. It’s England! I didn’t know what Scarborough was. Nobody sent me pictures, they didn’t even send me a map.”</p>

<p>In another report, he adds.</p>

<p>“I had no idea what to expect. When I asked people about Scarborough, one said it was like Coney Island, which is a huge entertainment centre. I haven’t seen one part of it that’s like Coney Island. I’m absolutely charmed by it.”</p>

<p>Hirsch loved his time in Scarborough and apparently enjoyed the anonymity and the ability to walk to work every day with the sea in view. He has stayed in contact with Alan over the decades and frequently mentions how much he enjoyed his time in Scarborough.</p>

<p>The in-the-round production was notable for its extraordinary intimacy and immersive creation of The Homeland Tavern, a bar in Lower Manhattan where the action of the play is set between Independence Day 1936 and the eve of the Bicentennial in 1976.</p>

<p>Fo the production, the front row was taken out and replaced by bar booths, so anyone sat in the front row was actually in the set on stage, occasionally heaving their tables vigorously cleaned by Hirsch.</p>

<p>This was apparently not only Hirsch’s first acting role in Europe, but also his first experience of performing in-the-round. Few who saw the extraordinary production – including myself – will forget the phenomenal performance by Hirsch.  He memorably said of the experience of performing in-the-round.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Poster for the Old Vic revival of Conversations With My Father (© The Old Vic)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>“It will be new to me to have the audience on your ear, in your nose and between your teeth!”</p>

<p>The play was a great success in Scarborough receiving critical plaudits not only for Hirsch but also Alan’s direction and the quality of the cast  (details can be found on Alan Ayckbourn’s Official website <a href="/career/directing-career/conversations-with-my-father">here</a>). So successful was it that the following March, it transferred to the Old Vic in London for an acclaimed production, although it was generally agreed the intimacy of the original in-the-round production in Scarborough was not matched.</p>

<p>Indeed, this was a view shared by Alan and Hirsch. It was also little reported, but the playwright Herb Gardner even visited the production in Scarborough and was apparently very impressed with it. In 1995, Alan’s wife Heather Stoney wrote in correspondence, “Both Judd Hirsch and Herb Gardner felt it had never been done better. That intimate form of staging truly served the play.”</p>

<p>So that’s the story of how one of America’s finest actors came to work with Alan Ayckbourn in his home town of Scarborough. An experience that neither Alan, Hirsch nor – I suspect – anyone who worked on the show or saw it, will have forgotten. </p>

<p>There’s also one final little piece of archivist trivia that I have to share. <em>Conversations With My Father</em> probably has one of the best programme ‘thank yous’ of the company’s history. At the bottom of the credits, it reads: “Thanks also to all those people who offered us mooseheads that turned out to be stags, deer and other forms of wildlife.”</p>

<p>But then, how many moose have you seen recently on the Yorkshire coast…</p>
