<p>The actor Sir Michael Gambon CBE has died at the age of 82.</p>

<p>In a long and illustrious career on stage and screen, he also has to be regarded as one of the most significant performers of Alan Ayckbourn’s work in the 20th century.</p>

<p>Upon hearing of the sad news, Sir Alan said of the actor.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn and Michael Gambon at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round, Scarborough, in 1990 (© The Scarborough News)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>“I owe an enormous amount to Michael.&nbsp;He was a remarkable stage performer.&nbsp;It was a privilege to watch him at work on my stuff.&nbsp; You couldn’t really term it acting – more an act of spontaneous combustion.”</p>

<p>Michael Gambon performed in more West End productions of Alan’s plays than any other actor and under Alan’s direction won three Olivier Awards. He was once described by the playwright as a ‘wonderful, limitless machine, like a Lamborghini’ and both had a mutual respect for each other’s work.</p>

<p>Gambon once said of his relationship with Alan: “I fell in love with Alan Ayckbourn the day I met him and, starting with&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>, went on to do eight plays with him. He tells you just what you want to know and has a brilliant way of solving problems.”</p>

<p>Alan first met Gambon in the London premiere of his trilogy, <em>The Norman Conquests</em>, in 1973. Performed at the Greenwich Theatre with an astonishing cast which included not only Gambon but Tom Courtenay, Felicity Kendal, Penelope Wilton and Penelope Keith, the trilogy was ostensibly directed by Eric Thompson but with Alan also on hand.</p>

<p>The production transferred into the West End, winning the Evening Standard and Play &amp; Players Best Play Awards, and began a long and successful relationship between Alan and Gambon. In 1990, Alan said of the actor.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Michael Gambon &amp; Felicity Kendal in The Norman Conquests (© Stephe Moreton Prichard)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>&nbsp;“He’s one of the most daring actors I know. If you are asking an actor to play a role, then as much of that actor as possible should get through to the part. A director can easily get in the way. I try to trot beside Michael like a sheep-dog, pointing out a few gates he may like to inspect. The more astute the actor, the more astute his choices will be. You can make suggestions and the best actors will give you the benefit of the doubt. You may think they’ve forgotten what you said, but then suddenly they’ll re-invent the idea for themselves. Michael does this superbly. He can adapt and improve and make something entirely his own.”</p>

<p>Gambon went on to play Neil in the 1977 West End premiere of <em>Just Between Ourselves</em> opposite Colin Blakeley and then joined Alan at the National Theatre as Patrick in <em>Sisterly Feelings</em> (1980) and Dafydd ap Llewellyn in <em>A Chorus of Disapproval</em> (1985); for the latter he won an Olivier Award for Best Comedy Performance.</p>

<p>When Alan was offered the role of company director at the National Theatre by Sir Peter Hall in 1986, Gambon was the first person Alan approached to join his company and the first to agree. Apparently the pair of them decided Alan’s choice of plays over starters at lunch, both preferring to get it out of the way and to concentrate on the main task of enjoying the meal ahead and each other’s company!</p>

<p>Gambon starred in Alan’s adaptation of Evans &amp; Valentine’s <em>Tons of Money</em> as the butler Sprules, as Jack McCracken in the acclaimed premiere of Alan’s own <em>A Small Family Business</em> and, most famously, as Eddie Carbone in Alan’s lauded production of <em>A View From The Bridge</em>.</p>

<p>Gambon won his second Olivier with Alan for Best Actor with this performance which was hailed by Miller as the ‘Best Eddie ever’, whilst also declaring the production had been the finest he had ever seen of his play. It transferred from the Lyttelton at the National to the Aldwych Theatre in the West End.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Michael Gambon in Man of the Moment (© John Haynes)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>In 1990, Gambon even spent a season in Scarborough with Alan at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round where he gave what Alan would consider a definitive performance as bucket salesman Roland Crabbe in Alan’s only true farce, <em>Taking Steps</em>.</p>

<p>That same year he would also give an acclaimed performance – cast against type – as the naive and innocent Douglas Beechey in the West End premiere of Alan’s <em>Man of the Moment</em>, which also won him an Olivier Award for Best Comedy Actor.</p>

<p>Gambon also appeared as Geoffrey in the BBC’s television adaptation of <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> in 1985, one of the best small screen adaptations of an Ayckbourn play.</p>

<p><em>Man of the Moment</em> marked the final time the pair worked together but Alan has said in the past Gambon gave some of the definitive performances of his work. In 1990, he spoke of the pleasure of working with Gambon and noted.</p>

<p>“What he does, he does brilliantly.”</p>
