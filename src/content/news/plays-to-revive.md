<p>An interesting question last week asked which Ayckbourn plays, as his archivist, I’d most like to see revived.</p>

<p>It’s a good question – do I choose favourites or plays I haven’t ever seen? Full admission here, despite being Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist, I’ve only seen 78 of his 90 plays (and I doubt I’ll get to see most of those missing 12!)</p>

<p>Giving it some thought I realised, it’s not the withdrawn plays but the rarely performed ones, I’d like to see come back. Ones that I genuinely loved to watch or read and wished had got a bit more exposure and which, in all likelihood, I’m probably not going to get the chance to see again (unlike, say <em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></strong></em>, <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em> or <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></strong></em>). So – for what it’s worth – here’s my list of five Ayckbourn plays I’d love to see revived. Feel free to disagree, agree or throw in your own favourites.</p>

</div>

<p><strong>1) <a href="/plays/wildest-dreams"><em>Wildest Dreams</em></a> (1991):</strong> I did see the world premiere of this and loved it – it was, in my opinion, way ahead of its time with a group of ordinary people using a fantasy role-playing game to escape reality only for reality to intrude with devastating consequences. RPGs were on the fringe in 1991 rather than the mainstream phenomenon they are today and it seems awfully prescient about how we use gaming to escape the ordinariness of life.</p>

<p>It’s also one of his early plays which brings fantastical elements into suburban environments – something which becomes a hallmark of his work from the ’90s onward. I know this divides some of his fans, who prefer to see the more rooted comedy of his early years – but Alan is a playwright constantly wanting to move forward and the introduction – often quite subtle – of the extraordinary into the ordinary has always fascinated me with regard to Alan’s plays.</p>

</div>

<p><strong>2)<a href="/plays/private-fears-in-public-places"> <em>Private Fears in Public Places</em></a> (2004):</strong> The play ignored by British critics but adored by American critics. This is, to my mind, the best play Alan wrote during the ’00s, a cinematically conceived piece of 54 fast-moving scenes looking at the lonely lives of thirty-somethings in London. It’s a sad, touching piece which doesn’t go where expected and structurally is quite unlike anything else he has written. The world premiere production was extraordinary, but it was also wonderfully reconciled as an almost immersive piece by Laurie Sansom for the Royal &amp; Derngate back in 2009.</p>

<p><em>Private Fears in Public Places</em> was also beautifully realised on film by Alain Resnais as <em><a href="/plays/private-fears-in-public-places/coeurs"><strong>Coeurs</strong></a></em> in 2006; a cinematic play met a theatrical film director and the result is, arguably, the finest film adaptation of an Ayckbourn play. <em>Private Plays in Public Places</em> is a play which touched me and is probably my favourite Ayckbourn of this millennium.</p>

<p><strong>3) <a href="/plays/man-of-the-moment"><em>Man of the Moment</em> </a>(1988):</strong> Another rather prescient play putting the media under the spotlight – although also better known as the play with the swimming pool. Again, rather prescient in how a criminal has become a media star whilst the good man who thwarted him years before has been forgotten. Given the explosion of reality television in the past two decades and the type of characters who have emerged as ‘stars’, one cannot help but feel Alan was reading the way media was heading very astutely.</p>

<p>It’s also though, a thoughtful and striking meditation on the nature of good and evil – a theme Alan has returned to several times subsequently. Sadly, Man of the Moment is rarely revived due to its cast size and, yes, that swimming pool. And what a shame, if only because how often do you see the stage direction <em>Vic turns and swims away</em>…?</p>

</div>

<p><strong>4) <a href="/plays/sisterly-feelings"><em>Sisterly Feelings</em></a> (1979):</strong> Alan’s original ‘random’ play in which a toss of the coin decides the choice of second scene. I’m with Alan here, as I know he’s talked to me about his desire to revive it since the early ’00s. It’s a piece where Alan is experimenting with structure – and is obviously a precursor to his epic <em><a href="/plays/intimate-exchanges"><strong>Intimate Exchanges</strong></a></em> with its 16 variants – but also a very typical Ayckbourn play about relationships and the people we choose – or don’t choose – to love. Unlike<em> Intimate Exchanges</em>, the structure is also interesting in that whatever decisions are made and direction the play takes, we always end up in the same final scene. Make of that what you will.</p>

<p>Sadly – much like <em>Man of the Moment</em> – the variants, large cast and – in the round, at least – need to take a quarter of the seating out has always made it a hard sell to theatres to revive. Yet this is a play which really epitomises Ayckbourn during this period, pushing at the boundaries of structure whilst reflecting on human nature, life, loss and love.</p>

<p>5) Finally, we’re back to more recent times and it’s a toss-up between <em><strong><a href="/plays/arrivals-and-departures">Arrivals and Departures</a></strong></em> (2013) or <em><strong><a href="/plays/a-brief-history-of-women">A Brief History of Women</a></strong></em> (2017). Both plays I found extremely moving on their world premiere productions.</p>

<p><em>Arrivals and Departures</em> has an unusual structure which is both surprising and allows for a stunning gut-punch surprise of a climax, whilst also putting the audience in the position of knowing far more about the two protagonists than they themselves will ever know. It’s a sad and tender play about love and loss which has been criminally ignored.</p>

<p><em>A Brief History of Women</em> is another touching play which is – again – structurally unusual. It covers 60 years of one man’s life – played throughout by the same actor – and the house he keeps returning to. Encompassing social and personal change, it offers a view of a life through the eyes of the women he has loved. There is sadness, laughter, tragedy – often closely connected – and a beautifully poignant ending. It is also a great piece for a company, giving one man a chance to age 60 years and the rest of the company to play multiple characters across different decades.</p>

<p>These then are a selection of Alan Ayckbourn’s plays I’d love to see revived. But, let’s face it, I’d like to see all of them revived. Repeatedly! But these present some of the plays I would most like to see and to also give audiences the chance to experience some of the less popular, but still great plays. <em>Relatively Speaking</em> is a classic, but just how many revivals can you do of it!</p>

<p>Especially when there’s another 89 plays out there waiting to be tackled!</p>

<p>Let me know your thoughts about plays you’d love to see revived, I’m sure they’ll all be fascinating.</p>

<p><em>Simon Murgatroyd, 2024</em></p>
