<p><em>2024 marks the 50th anniversary of Alan Ayckbourn’s famed trilogy transferring to the West End. To mark the anniversary, the blog will be running several articles relating to the trilogy over the next couple of weeks.</em></p>

<p><em>We begin by offering an overview of the trilogy from its creation in Scarborough to its West End triumph.</em></p>

<p><strong>The Norman Conquests: From East Coast to West End </strong>by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>Arguably the most famous of Alan Ayckbourn’s plays and certainly amongst the most successful,&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>&nbsp;are an early landmark in Alan Ayckbourn’s writing career.<br><br>Yet for something that is regarded as a milestone in 20th century British theatre, the plays had a surprisingly pedestrian origin.</p>

<p>In September 1972, in the aftermath of the success of&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></em></strong>&nbsp;at the playwright’s home venue, the&nbsp;<strong><a href="/career/ayckbourn-and-the-sjt/sjt-venues/theatre-in-the-round-at-the-library-theatre">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong>, Scarborough, a reporter for a local newspaper (the precise publication being lost to time) asked Alan what he planned to write next. Rather flippantly Alan replied he was thinking of writing a trilogy as apparently he couldn’t think of anything else to say; he did not mention this plan to any other publication and an end of season report in&nbsp;<em>The Stage</em>&nbsp;quotes Alan as saying he would write a new play – no mention of a trilogy – for the following summer.</p>

<p>Alan thought nothing more of the conversation until the unidentified newspaper in question ran a story in early 1973, while he was away in London, stating his next project would be a trilogy. A panicked call from the Scarborough Theatre Trust board – totally unaware of this proposal – enquired whether this was his actual intention. After brief consideration, Alan decided it wasn’t such a bad idea and began writing his first trilogy.</p>

<p>However, the idea of a multi-part play didn’t entirely come out of the blue, the seeds of it had come earlier in 1972 as he revealed in an interview about the trilogy in 1975.</p>

<p>Alan recalled being asked twice on the same day in 1972 if he would write plays for the recently opened Crucible Theatre in Sheffield; the Scarborough company was presenting a pre-season play there. Realising the venue had two auditoria, Alan postulated he could have two plays running simultaneously with the same cast; something which he would actually bring to fruition in 1999 with&nbsp;<em>House &amp; Garden</em>.</p>

<p>But this idea of a multi-part play likely originated here, given extra credence by the fact the same story was recalled by the trilogy’s London director Eric Thompson, who believed that Alan adapted this idea for Scarborough as one of the inspirations for&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>.</p>

<p>It should also be considered that Alan didn’t have to write a trilogy – there was no obligation despite the newspaper report. He could have either denied it or noted circumstances had changed. Why didn’t he? Looking back at Alan’s career and his creative decisions, one has the sense the challenge of the trilogy would have appealed greatly to him. Not only to write and direct a trilogy successfully, but also to write three plays for a summer seaside audience which would satisfy anyone whether they came to see one, two or three plays, without compromising the individual plays whilst also offering an extra satisfaction for those seeing all three works.<br><br>The reality of writing a trilogy was even more complex than it first appeared as paramount in Alan’s mind was this idea of not only writing three plays which worked together, but that were – in essence – independent of each other and that could be seen in any order and not necessarily with either of the other two plays. Alan was well aware the plays would dominate Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre’s summer season and that the company relied on tourists who might not necessarily be inclined to spend three nights of their holiday at the theatre and who might be put off by the thought of seeing a trilogy. As a result, the trilogy nature of the plays was not emphasised nor that ultimate satisfaction required seeing all three productions. This is most obvious in the fact the trilogy did not have an over-arching name during the original production.&nbsp;<br><br>Indeed, the plays were neither conceived of nor originally known as&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>. They were written with only their individual titles and referred to throughout the initial opening Scarborough season by the playwright and the company as ‘the trilogy’.<br><br>The title of&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>&nbsp;would only come later in London, probably inspired by Alan’s original Scarborough programme note for the trilogy which read “A weekend view of the Norman Conquest” alongside an article explaining the concept of the work..<br><br>The plays were written simultaneously over a week in May 1973 with Alan writing each play cross-wise (i.e. all the scenes ones after each other, than all of the scenes twos etc). Despite this method of working, Alan firmly believes each play has its own very distinct character:&nbsp;<em>Table Manners</em>&nbsp;is the funniest;&nbsp;<em>Round And Round The Garden</em>&nbsp;the more casual and conventional;&nbsp;<em>Living Together</em>&nbsp;a slower piece with a deliberate slackening of the pace. As to why Norman appears so late in&nbsp;<em>Table Manners</em>&nbsp;(the first of the plays to be rehearsed), this is simply because the actor playing Norman, Christopher Godwin, was unavailable for the first week of rehearsals and the script was written to accommodate this.</p>

<p>The plays – at this point called:&nbsp;<em>Fancy Meeting You</em>,&nbsp;<em>Make Yourself At Home</em> and&nbsp;<em>Round And Round The Garden</em>&nbsp;– opened in the summer of 1973 at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre and – once people had realised what was happening – were an enormous success. Alan directed and the production starred Christopher Godwin, Rosalind Adams, Janet Dale, Ronald Herdman, Alex Marshall and Stanley Page.</p>

<p>The local press reported the plays were selling out at every performance and&nbsp;<em>The Stage</em>&nbsp;announced the plays had broken every box office record at the venue. The plays are also fondly recalled at the theatre for the extraordinary occasion when a member of the audience laughed so hard she spat her false teeth out, which her husband came to recover from the box office the next day!<br><br>Although the trilogy was a huge success in Scarborough, a transfer to the West End was by no means inevitable nor – extraordinary as it seems now – was any sort of future guaranteed for them. Alan was of the opinion that it was probably not viable to take a trilogy to London (or anywhere else!) and had also been forewarned by his agent Margaret Ramsay (better known as Peggy) and various producers that a trilogy was box office death and it was unlikely anyone would realistically want to pick it up; as Alan subsequently noted, what this assumption was based on given the lack of theatrical trilogies in general at the time is anyone’s guess.<br><br>Several producers did tussle with the challenge of transferring the plays to London – albeit singularly rather than as a trilogy – but each favoured a different play; this did suggest to Alan all three plays were viable even if West End producers couldn’t agree which one was the best. Near the end of the original run in Scarborough, Alan’s current London producer Michael Codron reluctantly agreed to produce all three plays in London. Jubilation quickly turned to disappointment when several weeks later, he withdrew the offer having had second thoughts and deemed it too much of a financial risk.</p>

<p>Obviously disappointed, Alan put the trilogy away and began work on his next play&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a></em></strong>. At which point, it should be stressed Alan genuinely believed there was probably no future for the plays and they were unlikely to ever be picked up again. Indeed, Alan had so little faith in the trilogy being picked up that when his agent, Peggy, asked him what to do with the pile of scripts ‘cluttering’ her office. Alan told her to ‘burn them’! whilst he filed the plays away, genuinely not expecting they would be produced again</p>

<p>Historically, several Ayckbourn plays have been ‘put in the drawer’ and then either never been produced again, withdrawn or only been picked up again following extensive revisions. It is both the playwright and his wife’s contention that&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em> would probably have been forgotten and never produced again had they not been contacted by Eric Thompson, who had previously directed the West End productions of&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/time-and-time-again">Time And Time Again</a></em></strong>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em>Absurd Person Singular</em>.</p>

<p>There was a huge element of luck involved in saving <em>The Norman Conquests</em> from obscurity, not least the fact that Eric Thompson was in hospital and asked Alan if he had anything to read to while away the hours. Alan agreed and sent him some light reading with the trilogy! Eric read them and was very enthusiastic, quickly formulating for a plan to produce them. He believed Greenwich Theatre might be a viable venue and suggested Tom Courtenay – who had previously starred in&nbsp;<em>Time And Time Again</em>&nbsp;– might be interested in the trilogy. Eric’s suggestion the trilogy be mounted at a fringe venue offered less financial risk as well as a testing ground, where – if the plays were demonstrated to be both viable and successful – it would be easier to sell a West End transfer.</p>

<p>Greenwich Theatre agreed to stage the plays and Eric’s choice of an exceptionally strong cast of relative unknowns – Michael Gambon, Penelope Keith, Felicity Kendal, Mark Kingston and Penelope Wilton – working alongside the more established Tom Courtenay arguably helped to guarantee the plays’ future and success in London. Not that Eric was totally convinced he had a hit on his hands as Alan Ayckbourn recalls of a conversation on the first of the three premieres: “Eric said, ‘Do you realise we could be the first people in history with three flops in a row, because if they don’t like one, they sure as hell aren’t going to like the others!”‘ Fortunately for all concerned, the opposite occurred.</p>

<p>To expedite the prolonged rehearsal and help with the casting, Peggy meanwhile contacted Michael Codron – who was aware of the Greenwich plans. A deal was struck that Codron would underwrite the cost of rehearsals in return for first refusal on any West End transfer. In a shrewd move for all concerned, the deal was struck and Codron’s involvement kept quiet.<br><br>Rehearsals began in London and, unusually, Alan became a participant in the production at Greenwich and was actively involved in rehearsals; Eric would direct the brunt of the play, but when it came to the set-pieces such as the dinner party in&nbsp;<em>Table Manners</em>, he would invite Alan to step in, who would block the scene and direct the basics before Eric took over again. This participation was only made public in a BBC documentary in 2011 and it could be argued&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests&nbsp;</em>rather than&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></em></strong>&nbsp;marked Alan’s directorial debut in London.</p>

<p>Further weight could be given to this claim as it has subsequently transpired that during its 20 month West End run, there were several cast changes which Eric Thompson was unable to direct. The producer, Michael Codron, instead asked Alan to redirect the recasts, but he was uncredited in programmes and apparently paid cash-in-hand once a week! Despite all this, <em>Bedroom Farce&nbsp;</em>is still officially regarded as Alan’s London directing debut in 1977.</p>

<p>As for the plays themselves, there was little alteration to the texts between Scarborough and London, but there were several other notable changes. The trilogy now became known as&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>&nbsp;as a means of emphasising to the audience they should see all three plays – although in no particular order. Two of the original titles were also altered:&nbsp;<em>Fancy Meeting You</em>&nbsp;became&nbsp;<em>Table Manners</em>;&nbsp;<em>Make Yourself At Home</em>&nbsp;became&nbsp;<em>Living Together</em>, leaving only&nbsp;<em>Round And Round The Garden&nbsp;</em>unaltered.<br><em><br>The Norman Conquests&nbsp;</em>played at Greenwich from May to June, 1974, and was an incredible and immediate success. Such was the overwhelming response to the trilogy that it was reported at the time there was a rush from West End producers to take on the plays with one article citing the producer Michael Codron had to apparently pay a vastly inflated price to secure the rights as a result of this. This was journalistic invention though, as Codron had been a silent partner throughout the Greenwich experience. His involvement had been kept quiet as it was felt if the management of Greenwich Theatre realised he had helped fund the trilogy – implying the transfer to the West End was practically a given – there would be scope for the theatre to make more financial demands. As a result, Codron’s role in the Greenwich success was played down and he only stepped into the limelight when the transfer was officially confirmed, giving the impression he had only just become involved with it.</p>

<p>The Greenwich production also marked a first for the trilogy for on Saturday 29 June 1974, Greenwich Festival Gala Day saw all three parts of the play performed on the same day; this had never been attempted during the world premiere run in Scarborough. It also – intriguingly – broke the established performance pattern which has generally been used for trilogy day.&nbsp;<em>Living Together</em>&nbsp;rather than&nbsp;<em>Table Manners</em>&nbsp;was performed first at 11am, followed by&nbsp;<em>Table Manners</em>&nbsp;at 3.30pm and&nbsp;<em>Round And Round The Garden</em>&nbsp;at 8pm. For the day, every female audience member was presented with a flower and a ticket to see all three plays cost either £1.80, £2.45 or £3.80 depending on the seating – 60p a play for the cheap seats!<br><br>Michael Codron took the trilogy into the West End with virtually the same cast – Bridget Turner taking over from the unavailable Penelope Wilton – and it opened at the Globe Theatre on 1 August, 1974. The response to the transfer was just as enthusiastic as it had been at Greenwich and&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>&nbsp;would win the&nbsp;<em>Evening Standard</em>&nbsp;and the&nbsp;<em>Plays And Players</em>&nbsp;Awards for Best New Play; notably this marked the first time Alan won the Evening Standard Best Play Award. In addition, Felicity Kendal also won the Variety Club’s Most Promising Newcomer Award. </p>

<p>The plays would run until 13 March 1976 and the long-running success included a transfer from the Globe Theatre to the Apollo Theatre in December 1975. By this point, Alan was breaking theatre records with five plays running simultaneously in the West End (<em>Absurd Person Singular</em>,&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>&nbsp;trilogy and&nbsp;<em>Absent Friends</em>).</p>

<p>Following the conclusion of its successful London run in 1976, there was immense interest in the future of the trilogy with one of the first suggestions being a national tour originating with the Cambridge Theatre Company. Alan, however, was keen that the plays be released to repertory theatres as soon as possible where he saw a more natural home for them. No-one could actually see how a tour of&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>&nbsp;would work anyway – significantly Michael Codron had already dismissed the idea unless each theatre on the tour committed to at least three-week long visits.</p>

<p>With considerable demand from repertory theatres across the country, the trilogy was released for production in 1976 and the first regional production was staged by the recently opened Theatre Clywd, Mold, on 9 August 1976. Other productions soon followed throughout the UK, cementing the trilogy as a popular draw and Alan’s burgeoning reputation as one of the country’s most successful playwright. In 1977,&nbsp;<em>The Norman Conquests</em>&nbsp;was apparently the most performed work in the country followed by&nbsp;<em>Macbeth</em> and Alan’s previous hit&nbsp;<em>Absurd Person Singular</em>. It was estimated that in 1977 alone, 950,000 people saw the trilogy on stage in the UK alone.</p>

<p>It would only get bigger over time. It transferred to Broadway, was filmed for television, adapted for the radio and proved – despite the author’s beliefs – to be hugely popular with both professional and amateur companies both in the UK and around the world. It would become one of Alan’s biggest successes and one of his most acclaimed, awarded and well-known works. In 2000, the National Theatre named it as one of the 100 most significant works of the century.</p>

<p>And all because of a flippant remark to a journalist in Scarborough.</p>
