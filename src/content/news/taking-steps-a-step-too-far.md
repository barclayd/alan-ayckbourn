<p><em><a href="/plays/taking-steps">Taking Steps</a> is considered by Alan Ayckbourn to be his only true farce and it has delighted audiences across the globe since it premiered in 1979.</em></p>

<p><em>It is one of the few plays by Alan Ayckbourn which is specifically written for theatre-in-the-round; whilst the vast majority of Alan’s works are conceived and premiered in-the-round, most can transfer successfully to the end-stage.</em></p>

<p><em>Taking Steps though is a play whose effectiveness is, inarguably, deeply compromised by any staging other than the round due to its central conceit of having three stories of the same house portrayed in the same space with characters on different levels crossing over and responding to what is above and below. For this to work, the audience has to see the stage floor.</em></p>

<p><em>On 24 March 2010, Taking Steps opened in London at the Orange Tree Theatre, directed by Alan Ayckbourn. It marked the first time the play had been performed in London in-the-round and was a huge success.</em></p>

<p><em>But Taking Steps had been performed in the Wests End thirty years earlier with an entirely different result. This is the story of how Taking Steps initially ended up in the West End and the effect it had on Alan Ayckbourn.</em></p>

<p><strong>Taking Steps: A Step Too Far</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>When <em>Taking Steps</em> opened in Scarborough in 1979, its success took even the playwright by surprise. This was, he considered, his first true farce and he had not approached it lightly.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2022/taking-steps-w400.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="400" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>West End poster for Taking Steps</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Despite his phenomenal success in writing comedy and tragi-comedy, the playwright had largely avoided farce until then as he believed – and still believes – it is extremely difficult to write well and requires a lot of experience to pull off. Indeed, he had such difficulties finishing <em>Taking Steps</em> that rehearsals had to be delayed by two days whilst he worked on the climax!</p>

<p><em>“I took an enormous breath when I wrote Taking Steps – it was a tussle hence the late delivery of the script. I hadn’t written a farce for a long time and by God, they’re hard work.”</em></p>

<p>All the effort proved to be worth it as the play was enthusiastically received by the Scarborough audience. The laughter was so loud, it blew the theatre’s relay and Alan was delighted to see – for the first time – someone actually fall out of their seat laughing! It was a huge success.</p>

<p>Inevitably, Alan knew there would be immediate interest in transferring <em>Taking Steps</em> to the West End and Alan was wary about this, particularly so soon after its immediate success. Alan’s recent forays into the West End had not been the successes of the past and he was wary of overexposure and a backlash.</p>

<p>He had taken on the role of director for his West End productions for the first time in 1977 with <em><a href="/plays/ten-times-table">Ten Times Table</a></em>, followed by <em><a href="/plays/joking-apart">Joking Apart</a></em> in 1978 (prior to this he had also directed <em><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></em> at the National Theatre in 1977). Whilst <em>Bedroom Farce</em> has been an overwhelming success at the National, <em>Ten Times Table</em> had only done respectably well whilst <em>Joking Apart</em> had actually lost money; neither of the latter two being the hoped for successes in London.</p>

<p>One of the problems was Alan’s plays were being measured against the phenomenal success of <em><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></em> and <em><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></em>; but just four years on and Alan was not the same playwright he had been then and his plays had taken a darker and more serious turn which had not necessarily gone down well with West End audiences.</p>

<p>He was also beginning to have serious concerns about the West End. He was convinced that rarely if ever were his original Scarborough actors improved on in the West End and the London productions were generally loaded with stars who either deliberately or accidentally unbalanced what were always written as ensemble pieces. With the reception of <em>Taking Steps</em> in Scarborough fresh in his mind, he expressed disquiet to both his agent Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay and his regular London producer Michael Codron about a transfer, wondering what the point was of transferring a play to London, which would almost inevitably be inferior to the original production.</p>

<p><em>“I’m deeply concerned about the future of Taking Steps. I feel it shouldn’t be done and put on. It’s just had three of the best receptions that I’ve ever had for any play of mine in Scarborough and suddenly there we were talking about Donald Sinden and Dinsdale Landen and I thought what are we doing? It’s lovely the way it is. Not perfect but undoubtedly better than those two could do it. And it will get better….”</em></p>

<p>Codron reassured Alan that the best interests of the play would always be served and despite Alan’s initial misgivings, a decision to take it into the West End was agreed. However, Codron decided not to give Alan the opportunity to direct the play, which Alan’s biographer Paul Allen suggests was the decision of Codron given the lack of success of his previous West End production <em>Joking Apart</em>. If true, it was a vote of no-confidence which backfired. Whatever the reason, Alan meanwhile went to direct <em><a href="/plays/sisterly-feelings">Sisterly Feelings</a></em> at the National Theatre, the run of which crossed over with that of <em>Taking Steps</em>.</p>

<p>Directing duties were given to Michael Rudman, then Director of the National’s Lyttelton Theatre and a well-respected director, although he had never directed an Ayckbourn play before. Although Alan would converse in writing with Michael, he largely absented himself from the rehearsal process as previous experience has demonstrated that his own strong feelings about how his work should be directed might interfere with the actual director’s vision of the play.</p>

<p>When Alan did visit the rehearsals, it was not a happy experience. As had happened so frequently in the past with London productions of Alan’s plays, a star turn was cast in an ensemble piece with the actor Dinsdale Landen turning in a bravura and overwhelming performance as Roland Crabbe, becoming the de facto star. Alan had expressed strong reservations about Landen very early in the process. To him, it emphasised the play had been misinterpreted. As the playwright has frequently pointed out, if <em>Taking Steps</em> has a lead character (or a character who has a dramatic journey), it’s Tristram not Roland.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn on the in-the-round set of the world premiere of Taking Steps (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Alan had long believed the play would be extremely difficult to produce in a proscenium arch theatre and his fears were quickly confirmed. The play’s designer was Alan Tagg, who had just finished working with Alan on <em>Sisterly Feelings</em> (another difficult play to stage as Tagg had also had to deal with the vast Olivier auditorium at the National Theatre). Alan has a great deal of respect for Tagg, but felt even he had been bested by what was an impossible situation.</p>

<p><em>Taking Steps</em> was intended to be performed where the entire audience could see the entire layout of the stage-floor and appreciate the joke of the overlaid floors. In the proscenium arch, the overlapping set was just confusing and for the staircases – which had worked so elegantly and clearly in the round – the company ran on the spot by a banister. It might have been good for a cheap laugh, but the subtleties of the original were being steadily lost. Alan would later say the designer was forced to meet an impossible brief.</p>

<p><em>“When Michael Rudman did it in London, he tried to stage it – with the excellent Alan Tagg designing – in a more conventional way. The result, I have to say, was a terrible, unsatisfactory mess. It looked a bit like a furniture store.”</em></p>

<p>Ultimately, there is no avoiding the fact that <em>Taking Steps</em> was written specifically for the round and this does not seem to have been seriously taken into account in the desire to transfer it to the West End.</p>

<p><em>Taking Steps</em> opened on 2 September 1980 at the Lyric Theatre with Alan in attendance; Paul Allen’s Ayckbourn biography sums up the experience.</p>

<p>“On the London opening night of <em>Taking Steps</em> the Act 1 curtain arrived to almost complete silence, in contrast to the aching roar greeting it in Scarborough, and things were not much better at the end. Alan was aware of the sound of [his partner] Heather sobbing beside him. ‘She was more upset than I was. I just went out into the night,’ he says, but I suspect his upset was simply buried at once.”</p>

<p>The reviews were not deadly, but they were mixed to say the least, ranging from poor to indifferent to good. Landen’s performance was praised, which must have made Alan wince as it gave the mistaken impression Roland was the star part. The play ran for nine months and achieved a longer run than the previous West End Ayckbourn production <em>Joking Apart</em>; although Codron’s autobiography states <em>Taking Steps</em> did not recoup its production costs during its West End run, only turning a profit after it had completed a subsequent tour.</p>

<p><em>“Taking Steps had a rather unhappy London run in a conventional proscenium theatre… it was the one that got away.”</em></p>

<p>To Alan’s mind the production was a colossal disappointment and he’d seen a successful show become so much less in London. His only comfort was that the play was running simultaneously in Scarborough with the polar opposite reaction.</p>

<p>To this day, <em>Taking Steps</em> marks the only time a West End production and a production at his home theatre in Scarborough have over-lapped. After his experiences with <em>Taking Steps</em>, he became increasingly cautious about West End transfers and would never allow an overlap with one of his own productions.</p>

<p><em>“It’s very interesting: it’s the first time that a play of mine – Taking Steps – opened in London while it was still running in Scarborough. I’ve never done that before. It was like looking at two pictures. And you say: ‘Well, I don’t care what they say down there and whether they think this or that. There’s a whole group of people in here who are having a marvellous time.’ And in that sense one was perhaps able to survive the buffers of that experience better.”</em></p>

<p>Alan and Rudman would go on to have long and involved correspondence about the show, analysing what went wrong. That mistakes were made cannot be denied, but perhaps the biggest mistake was ignoring Alan’s initial belief that this play should never have transferred to the West.</p>

<p>But perhaps the biggest issue lay just in the different perceptions of the play. Alan and Rudman had differing opinions about how farces should be structured and work. Alan has always firmly stated that for farces to be successful they have to be rooted in the normal and ordinary for the audience to be willing take the trip into ever more improbable situations. He felt the Lyric production was a lost cause from the start as the characters had been made as extraordinary as possible from the word go.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2022/taking-steps-2010-w400.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="576" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Poster for The Orange Tree’s 2010 revival of Taking Steps</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p><em>“The productions that haven’t worked have been the ones where – and I’m afraid that’s what happened in the London production – you try to comment and indicate and make it comic. Of all my plays, this one needs the most serious playing.”</em></p>

<p>The combination of another less than happy West End experience in contrast to a very successful run of <em>Sisterly Feelings</em> at the National Theatre led Alan to quietly re-evaluate his position in London. Between 1979 and 1986, not one Ayckbourn play had its London premiere in the West End. Any West End productions during that period were transfers from other London theatres, be it <em><a href="/plays/seasons-greetings">Season’s Greetings</a></em> and<em> <a href="/plays/intimate-exchanges">Intimate Exchanges</a></em> from Greenwich or <em><a href="/plays/a-chorus-of-disapproval">A Chorus Of Disapproval</a></em> from the National Theatre.</p>

<p>It would be 1986 before Alan staged a London premiere of a play in the West End with <em><a href="/plays/woman-in-mind">Woman In Mind</a></em> and he would never again let anyone but himself direct one of his new plays in the West End. <em>Taking Steps</em> undoubtedly marked a watershed for Alan and his relationship with London and he would never again be comfortable taking his writing into an environment which seemed to desire the opposite of what he intended for this plays.</p>

<p>It was not until 2010 that Alan believed that <em>Taking Steps</em> received the production it deserved in London with the revival at the Orange Tree Theatre, in-the-round and directed by Alan himself. It was a critical and commercial success and Alan felt vindicated that ‘the one that got away’ finally got the production it deserved.</p>
