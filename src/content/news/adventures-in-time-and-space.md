<p>Today marks the 60th anniversary of the long-running British sci-fi television series, <em>Doctor Who</em>.</p>

<p>And what an excellent opportunity to indulge and combine some of my great passions, Alan Ayckbourn, theatre in the round and Doctor Who looking at some connections between them.</p>

</div>

<p>Also I harbour the suspicion that most archivists have a secret envy of the good Doctor – for in his craft the TARDIS, he has a machine larger on the inside than the outside and which is capable of travelling in time. As an archivist, I yearn for unlimited shelf space for the archive and the ability to nip back and pick up anything missing from the Ayckbourn Collection.</p>

<p>But I digress…</p>

<p>Doctor Who premiered on 23 November 1963, famously slightly delayed due to the BBC making an announcement about the previous day’s assassination of President John F Kennedy. In fact, Alan Ayckbourn himself recalls taking to the stage at the Victoria Theatre, Stoke-on-Trent, prior to that evening’s production of his play <em>Mr Whatnot</em>, to announce the President had been shot but the production would still be continuing.</p>

<p>The first major connection with <em>Doctor Who</em> is at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough – the theatre where Alan began writing and directing professionally and which, as the Stephen Joseph Theatre, he is still actively connected to today.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2023/dw-01-w240.jpg" alt="" width="240" height="300" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Elisabeth Sladen as Sarah Jane Smith and Tom Baker as the fourth Doctor (© BBC)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>The 1968 summer season saw a young actor named Tom Baker, very early in his acting career, join the rep company. This ebullient actor would appear in <em>Arden Of Feversham</em>, Noël Coward’s <em>Hay Fever</em>, Carl Sternheim’s <em>The Strongbox</em> and David Bramley’s <em>A Boat In The Backyard</em>. Shortly afterwards he was spotted and encouraged to audition for the National Theatre, where he worked for three years.</p>

<p>In 1974, Tom would then step into his most famous role as the fourth Doctor – regenerating from the previous incumbent Jon Pertwee. With his floppy hat and long scarf, he became an instantly recognisable figure and is regarded as one of the most famous Doctors. He would hold the role from 1974 until 1981; the actor to have played the Doctor for the longest amount of time and would be forever associated with the role, even returning for its 50th anniversary in 2013 as the mysterious Curator.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Elisabeth Sladen in Family Circles and Tom Baker in The Strongbox at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Every Doctor has their companion and, for Tom, it was initially Sarah Jane Smith, one of the most popular of all the companions. Sarah was played by Elisabeth Sladen, who worked with Alan Ayckbourn at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre for two seasons in 1969 and 1970.</p>

<p>Elisabeth appeared in seven productions including the world premieres of Alan Ayckbourn’s <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> (1969) and <em>Family Circles</em> (1970). She was also one of the last people to act opposite Alan on stage. Alan had retired from acting in 1964 but when the actor playing Frank Foster slipped a disc during the first week of <em>How The Other Half Loves</em>, he took to the stage for several performances on the book as Frank opposite Elisabeth as his wife, Fiona.</p>

<p>Famously, on the the first night, he discovered he had lost the final page of the script and promptly began improvising the climax besides an astonished Elisabeth. So impressed was he with some of the improvisation, he apparently subsequently added some of it to the script!</p>

<p>Elisabeth joined <em>Doctor Who</em> in 1973 opposite Jon Pertwee’s third Doctor and stayed until 1976 with Tom Baker. She would return several times to the show, most notably when she appeared opposite David Tennant in the episode <em>School Reunion</em> (2006). This would lead to several more adventures with the Doctor and her own successful spin-off series <em>The Sarah Jane Adventures</em>.</p>

<p>Elisabeth sadly died in 2011, aged just 65, and Alan Ayckbourn paid tribute to her work in Scarborough in the documentary<em> Our Sarah Jane</em>.</p>

<p>For the next major connection, we have to move forward in time – fortunately we have the TARDIS in Scarborough on the sea front, as you can see from the top picture of the ‘enigmatic’ Archivist stood outside what appears to be a normal vintage Police telephone box. We now travel forward to 1994 when a young writer called Robert Shearman had his first professionally commissioned play from Alan Ayckbourn with <em>White Lies</em>, which premiered at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round.</p>

<p>Rob would go on to have five world premieres in Scarborough between 1994 and 2001, two of which – <em>Fool To Yourself</em> (1997) and <em>Knight in Plastic Armours </em>(1999) were directed by Alan Ayckbourn. He subsequently went onto to an award-winning career as playwright, prose writer and screenplay writer. His mind-bending collection of short stories, <em>We All Hear Stories on the Dark</em> (2020), is influenced by Alan’s play <em>Intimate Exchanges</em> with its virtually infinite number of permutations.</p>

<p>Rob wrote one of the most acclaimed episodes of the modern incarnation of <em>Doctor Who</em> with the episode <em>Dalek</em>. Written for the first series of the re-launched programme in 2005 with Christopher Eccleston as the 10th Doctor, it reintroduced the Doctor’s nemesis the Daleks to the screen after a 17 year absence and was massively popular and acclaimed. Rob would also write the audio play <em>The Chimes of Midnight</em> (2002) for the eighth Doctor, Paul McGann, which is regarded as one of the most popular of the many audio adventures of the Doctor.</p>

<p>Finally – and bringing us bang up-to-date – this Saturday sees the return of <em>Doctor Who</em> on BBC1 with the debut of the 14th Doctor played by David Tennant – who also played the 10th Doctor. (Don’t worry if you’re not keeping up with this, it’s all very timey-wimey as the Doctor would say).</p>

<p>In it, the Doctor reunites with his former companion Donna Noble (played by Catherine Tate, who played Belinda in the National Theatre’s 2010 revival of Alan Ayckbourn’s <em>Season’s Greetings</em>) and her mum, Sylvia Noble, played by Jacqueline King. She first appeared in <em>Doctor Who</em> in 2006 and has been a popular recurring character ever since. </p>

<p>Jacqueline has worked extensively with Alan Ayckbourn in both his home town of Scarborough and the West End. She first worked with him in his 1997 revival of <em>Absent Friends</em>. before appearing in the world and West End premieres of <em>Comic Potential</em> (1998/1999). She then went onto appear in the world and West End premieres of the <em>Damsels in Distress</em> trilogy – <em>GamePlan</em>, <em>FlatSpin</em> and <em>RolePlay</em> (2001/2002). Subsequently she has worked with Alan in Scarborough in a revival of <em>Henceforward…</em> (2016) and the world premiere of <em>No Knowing</em> (2016).</p>

<p>And these are my favourite <em>Doctor Who</em>, Alan Ayckbourn and Theatre in the Round connections. There are many, many more creatives Alan has worked with in Scarborough and the West End who have made notable contributions to<em> Doctor Who</em>, but these are personal favourites.</p>

<p>So it’s time for me to step-back  into my TARDIS now. There’s an infinite number of shelves to catalogue and archive – and dust…</p>

<p>Or perhaps I should just take a break and hop into the future to see the new <em>Doctor Who</em> adventure or maybe the world premiere of next year’s Alan Ayckbourn world premiere, <em>Show &amp; Tell</em>. Spoilers…</p>

<p>Happy 60th Anniversary, Doctor Who!</p>
