<p><em>2024 marks the 50th anniversary of one of Alan Ayckbourn’s most obscure and least seen works, <strong><a href="/plays/service-not-included">Service Not Included</a></strong>.</em></p>

<p><em>If it rings no bells with you, you won’t be alone. This is Alan’s only produced screenplay for television, was broadcast only once in 1974 in the UK and has never been made available since. It truly is an Ayckbourn obscurity.</em></p>

<p><em>To mark the 50th anniversary, here’s a little history of the piece and how it came to be.</em></p>

<p><strong>The Forgotten TV Play</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>One of the most notable aspects of Alan Ayckbourn’s long writing career is that, unlike the majority of bis peers, Alan’s head has never been turned by the allure of the screen – large or small.</p>

<p>Although many of his works have been adapted to film and television, he has never adapted them himself and has had next to no involvement in the vast majority of them. He is, as he has joked, a “theatre beast” and has never shown any particular interest in writing for television or film.</p>

<p>Which means that even the most ardent Ayckbourn fan would probably tell you Alan has never written a produced screenplay, as the playwright himself has frequently noted how it is not something which has ever interested him.</p>

<p>Yet, 50 years ago on BBC2, a new work by Alan Ayckbourn debuted, written specifically for television. It was and remains his only produced screenplay and it has largely been forgotten and is now barely remembered or known.</p>

<p>The story begins in 1969 when the BBC adapted Alan’s break out West End hit, <em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking"><strong>Relatively Speaking</strong></a></em>, for television. This marked the first time one of his plays had been adapted for television – although there had been, two years previously, an abridged broadcast of the West End production of the play.</p>

<p>The television adaptation of  <em>Relatively Speaking</em> was directed by Herbert Wise, who – in 1977 – would also direct the phenomenally successful television adaptation of <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></strong></em> and would become a BAFTA and Emmy Award winning director particularly known for the acclaimed BBC series <em>I, Claudius</em>, starring Derek Jacobi.</p>

<p>In 1973, he approached Alan about participating in a newly commissioned drama for BBC2 called <em>Masquerade</em>. The idea was to gather six leading writers to produce half-hour pieces which shared the theme of ‘a masked fancy dress party, taking place now in a large 19th century country house.’ Other than that, the writers were free to take the idea wherever they liked – dependent on budget, naturally.</p>

<p>Alan – who had no experience writing screenplays – agreed to participate largely “as a personal favour” for Herbert, whom he knew. He was commissioned by the BBC in July 1973 to write a single half-hour episode for <em>Masquerade</em>.</p>

<p>Naturally it transpired there were certain other limitations to what he could write within this agreed framework. Alan was advised the screenplay should have no more than four main speaking parts and not more than four sets.</p>

<p>Undeterred, Alan wrote to Herbert noting he already had an idea about “an end-of-convention party for a group of Rentokil representatives and their wives.” He didn’t immediately set about writing the play as he was in the midst of running the summer season at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, and had only just finished directing the world premiere productions of what was then dubbed ‘The Trilogy’ but which would, the following year, become famous as <em>The Norman Conquests</em>.</p>

<p>Once the season had finished in September, Alan turned his mind to other projects and, in November 1973, wrote his first screenplay under the title <em>Now Being Served</em>. A single page of handwritten notes survive which hold his basic idea as well as a sketch of the layout of the ‘dance hall’.</p>

<p>The notes read: “Waiter’s eye view of man trying to make the girl (she a culture snob, the intended and plain friend plus rival man). Hotel – A weekend convention, out of season.” There are also ideas for names including the waiter being named as Reg and Jerry – both crossed out – with Jace written over the latter name.</p>

<p>Alan finished writing the first draft of the script on 30 November 1973 and sent it both to his agent, &nbsp;<strong><a href="/life/influences/ramsay-margaret">Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay</a></strong>, and the BBC on 1 December.</p>

<p>It was not a pleasant writing experience and Alan wrote to Peggy that: “It has taken me more time than any full length play ever took me [to write] – and I’m far less sure of the result.” Accompanying the script to the BBC was a note to Herbert suggesting it was “very downbeat for me.”</p>

<p>Alan later noted that it was probably the most expensive episode of the series to produce. “Since I had no clear brief aside from a fancy dress party, I wrote multiple locations with a huge cast; consequently it cost more than a low budget film to produce.”</p>

<p>The screenplay was, however, well-received although there were initial concerns about the budget and the size of the cast – which included a three piece band. Enthusiastic about the idea, Herbert arranged a slightly larger budget with only the band having to be dropped.</p>

<p>With these amends in hand, Alan wrote a final draft of the screenplay which was sent to Herbet on 4 February 1974. The final script has no overarching plot as such, but offers a waiter’s eye view of an end of conference party at a hotel. All the events of the night are seen through the eyes of the waiter Jace, offering snippets of conversation between the party-goers, particularly focusing on the desperate attempts of one employee to have the manager see his proposals whilst his wife is having an affair with an associate.</p>

<p>The first read-through for the script took place on 18 March 1974 and the cast notably included Alan Thompson as Jace the waiter, Alan Wilson as the porter with Colin Farrell (not the one you’re probably thinking of!) as the servile company man, Donald, with Julian Curry as the lecherous Neil and Ann Beach as Neil’s wife, Freda, and Irene Hamilton as Donald’s wife, Cathy. Also of note is a cameo by Heather Stoney – Alan’s partner at the time and future wife.</p>

<p>Filming was scheduled for 26 – 27 March, following a week of rehearsals, and took place entirely on location at the Berystede Hotel, Ascot. All interior and exterior shots were filmed on location and the entire cast numbered 16 actors, most in non-speaking roles.</p>

<p>With the title now altered to&nbsp;<em>Service Not Included</em>, it was broadcast on BBC2 at 10.25pm on 20 May 1974.  It was advertised in the Radio Times as “A post-conference celebration at the Magpie Manor Hotel. Beneath the gaiety – welter of casual back-stabbing. The hotel staff look on with deafening silence, alert for the pickings.”</p>

<p>There are no viewing figures held for the broadcast but Alan once joked it had “an audience of five people. Hardly worth it” and noted there was “virtually no response from the public or critics. It was soon forgotten.”</p>

<p>Indeed, there is only one review held in archive, which was published in The Stage newspaper on 23 May 1974 written by the critic James Scott under the headline ‘Enjoyable Half-Hour’. It’s an odd review as, although positive, it does begin with the somewhat contradictory line, “This story was neither original nor very exciting… I can and do say that it was the most enjoyable half-hour of television drama I have seen in a long time.”</p>

<p>The review goes on to say the drama was “done here so wickedly well, so bulls-eye accurate, that it came over fresh, pointed and cunningly cruel. Each actor seemed to get his or her part just right, playing for no more and no less than the full value of the character portrayed.”</p>

<p>Despite this review, the episode was never repeated and has never been seen again on television. It has also never been released commercially. No wonder, as Alan said, it was soon forgotten.</p>

<p>In fact there does seem a bit of a mystery about the entire series. It was billed in the Radio Times as “Six writers invite you to come to the party – and see what happens.” But only five episodes were ever broadcast – with Alan’s being the final episode – and no explanation ever given as to what happened to the sixth episode.</p>

<p>It was also – considering its late time-slot – not a low-profile series and the list of contributors was impressive. The series ran with <em>Turkish Delight</em> by Caryl Churchill (22 April) starring Julia Foster and Elizabeth Bell; <em>May We Come In</em> by Eric Chappell (29 April) starring Ian Holm and a, then unknown, Michael Gambon; <em>Miitzen Ah!</em> by Charles Wood (6 May) starring Leonard Rossiter; <em>Something Down There Is Crying</em> by Adrian Mitchell (13 May) starring Elkie Brooks.</p>

<p>It’s also worth noting that Alan’s episode was broadcast just after the London premieres of the first two parts of <em>The Norman Conquests</em> at the Greenwich Theatre, which had secured an extensive amount of press and media coverage – yet there appears to be no attempt to cross promote the series or tie into Alan’s undoubted popularity in the West End. Indeed there seems to have been very little advertising at all for the series in general.</p>

<p>And that was that. Alan’s only screenplay filmed, broadcast and forgotten. Except, of course, noting is ever truly forgotten or wasted. In this case, the idea of a waiter’s eye view of an evening was something that Alan reworked into one of his plays later that same year, confident that no-one would recognise he had used the same device on television.</p>

<p>“Later I was to re-use the idea of the camera following the waiter,’ notes Alan, ” translating this into stage terms in my short play&nbsp;<em>Between Mouthfuls</em>&nbsp;as part of <em><strong><a href="/plays/confusions">Confusions</a></strong></em>.”</p>

<p>One of the most enjoyable and technically challenging of the five plays which comprise <em>Confusions</em> (1974), <em>Between Mouthfuls</em> sees a waiter serving two tables with the audience hearing only what the waiter hears as he moves back and forth between the diners.</p>

<p>Unlike <em>Service Not Included</em>, <em>Between Mouthfuls</em> has been seen by a huge amount of people over the past five decades given that <em>Confusions</em> is one of Alan’s most popular and revived works.</p>

<p>That wasn’t quite the end of <em>Service Not Included</em> though. Having rediscovered an original shooting script for the production, Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist and theatre historian, Simon Murgatroyd, led a read-through of the screenplay during an event dedicated to the playwright in Scarborough in 2011. Participants on the course took on the roles and gave the play its first ‘performance’ since 1974.</p>

<p>The shooting script is now held in the Ayckbourn Archive at the <strong><a href="http://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a></strong> at the University of York, whilst the actual programme is held in archive by the BBC. Likely to never see the light of day again.</p>

<p>Not that Alan Ayckbourn minds at all. Best left forgotten.</p>
