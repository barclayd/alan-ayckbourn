<p>Here on the Ayckbourn Blog, we’re celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in July 1955.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn was a vital and significant presence at <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> and these articles celebrate the plays he wrote for the company between 1959 and 1976. They offer an in-depth look at the creation of and background to some of the playwright’s most famous – and most obscure – works.</p>

<p>In the previous article, we looked at his eighth play – <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-sparrow">The Sparrow</a></strong></em> – and we now come to 1969 and the second of his huge West End hits, <strong><em><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves">How The How Other Half Loves</a></em></strong>.</p>

<p><strong>How The Other Half Loves (1969)</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p><em>The Sparrow</em> had been something of a disappointment for Alan Ayckbourn. Premiered in 1967 at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, it had run for just three weeks and was, essentially, never heard of again.</p>

<p>Part of the reason for this was the producer Peter Bridge had optioned the play for the West End and then just sat on it, unwilling apparently to seriously consider producing it. As a result, Alan could do little with the successor to the play which had launched him to fame.</p>

<p><em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></strong></em> had opened earlier to tremendous success the same year in the West End and had made Alan famous and wealthy very quickly. But therein lay the problem. Bridge wanted to repeat the success of <em>Relatively Speaking</em> with a play in the same vein. <em>The Sparrow</em> was not it, so Bridge shelved the play and waited for Alan to write the successor he wanted.</p>

<p>The problem was, there was no guarantee this would happen. By 1968, Alan was not attached to any theatre and that year he wrote no plays.</p>

<p>Indeed, one might have never known that he had just achieved such extraordinary success. He was still living in a council flat with his family near Leeds and his full-time job was working at the <strong><a href="/career/bbc-career">BBC</a></strong> as a Radio Drama Producer. His profile as a playwright would have been flat were it not for the fact that <em>Relatively Speaking</em> followed its West End closure in February with a major UK tour followed in the spring by widespread productions by rep companies throughout the UK.</p>

<p>This was almost a liminal moment with Alan stuck between two possible futures, continuing his work at the BBC or returning to the theatre. Throughout 1968, he concentrated on his work at the BBC and his only theatre-related work was directing an adaptation of <em><strong><a href="/career/directing-career/the-wizard-of-oz">The Wizard of Oz</a></strong></em> for children that summer at York Theatre Royal.</p>

<p>Back in Scarborough, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre – having re-opened as a professional venture in 1967 without its founder, Stephen Joseph – was operating purely for summer seasons with a seasonally appointed Director of Productions. In late 1968, the Scarborough Theatre Trust board approached Alan about taking on the role for 1969 as well as writing a play for the season. He agreed, presumably with the blessing of his boss at the BBC, Alfred Bradley, who was also on the board of the trust.</p>

<p>It was a decision which would have an extraordinary impact on both Alan and the theatre’s future. The company was not in great shape – in fact, there is an argument it was largely being sustained by the royalties it was receiving from <em>Relatively Speaking</em>. It could not even afford to pay Alan for his work as Director of Productions and all that could be offered was accommodation for the season.</p>

<p>That this came about is largely due to good timing and good fortune. Without the financial success of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, Alan would not have been able to afford to take on the role. Equally, it could only have come to fruition for the fact that Alfred Bradley turned a blind eye to what was happening with Alan’s work that summer at the BBC.</p>

<p>It’s always been assumed that Alan split his time between Leeds and Scarborough during the summer of 1969. In 2026, he revealed this wasn’t the case. He spent practically all of the summer in Scarborough with his secretary at the BBC forwarding calls to the theatre. He was absent without leave essentially and admitted that part of the reason he left the BBC in 1970 was guilt at having taken such liberties the previous summer.</p>

<p>Rehearsals for the summer season began on 16 June 1969 with the first production, <strong><em><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-2/page-108/page-146/page-262/page347.html">The Dynamic Death-Defying Leap of Timothy Saturn the Great</a></em></strong> by Peter Hawkins, opening on 23 June, directed by Alan. He would then direct <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-2/page-108/page-146/page-263/page348.html">A Little Stiff Built Chap</a></strong></em> by Leonard Barras, which opened on 3 July.</p>

<p>There were four plays scheduled for the season with Alan directing three of them – Pamela Craig directed the fourth, <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-2/page-108/page-146/page-264/page349.html">Ask Me Tomorrow</a></strong></em> by Stan Barstow and Alfred Bradley, which opened on 26 June. The final play of the season was Alan Ayckbourn’s unwritten and untitled ninth play, <em>How The Other Half Loves</em>.</p>

<p>When the season opened on 23 June, the theatre still did not have a title for the play and it would only be officially announced in The Stage newspaper on 3 July. As for writing the play, Alan has noted he began work on it no later than a week before rehearsals began. The earliest he was likely to begin was 4 July – as he was rehearsing <em>A Little Stiff Built Chap</em> prior to that – and given rehearsals were generally for one-and-a-half to two-and-a-half weeks at this time, they probably began on 14 or 21 July. In all probability, Alan began writing <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> circa 7 July at the earliest or 14 July at the latest. </p>

<p>However, as he has also frequently explained, he was now getting to the point in his writing career where he would be planning plays for several months in his head before writing them down in a white heat of several days at the latest possible deadline – largely to motivate him to get the play finished!</p>

<p>The play itself was unusual in that its central – and ground-breaking – premise was significantly informed by Alan’s own experiences living in Leeds.</p>

<p>“My secretary had just got married and wanted a flat, and I said ‘There’s one going two doors down’. So she got that one. Of course the flats were identically designed and unless you were some weird eccentric you had to put your furniture in a certain way – your television there because of the plug in the wall there, and the dining hatch was there. Often I’d go for a drink with her and her husband and one would say ‘I wonder which flat we’re in’. And I started to think that if you actually ripped the lid off those boxes and looked in, you’d see people treading these identical little patterns, wearing out the same bit of carpet. I was also thinking ‘I don’t think I can write a full-length play this year. I’m a bit exhausted’. So I thought ‘I’ll try writing two at once. Let’s be clever’. So I got this family going this way, and this the other, and eventually they tied up.”</p>

<p>This led to the idea of a composite set with two spaces overlaid each other allowing action to occur simultaneously in two different places. To this, he then added the memorable twist for one scene of having one couple simultaneously attend two events at two different locations on two different days. It is widely credited this was a theatrical innovation by Alan, never having been attempted before.</p>

<p>Other influences in shaping the play, according to Alan, were: “being drawn into the comet’s tail of somebody else’s breaking marriage”, being “fascinated by the uses of distortion of time and space; especially the comic potentialities” and a desire to “write a play which highlighted different and contrasting social lifestyles.”&nbsp;He also suggested in an interview for the Broadway premiere of&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em>, the character of Teresa was inspired by his then wife Christine Roland.</p>

<p>The play was written for a company of six, all drawn from the rep company Alan had chosen for the season. It consisted of Elizabeth Ashton, Colin Edwynn, Jeremy Franklin, Brian Miller, Elsabeth Sladen and Stephanie Turner with Alan directing the play. This would be the first of many rep companies Alan would form at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre and its subsequent homes with none of the company having worked in Scarborough before; several of them had worked with Alan at the BBC though.</p>

<p>The play opened on 31 July 1969 at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, performed in-the-round and the next day, the Scarborough Evening News declared the play “hilarious.” In the same review, Alan was quoted with an eye to pulling in fans of its famed predecessor.</p>

<p>“I suppose it’s inevitable that comparisons will be drawn between this and&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>. In fact, it is an exploration of the same sort of theme, but with a few decided differences.”</p>

<p>Other reviews of the play are scarce, but in correspondence Alan noted: “Reviews have been pretty good – Guardian (aside from a rather snide sentence), Yorkshire Post, Express and the locals.” The premiere was also attended by Alan’s London producer Peter Bridge who felt it had “all the makings of another&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>“; although later actions would betray his discomfort with certain aspects of the play.</p>

<p>That discomfort is one that has largely been ignored over the subsequent decades; the play was not only conceived and written for theatre-in-the-round, but actually works far more successfully in-the-round than the proscenium arch where the vast majority of productions have been staged.<br><br>Bridge’s reaction to the play was largely predicted by Alan’s agent&nbsp;<strong><a href="/life/influences/ramsay-margaret">Margaret Ramsay</a></strong>&nbsp;– better known as Peggy – after she had read the script. She liked the play but wondered whether it was suitable for the proscenium arch and, as a result, whether Peter Bridge would be really interested in the piece. In hindsight, this was perhaps the first indication that Peter Bridge’s tenure as Alan’s producer was not going to be a long term affair. In fact, it would unravel throughout the course of&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em>&nbsp;and it was the final Ayckbourn play he would produce.</p>

<p><em>How The Other Half Loves</em> was a huge success for Scarborough and, essentially, marked the start of a new phase of Alan Ayckbourn’s career. He was now essentially embedded with the Scarborough company and in 1970 would resign from the BBC to again accept the role of Director of Productions for the 1970 summer season in Scarborough. Two years later, he would be offered the full-time position of Artistic Director of the company, which he would hold until 2009.</p>

<p>The production also marked the end of a previous career, which he had not thought to resume…</p>

<p>Soon after the play opened and Alan had returned to Leeds, he received a phone call from his stage manager. Jeremy Franklin, the actor playing Frank Foster, had slipped a disc and was in Scarborough Hospital in traction for two weeks. Alan rushed back to Scarborough and took to the stage for that evening’s performance, ironically as the author, having to read from the book. All seemed fine until, alarmingly, as the play reached its climax Alan discovered his script was missing pages.</p>

<p>“I distinguished myself… by actually losing several pages during the action and having to ad-lib a scene, My fellow actors confronted by the sight of the author-director in full-flow, spouting fresh dialogue, stood uncertainly about convinced that these must be new re-writes about which they hadn’t been told. I later rushed off, jotted it down and it’s in there somewhere to this day.”<br><br>The next day, Alan learnt the part afresh and performed it for the remaining three performances that week. Fortunately, the play was in repertory and was not due to return for another two weeks, by which point Jeremy Franklin had recovered and returned to the role. It was only four performances, but five years after he had officially retired from acting,&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em> proved to be Alan’s ultimate on-stage acting swan-song.</p>

<p>The success of <em>How The Other Half Loves </em>in Scarborough did not guarantee its future. Incredible as it may be to consider, given this is one of the most popular and perfumed Ayckbourn works, there initially was no commitment by Peter Bridge to take it any further.</p>

<p>By the time the play closed at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in September 1969, Peter Bridge had not optioned the play and seemed very wary of it. Given that Bridge had previously optioned <em>The Sparrow</em> and, two years on, there was no movement on it at all, Alan was pessimistic that <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> was going to be consigned to history too. Alan wrote to Bridge that month to express concern.</p>

<p>“People I know reckon it could be a winner and I’m very anxious it shouldn’t go the way of&nbsp;<em>The Sparrow</em>,&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/standing-room-only">Standing Room Only</a></em></strong>&nbsp;or even&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="http://mrhwatnot.alanayckbourn.net">Mr Whatnot</a></strong></em>!”</p>

<p>In an attempt to mollify Alan, Bridge signed a contract but was then ominously silent about his intentions, leading Alan to despair in November that he could “only assume that he’s [Bridge] cooled off on this one as well…. I must say I find the ecstatic almost festive air of congratulations which surrounded&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>&nbsp;in bewildering contrast to the total disinterest for the latest one.”</p>

<p>It’s odd to think that a play so closely associated for more than six decades with Alan was not viewed as a potential success. It later transpired, the problem was Bridge could not work out how it would transfer successfully into the proscenium. It took a clever designer  in Alan Tagg to solve it but the damage had now been done to Alan and Bridge’s relationship.</p>

<p>By the time <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> stormed to success in the West End in 1970, Peter Bridge’s days as Alan’s London producer were rapidly coming to a conclusion. His lack of faith in Alan’s work or vision in the potential of How The Other Half Loves never forgotten by either Alan or Margaret Ramsay even once the royalties began pouring in.</p>

<p>For <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> though would go on to phenomenal success, eclipsing <em>Relatively Speaking </em>in the West End and truly cementing Alan’s reputation as one of the hottest young playwriting talents in the country.</p>
