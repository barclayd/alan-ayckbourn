<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1962 and Stephen Joseph’s decision to open a theatre-in-the-round venue in Stoke-on-Trent, which had significant implications for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p><strong>1962: The Victoria Theatre</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>1962 saw a development which has significant repercussions for Stephen Joseph’s now eight year old Scarborough company based at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>Stephen had long had the ambition to open a purpose-built theatre-in-the-round venue and had, essentially, been using the Scarborough company as a testing ground for its viability; indeed, the company’s touring policy was formulated on the grounds of visiting towns without civic theatres in the hope this ‘new’, affordable form of theatre might lead to an offer of a permanent home for the company.</p>

<p>It should be emphasised, Stephen had no long-term commitment to Scarborough. As noted previously, he had only come to choose the town by chance for his pioneering theatre project. He was more than ready to move to a new home if better opportunities presented themselves.</p>

<p>And, in 1962, that seemed the case when Stephen had the opportunity to open a year-round theatre-in-the-round venue in in Stoke-on-Trent with the <strong><a href="/career/victoria-theatre">Victoria Theatre</a></strong>; Scarborough was home to the UK’s first professional theatre-in-the-round company but it was limited to summer seasons and a short winter season. It wasn’t based in a permanent theatre and the company was seasonal.</p>

<p>In 1961, Scarborough Council had actually taken a vote as to whether to build a theatre-in-the-round civic theatre in the town but it had been narrowly defeated and Stephen increasingly viewed Scarborough as having no long term potential. Hence, when the possibility of an opportunity arose elsewhere, he took it.</p>

<p>For an overview of the Victoria Theatre and how it developed, here’s Stephen Joseph’s own account from his book <em>Theatre in the Round</em> – the implications of this development in relation to Scarborough are explored afterwards.</p>

<p>“Proposals for a permanent theatre in the round had been discussed for several years in Scarborough, but it seemed unlikely in a town where the main industry was entertainment that anything approaching civic support could ever be given to a theatre. Various improvements were made to the concert room so that it could more readily serve our purpose. But the concert room did not belong to us, and we could hardly expect it ever to be more than a makeshift theatre.</p>

<p>“Our visits to the Municipal Hall in Newcastle-under-Lyme carried a real hope from the start that a permanent theatre might be established here… The premises we finally found were just over the border that separates Newcastle from Stoke…. Our audience had to come from a wide area and the theatre would have to serve the whole of the Potteries, indeed the whole of North Staffordshire, and from this point of view the position carried no disadvantages.</p>

<p>“The Victoria Theatre had been built as a small cinema, serving an artisan residential locality, on the main road between Stoke and Newcastle. The site unfortunately included no car parking facilities, and the frontage could never be anything but modest. But in many ways the building itself seemed ideal for conversion. Several plans were drawn up. We had only a few pounds available and a few promises.</p>

<p>“At worst we could simply clear away the debris and set up our portable rostrum units as soon as the Scarborough season had finished. But generous help came from William Elmhirst and Miss Margaret Rawlings; Granada Television offered us surplus seating from their cinemas; ABC Television presented us with a cheque for £700; and the Gulbenkian Foundation paid for much-needed decoration in the foyer. We were able to embark on a fairly substantial scheme of alterations. Our architect, Peter Fisher, took full charge of the conversion while the summer season kept us at Scarborough. He drew up plans for permanent seating rows, the construction of dressing rooms, an extensive control room and arrangements to put at any rate some spotlights in the roof void. The work had to be carefully discussed and detailed if it were to be completed in time for the Scarborough company to move in…</p>

<p>“The Victoria Theatre opened on 9 October 1962 with a performance of <em>The Birds and the</em> <em>Wellwishers</em>, a play by William Norfolk that had been among the new productions at Scarborough. Directed by Peter Cheeseman, the play gave good comic opportunities to Alan Ayckbourn as an earnest do-it-yourself clerk and to an adenoidal Elizabeth Bell as a romantically minded telephonist. The author had written the play with a fine sense of humour that appealed strongly to young people. In some ways it was a good play to open with…</p>

<p>“Peter Cheeseman assumed the job of manager, in charge of the Victoria Theatre, with a free hand and the responsibility to get the thing going. No easy task. If in an area as highly populated as the Potteries there is no theatre and if, further, a few years ago there used to be several theatres, it is not a rash conclusion that the people don’t want a theatre and know they don’t want one. Obviously the situation is not as simple as that. Perhaps the theatres were too big, or had the wrong policy, or were in one way or another out of date or otherwise unsuitable. But the Victoria Theatre, no matter how much it tried to avoid mistakes could not escape the disadvantages of its situation, its lack of car-parking facilities, the sheer simplicity (to use a polite word) of its conversion, and above all the known fact that a theatre is not for the likes of us (as it were). But Cheeseman set about the task of winning an audience with huge enthusiasm and bristling energy. Results so far: a young audience, growing attendance figures and some interesting successes – notably Marlowe’s <em>The Jew of Malta</em>, Brian Way’s <em>Pinocchio</em> and the theatre’s own musical entertainment called <em>The Jolly Potters</em>.”</p>

<p><em>Stephen Joseph (Theatre in the Round, Barrie &amp; Rockliffe, 1967)</em></p>

<p>So what did this mean for Scarborough? Well, much of that answer can be ascertained from Stephen’s writing. The summer season at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre closed on 15 September and, essentially, the entire Scarborough company transferred en masse to Stoke-on-Trent with the summer’s repertoire.</p>

<p>The immediate consequence of this was Scarborough’s winter season and its adjacent winter tour were obviously cancelled as Stephen was both too busy setting up the Victoria Theatre and there was no company available to build on. The entire winter season would have had to be started from scratch – which there was neither time nor money to do.</p>

<p>As noted, Alan Ayckbourn left Scarborough with the company – and, essentially, for the next two years was deputy to Peter Cheeseman working as writer, director and actor at the Victoria. He would not return to Scarborough until 1969; between 1962 and 1968, Alan’s only contribution to Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was writing two plays for the company – <em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Meet My Father</a></strong></em> in 1965 and <strong><em><a href="/plays/the-sparrow">The Sparrow</a></em></strong> in 1967, the latter he also directed.</p>

<p>But there was far more significant and potential disruptive issue. Stephen’s company, <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-40/">Studio Theatre Ltd</a></strong>, had transferred lock, stock and barrel to Stoke-on-Trent. And the Arts Council funding which it received followed it.</p>

<p>The Arts Council would not spilt its funding nor offer separate funding for Scarborough. This news, combined with a slash of the annual Arts Council grant, led the board of Studio Theatre Ltd to decide to cut Scarborough adrift and retain all its financial resources for Stoke-on-Trent. Perfectly understandable, but it put Scarborough in a precarious situation.</p>

<p>A new company had to be formed for Scarborough – <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-40/">Theatre in the Round Ltd</a></strong> – and a decision made as to how and whether Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre could continue. Stephen still had, despite all the challenges it presented, some loyalty to Scarborough and hoped theatre-in-the-round could continue there in some form.</p>

<p>But the prognosis wasn’t good. Budgets would have to be slashed and the winter seasons and touring would have to end. Neither would resume until 1974, 13 years after the previous winter season had taken place.</p>

<p>Stephen Joseph now had his wish of a permanent theatre-in-the-round venue, but – as 1963 approached – the future of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre hung in the balance.</p>
