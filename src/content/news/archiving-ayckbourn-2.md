<p><em>Archiving Ayckbourn is a regular feature in which Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist, Simon Murgatroyd, delves into the archives to highlight items of interest relating to the playwright and theatre in the round.</em></p>

<p><strong>Change of Image</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>The keen-eyed Ayckbourn fans among you may have noticed something recently with regards to Alan Ayckbourn’s new play <em><strong><a href="/plays/show-and-tell">Show &amp; Tell</a></strong></em>. The poster has changed.</p>

<p>The initial poster – launched in late 2023 – featured an empty seat, almost as though it was waiting for someone… And that someone has now arrived in the shape of Sir Alan Ayckbourn – albeit on a different seat and in a different room.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/sat-w700.jpg" alt="" width="700" height="520" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p>It made me think about other posters that have changed during the advertising run of Sir Alan’s productions over the years and how prevalent it is. It’s actually more common than I imagined and I’ve found at least 20 world or West End premiere productions which altered their posters.</p>

<p>Now, in most cases, we don’t know why there was a change. It might be it’s a genuine alternative poster running in tandem with the original, it may have replaced the original for some reason such as a change of cast or there may have been an error or issue with the original poster.</p>

<p>But they’re all quite interesting and to illustrate this, here’s a selection of posters and how they altered for the same production. Perhaps have a say on which you prefer.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/aps-w659.jpg" alt="" width="659" height="490" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p>The earliest example I’ve found is the poster for the 1972 West End premiere production of <strong><em><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></em></strong> (1972). The most common image is that of the partridge in the pear tree – tying in with the Christmas theme and the end of the second act. It gets changed to, arguably, a more generic if possibly more striking image during the course of its long run. Which could be the reason why it changed – the play was the longest running production of any Ayckbourn play in the West End and perhaps it was felt a refresh was needed.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/confusions-w700.jpg" alt="" width="700" height="490" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p>This example comes from the world premiere production of <em><strong><a href="/plays/confusions">Confusions</a></strong></em> (1974) at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, in autumn 1974 which was revived the following summer. The reason for the change is more obvious here. The programme format changed after the <em>Confusions</em> premiere and went from a glossy colour bi-fold to a heavy stock mono eight page programme. The change was thus a necessity and, by the way, features Stephen Mallatratt as the scout leader.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/bed-w700.jpeg" alt="" width="700" height="517" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p>More perplexing is the National Theatre’s 1977 London premiere of <em><strong><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></strong></em> (1975) which goes from a stylised, quite busy image to an iconic Ayckbourn poster image of just a bed; it would change again for the West End transfer to a cartoon-based image. I don’t know the reason why it changed, but it’s a good change; the revised poster has featured in exhibitions and books about the National Theatre. The only thing unchanged is the font, which I personally feel is just a fabulous font!</p>

<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/hence-w700.jpg" alt="" width="700" height="481" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>

<p>The next one is more obvious as, six months in, there was a change of cast in the 1988 West End premiere of <strong><em><a href="/plays/henceforward">Henceforward…</a></em></strong> (1987). Ian McKellen and Jane Asher’s roles were taken over by Martin Jarvis and Sara Crowe. What’s unusual is although both are patently similar, the artist changes. The original caricature of Jane Asher (left) is by the famed artist Gerald Scarfe – her husband – but Sara Crowe’s caricature is patently not Scarfe’s work (and is, in fact, uncredited). Presumably, either the producers didn’t want to use Scarfe for the alteration or Scarfe didn’t want to do it.</p>

<p>The next two examples are from the world premieres of Alan Ayckbourn’s plays at the Stephen Joseph Theatre, Scarborough. We have <em><strong><a href="/plays/drowning-on-dry-land">Drowning On Dry Land</a></strong></em> (2004) and <em><strong><a href="/plays/surprises">Surprises</a></strong></em> (2012) with the poster used in Scarborough and the poster then used for the tour of the exact same production, which commenced soon after the premiere run had finished. The differences are quite significant and it’s interesting to wonder why? There is no obvious reason such as change of cast. My preference is for the simpler pieces originally used. What do you think?</p>

<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/dodl-w700.jpg" alt="" width="700" height="491" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/surprises-w700.jpeg" alt="" width="700" height="502" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p>Finally – and a lot more recently – is Alan Ayckbourn’s 2019 revival of <em><strong><a href="/plays/seasons-greetings">Season’s Greetings</a></strong></em> (1980) at the Stephen Joseph Theatre. Another striking change produced during the actual run. Interestingly, Alan was unhappy with the group shot but it was still used on the programmes, whilst the updated – and very striking – image of the ‘pudding bomb’ was used on flyers and posters. I’ve always felt the latter seemed far more effective as a poster image than the former.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/sg-w700.png" alt="" width="700" height="495" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p>That’s just a selection of changes. I’ve also found different posters used for the world premieres of <strong><em><a href="/plays/snake-in-the-grass">Snake In The Grass</a></em></strong> (2002), <em><strong><a href="/plays/sugar-daddies">Sugar Daddies</a></strong></em> (2003) and <em><strong><a href="/plays/private-fears-in-public-places">Private Fears in Public Places</a></strong></em> (2004) among others and I’m sure there’s a lot more to be found. </p>
