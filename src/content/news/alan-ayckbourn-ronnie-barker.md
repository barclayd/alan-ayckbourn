<p>It is not particularly well known that Alan Ayckbourn and the famed British comedian Ronnie Barker worked together during the 1960s. Yet not only did the pair work together but it also marked one of only two forays into television-writing for Alan Ayckbourn. </p>

<p>This is the story of Ayckbourn &amp; Barker.</p>

<p>Conventional wisdom has it that Alan Ayckbourn’s initial West End triumph, <em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></em>, was his first foray into the West End.</p>

<p>It was not. His first transfer was such a flop, however, that it is frequently forgotten or ignored. Alan’s first West End production was actually <em><a href="/plays/mr-whatnot">Mr Whatnot</a></em>, which opened at the New Arts Theatre on 6 August 1964 before closing prematurely on 22 August 1964; this is also the shortest West End run of any Ayckbourn play in eluding the more notorious Ayckbourn / Andrew Lloyd Webber musical <em><a href="/plays/jeeves">Jeeves</a></em>.</p>

<p>One of the only positive aspects to emerge from the failure of  <em>Mr Whatnot</em> in the West End was that it introduced Alan to the comedian Ronnie Barker, who was soon to carve a path on television which would establish him as one of the UK’s most popular, successful and well-remembered comedians.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/1964-mr-whatnot-flyer-02-w600.jpeg" alt="" width="600" height="600" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>A detail from a flyer for the West End premiere of Mr Whatnot starring Ronnie Barker.<br>(© Peter Bridge Productions – held in The Borthwick Institute for Archives)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Barker played the role of Lord Slingsby-Craddock in <em>Mr Whatnot</em> and, unbeknownst to Alan, the character would have a significant impact and influence on the comedian.</p>

<p>At the time though, none of this was consolation to the young playwright who, having been mauled by the critics for <em>Mr Whatnot</em> and with the bitter taste of failure still fresh, made a decision to leave the world of theatre. He found himself instead working for the <a href="/career/bbc-career">BBC</a> as a radio drama producer, based in Leeds. Despite his initial intentions, Alan would still write and direct the occasional play, but his full time employment between 1965 and 1970 was with the BBC.</p>

<p>Concurrent with this, Ronnie Barker’s career was very much taking off. Although he had been consistently employed as an actor on radio and television since 1956, his big break came in 1964 when he appeared in the acclaimed satirical sketch show, <em>The Frost Report</em>. This led to Barker gaining his first own show, ‘s first <em>The Ronnie Barker Playhouse</em> in 1968.</p>

<p>This was followed in 1969 by <em>Hark At Barker</em> and with it, Alan and Barker’s path crossed once more.</p>

<p><em>Hark At Barker</em> was a sketch show based around one of Barker’s most famous creations Lord Rustless, an incomprehensible, cigar-smoking and sex-obsessed member of the aristocracy who lived at the stately home Chrome Hall. The character was so popular it would recur in the BBC series <em>His Lordship Entertains</em> and essentially re-appeared in <em>Futtock’s End</em> and in several sketches for the popular comedy series <em>The Two Ronnies</em>.</p>

<p>Lord Rustless was, according to Barker himself (see below), unashamedly based on Lord Slingsby-Craddock from <em>Mr Whatnot</em>. Bearing this in mind, it is no surprise that when it came to finding writers for <em>Hark At Barker</em>, the comedian approached Alan to write material for the show relating to Rustless’s scenes.</p>

<p>However, Alan was under contract at the BBC and not allowed to write for other organisations – and definitely not the BBC’s competitor ITV! However, he clandestinely agreed to write material for the show under the pseudonym of Peter Caulfield. The show had a similar format each week with Barker first introducing the episode as a continuity announcer before switching to Lord Rustless at Chrome Hall, who would pontificate on a chosen subject for the week frequently interspaced with comedy sketches.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/screenshot-2020-03-16-at-13-36-58-w600.png" alt="" width="600" height="600" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Detail from the first page of the first script for Hark at Barker crediting the main writer as Peter Caulfield (Alan Ayckbourn).<br>(© London Weekend Television)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Alan would mainly write the links between the sketches which featured Rustless and his household. Surviving shooting scripts from the television series credit Peter Caulfield as the show’s main writer with notable comedy writers such as Eric Idle, Graeme Garden, Bill Odie and Barker himself (writing under the pseudonym Gerald Wiley) contributing to the sketches. Without a doubt though, each show is built around Rustless and his household.</p>

<p><em>Hark At Barker</em> ran for two series between 1969 and 1970 with Alan writing for every episode of both series. With the exception of his only screenplay, <em><a href="/plays/service-not-included">Service Not Included</a></em>, this remains to date the only time that Alan has written specifically for television.</p>

<p>In early 1970, Alan left the BBC to concentrate on his playwriting and theatre directing careers. In 1972, he would become the <a href="/career/artistic-director">Artistic Director</a> of the <a href="http://careers.alanayckbourn.net/styled-6/styled-7/">Library Theatre</a> (now, in its third home, known as the <a href="http://careers.alanayckbourn.net/styled-6/styled-12/">Stephen Joseph Theatre</a>) and be associated with the company ever since.</p>

<p>But for a brief time, Alan Ayckbourn was a successful radio producer and television writer and working with Ronnie Barker. Not at all, what we associate with the playwright today!</p>

<p><strong>Ronnie Barker discusses Alan Ayckbourn’s involvement with <em>Hark At Barker</em></strong></p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/hark-at-barker-1970-rustless-image-w346.jpeg" alt="" width="346" height="350" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Ronnie Barker as Lord Rustless<br>(© London Weekend Television)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>” That [<em>Mr Whatnot</em>] was done at the Arts Theatre. It was about a piano-tuner who never spoke. I don’t know if he was dumb, he was a young man, Chaplin-esque really, because a lot of it was physical. And everything was done without props. It was a very strange piece, it was lovely. Very funny. Certain props, action props, were left out. We had a tennis match on the stage and there was no ball. It took a lot of rehearsing. I was the umpire and at one point I’d picked up a newspaper and was reading it and Alan, who was directing, said it would be nice if the ball hit that paper. And I said, ‘Well, why doesn’t it split it right down the middle?’ It got a very good laugh. It was very strange and quite surreal. A bit <em>Alice in Wonderland</em> in places – we were having a picnic in the garden and suddenly war broke out, we were attacked by the enemy and we were lobbing buns over the hedge. No props of course.”</p>

<p>Ask Barker if the role of Lord Slingsby-Craddock was a prototype for one of his most enduring creations, Lord Rustless, and he answers the question before it’s finished.</p>

<p>“Yes he was. Absolutely. He was Lord Rustless mark one, definitely. I did a character at Oxford rep and it was a part that was supposed to be done by a woman and Frank Shelley, who was running Oxford rep and was God there, said, ‘No we won’t play it as a woman, because we haven’t any women. Ronnie, you can play it as an old man.’ So I started doing this old chap and it was very successful and it worked very well. That character stayed at the back of my mind and he became Lord Rustless, because I enjoyed playing him so much. He’s also in <em>The Picnic</em> and <em>By the Sea</em>, but he just mutters in those. He’s not called Lord Rustless, no-one’s called anything. But to me he was Rustless. He was one of my favourite characters. When I did <em>Hark at Barker</em> – that was him, albeit with sketches. Alan Ayckbourn wrote all the links for that show but I don’t think he admits it. He called himself Peter Caulfield, but I don’t know whether he would like people to know that was him or not. He liked the character in <em>Mr Whatnot</em>, so he knew what the character was about. Rustless was really giving a lecture to the audience on a subject, such as “communication” or “servants” or something and he would illustrate it with sketches, which enabled me to pay lots of different parts.”</p>

<p><em>Extract from The Authorised Biography Of Ronnie Barker, Bob McCabe, BBC Books, 2004</em></p>
