<p>As visitors to the blog will be aware, we’ve been celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>With the anniversary year in full swing, the blog comes closer to a home with a look at all the plays Alan Ayckbourn wrote for <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> between 1959 and 1976.</p>

<p>Over the coming months, we’ll be looking at each of his Library Theatre plays in-depth, providing some insight and background into the creation of some of the playwright’s most famous – and obscure – works.</p>

<p>Alan’s second play actually premiered in the same year as his debut, 1959. We now move to winter of that year and the second play, <em><strong><a href="/plays/love-after-all">Love After All</a></strong></em>.</p>

<p><strong>Love After All (1959)</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p><em>Love After All</em> has always been one of the great enigmas of Alan Ayckbourn’s playwriting career. Rarely read, never published and never performed professionally since 1960, it was the last of Alan’s plays to be a genuine mystery largely due to the fact it was widely believed not a single copy of the original manuscript had survived past the 1960s.</p>

<p>This mystery was compounded by the fact that within the space of a year there were two very different productions of the play and reminiscences of the two productions often conflicted with each other.</p>

</div>

<p>Which is somewhat ironic given this was a very successful play when it premiered at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, in December 1959 and, certainly in the eyes of the company’s founder Stephen Joseph, cemented Alan’s place as a playwright to be encouraged and developed.</p>

<p>In that he was right, but when fame and fortune came to the playwright in the early 1970s, he – perhaps rashly – decided he wanted to deflect attention away from his early works and any possibility of them being revived. To that end, he began actively attempting to take the early plays out of circulation, particularly with regard to <em>Love After All</em>.</p>

<p>By the late 1970s, the playwright was firmly insisting that no copies of the manuscript were in existence, that they had all been destroyed because he didn’t want anyone reading the play, let alone producing it. This vehement insistence  by Alan is likely to explain why little research was apparently made as to establish whether it was, in fact, a true statement.</p>

<p>It wasn’t.</p>

<p>In 2007, Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist Simon Murgatroyd and the British Library brought to light what is believed to be the sole surviving copy of the original manuscript, held in the Lord Chamberlain’s Plays collection at the British Library. This significant ‘discovery’ helped to clear up many of the mysteries surrounding the play and restored the complete Ayckbourn play canon for the first time in more than 40 years.</p>

<p>However, the full story behind <em>Love After All</em> remains tantalisingly out of reach due to the likelihood that no copies of the script for the second production appear to have survived.</p>

<p>The discovery also threw up an archival mystery. How was a ‘lost’ script actually hiding in plain sight? In context, between 1737 and 1968, every play intended for public performance had to be submitted to the Lord Chamberlain’s Office for examination and licensing with the British Library holding every new script submitted to the office between 1824 and 1968. <em>Love After All</em> was written in 1959 and would have had to have been submitted to the Lord Chamberlain’s Office for approval.</p>

<p>Why, when the British Library was known to hold this collection, did no researchers think to search for <em>Love After All </em>in the collection? The only verified reference to it being consulted at the British Library before 2007 was by Tim Auld whilst researching for his Doctorate in 1996. Other than that, there is little evidence it had ever been consulted since 1970 with none of Alan’s major biographers referring to have either seen or read the play. Yet, it was just waiting to be found as the British Library was the one place Alan couldn’t remove from or destroy his script! Perhaps everyone just took Alan at his word or decided there was no value in so little known a work.</p>

<p>As to the play itself, it was commissioned in the immediate aftermath of Alan’s success with his debut play, <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-square-cat">The Square Cat</a></strong></em>, during the 1959 summer season at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. Like the previous play it was also a joint collaboration between Alan and his wife, Christine, and was attributed to his then writing pseudonym, Roland Allen; at the time Alan was still intent on becoming an actor and wrote primarily to showcase his own acting talents.</p>

</div>

<p>The play is – very – loosely based on the plot of <em>The Barber Of Seville</em> and one has to wonder why Alan ‘borrowed’ a plot (not that there’s any issue there, Shakespeare was borrowing plots left, right and centre!). However, this could have been due entirely to practical reasons and deadlines. He was commissioned to write <em>Love After All</em> no earlier than 9 September 1959 (the end of the summer season) for rehearsals beginning no later than 9 December 1959 – less than three months to come up with an idea, write, submit and correct a script! Perhaps borrowing a plot was the most sensible way to go.</p>

<p>The play was conceived as an Edwardian farce centred around Jim Jones’ attempts to win the hand of the beautiful Angelica, despite the machinations of her miser father Scrimes who intends to marry her off to a member of the local aristocracy. With Alan penciled in to play the hero, there was plenty of opportunity for disguises and multiple characters to showcase his acting range.</p>

<p>However, fate intervened and Alan was called up for a – short-lived – National Service and was not available to take on the lead role. This went instead to Barry Boys; in any case it was probably best he didn’t play the role as according to reports from the time, William Elmhirst apparently stole the show as the somewhat dim-witted, pig-breeding aristocrat, Rupert.</p>

<p>Alan’s National Service is actually worth a brief digression. At the time, the UK still required National Service for all ‘healthy young men’ aged from 17 -21 for 18 months; Alan was 20 years old in 1959 and had managed to avoid the call since 17. Famously, he attempted to fail the initial exam by scoring zero (he mistakenly got two questions right) but the army was well aware someone with ‘A Levels’ was unlikely to do so poorly and so he was enlisted in the Royal Air Force as a clerk.</p>

<p>In January 1960, he was sent to RAF Cardington for eight weeks basic training, his second day involving his medical / fitness examination. It transpired his examining doctor was writing a book about his army experiences and discovered Alan was a fellow writer. During conversation Alan let slip he had a literary agent (he didn’t) and wasn’t enthusiastic about doing his National Service. The Doctor promptly signed him out for his ‘cricket knee’ and two days later Alan was once again a civilian. National Service ended the same year with the last men entering service in November 1960.</p>

<p>Of the play itself, it is a very conventional period farce and has less merit and interest than <em>The Square Cat</em>, which at least itself has early indicators of his path moving forward. <em>Love After All</em>‘s has two singificant innovations for the playwright. Firstly, it’s his first multi-location set with a ground floor living room and first floor bedroom sharing the same set. Secondly, it is the only Ayckbourn play where a character gives an aside to the audience (breaking the in-the-round equivalent of the fourth wall) where the convention hasn’t been established as part of the narrative (i.e. the narrators / lead characters speaking directly to the audience in family plays such as&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/invisible-friends">Invisible Friends</a></em></strong>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/miss-yesterday">Miss Yesterday</a></em></strong>).</p>

<p>The world premiere of <em>Love After All</em> took place on 21 December 1959 at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, and was directed by Clifford Williams; later to become a hugely influential director with the Royal Shakespeare Company. It starred Barry Boys, David Campton, William Elmhirst, Faynia Jeffery and Dona Martyn – aside from Alan, the same company which had performed <em>The Square Cat</em>.</p>

<p>Although only several reviews survive, it appears the play was well-received and was, like <em>The Square Cat</em> before it, another sell-out success for the theatre – (although, in context, it has the shortest production run of any world premiere production of an Ayckbourn play, running from just 21 – 26 December with no production on Christmas Day).</p>

<p>The ‘success’ of the play led to it being revived for the following summer season; no play before this had been produced in two consecutive seasons at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>Prior to that, <em>Love After All</em> was taken on tour first to Newcastle-under-Lyme as part of a short repertory season and then, from 15 March 1960, to Southampton’s Chantry Hall, where it was one of a number of plays performed that week ostensibly to introduce the concept of theatre-in-the-round to a new audience.</p>

<p>The play was revived by Studio Theatre Ltd for the 1960 summer season with a new director, Julian Herington. He disliked several aspects of the play and chose to update it to a modern setting – although the single surviving review incorrectly insists it was still Edwardian – and changed many of the characters’ names. Alan was cast as the lead this time, now called Peter Jones, and gained a good review for his energetic, quick-changing performance.</p>

<p>The production was also a success with audiences and featured a largely new cast of Alan, David Campton, David Glover, Hazel Anne Lee, Anita Robinson and Derrick Gilbert. Shortly afterwards, Alan was commissioned to help David Campton write a piece for Christmas, which later became a solo endeavour, <em><strong><a href="/plays/dads-tale">Dad’s Tale</a></strong></em>, and his first taste of failure as a playwright.</p>

<p>Alan strongly believes this second version of<em> Love After All</em> was far less successful artistically than the original production thanks both to Herington’s alterations and being a far less capable director than Clifford Williams. Notably, this production marked the end of Herington’s career with the company, who was fired after allegedly spending the entire season’s budget on just his productions of <em>Wuthering Heights</em> and <em>Love After All</em>.</p>

<p>Unfortunately, no known manuscripts from the second production are known to have survived, making it difficult to ascertain how substantially the play was changed. Our knowledge of the play is also restricted by the fact that the single copy of the original manuscript of the original production held by the British Library is pre-performance and does not include any alterations or notes that came during the rehearsal and performance process.</p>

<p>Like <em>The Square Cat</em>, Alan has never allowed <em>Love After All</em> to be published or produced since 1960, saying it belongs to a period of his writing when he was still learning his craft. The sole original manuscript is held at the British Library, but the Ayckbourn Archive at the <strong><a href="http://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a></strong> at the University of York now also holds a copy of that manuscript.</p>

<p>But for all its mystery, one thing is certain, <em>Love After All</em> was a bona fide hit for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre and Alan became the first living playwright to have two sell-out successes with the company in the same year. These solid foundations would be essential as his career stumbled in two of his next three plays.</p>

<p><em>Simon Murgatroyd (2025)</em></p>
