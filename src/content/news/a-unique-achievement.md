<p>Over his decades as a regular fixture in London’s West End, Alan Ayckbourn has seen his plays deliver some extraordinary achievements.</p>

<p>The most notable was the then record-breaking achievement of having five plays running simultaneously in the West End in 1975 with <em>Absurd Person Singular</em>, <em>Table Manners</em>, <em>Living Together</em>, <em>Round &amp; Round the Garden</em> and <em>Absent Friends</em>.</p>

<p>But a decade later, there was another notable achievement and the question is, is this unique to Alan Ayckbourn? My theory is yes and I’ve yet to find anything to challenge this. But who knows?</p>

</div>

<p>The achievement was to have separate productions running simultaneously at the National Theatre, in the West End and on the London fringe. It’s such an unlikely coincidence that it feels as though it could well be unique to Alan.</p>

<p>So between 21 May and 13 June 1987, <em>A Small Family Business</em> was running at the National Theatre, <em>Woman In Mind</em> was being performed at the Vaudeville Theatre and Alan’s little-known revue, <em>The Westwoods</em>, was running at the Oxford Arms, Camden.</p>

<p>We can say with certainty that on 30 May 1987, all three productions were running simultaneously that evening. If nothing else, it’s certainly a notable achievement with regard to Alan Ayckbourn’s success in London.</p>

<p>Whilst productions of Alan Ayckbourn’s plays are less prevalent in the West End than they once were, it’s also worth noting that between 1970 and 2000, there were between one and five Ayckbourn productions each year in the West End (including the National Theatre). That’s an extraordinary achievement. Over a period of 31 years, you were essentially guaranteed an Ayckbourn play in the West End.</p>

<p>Again, it’s something which – Shakespeare aside – it seems unlikely to have been achieved by many playwrights.</p>

<p>What’s even more astonishing is that this wasn’t the case of a single play running for an inordinate length of time (such as Agatha Christie’s<em> The Mousetrap</em> or Stephen Mallatratt’s adaptation of <em>The Woman In Black</em>), but due to a body of work. Over the course of those three decades, 33 different Ayckbourn plays were produced in the West End (not including revivals).</p>

<p>We may be two decades since Alan was a permanent fixture in the West End, but it must not be forgotten just how much success he achieved and what an impact he had.</p>
