<p><em>In August 2023, The Ayckbourn Collection was donated to Scarborough Museums and Galleries by Sir Alan and Lady Ayckbourn and the playwright’s Archivist, Simon Murgatroyd.</em></p>

<p><em>It is a collection of approximately 60 significant items drawn from Sir Alan’s Archive and Simon’s personal collection exploring the contribution of Alan Ayckbourn, Stephen Joseph and theatre in the round to Scarborough’s cultural heritage and history.</em></p>

<p><em>Currently, items from the exhibition are on display at Scarborough Art Gallery until early 2024. To mark the exhibition, Simon Murgatroyd – who curated the Collection and facilitated its donation – is taking a weekly look at some of the individual items within it, their significance and why they were chosen.</em></p>

<p><strong>The Fish &amp; Chip Theatre</strong></p>

<p>One of the most important considerations when curating The Ayckbourn Collection was how to represent Stephen Joseph – who brought theatre in the round to Scarborough in 1955 and whom Alan Ayckbourn regards as his most significant influence and mentor.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Stephen Joseph (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>When looking at theatre in the round’s contribution to Scarborough’s cultural heritage, it all comes back to Stephen. Without him, there would be no theatre in the round in the town nor would Alan Ayckbourn have ever become associated with Scarborough nor – arguably – become the theatrical force he did.</p>

<p>Which led me to one of my favourite items in the Collection, Stephen Joseph’s designs for his ‘Fish and Chip Theatre’ because they epitomised what I believe Stephen represented and was attempting to achieve in his theatre work.</p>

<p>The plans consist of two sheets showing the ground floor and gallery of a proposed but never realised theatre dubbed ‘The Fish and Chip Theatre’; it’s not clear whether Stephen actually intended to build the venue or whether it was just to enable discussion about how to move theatres in the UK forward.</p>

<p>The designs were the culmination of Stephen’s experiences in Scarborough, he drew on what he had learnt running Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre for a decade and made it clear ‘Fish and Chip Theatre’ was designed specifically for Scarborough.</p>

<p>So what is it? Let’s turn to Stephen Joseph himself here as he described it in his book <em>Theatre in the Round</em>.</p>

</div>

<p>“[An] exciting theatre that would also be interesting to work in is my Fish and Chip Theatre, originally conceived for Scarborough. The architect’s brief is to build a theatre in the round, accommodating about 400 people… our special requirements include a rectangular stage that, taken as datum level, has rising seating rows on two sides, a low standing room pit on the third side, and a dining room level on the fourth. All around the theatre, at (or above) the level of the back row, there is a viewing bar, arranged so that you can sit for a drink and snack and watch the play at the same time. Kitchens and serveries and bars to be provided, offering a limited range of refreshments, including fish and chips… serve yourself at the counter. All sections of the auditorium should be organically connected to encourage free flow of audience from one place to another, even during the performance. The design should be asymmetrical, and invite movement. The seats should be generously spaced. Proportions so related to human stature that the actors and audience dominate the building and not the other way round.”</p>

<p>So that’s the idea. Why is it significant and what does it tell us about Stephen Joseph though? Firstly, this is about the democratisation of theatre. Stephen was looking for ways to attract as wide an audience as possible into theatres. Here we can see a theatre that caters to a wide variety of theatre experiences, tastes and prices. There’s standing room, there’s seating both normal and cafe style, where you can have fish and chips.</p>

<p>This may seem a bit of a gimmick, but we have to consider the period. During the mid 1960s, Scarborough was still a phenomenally popular tourist resort where people from all over the UK would come for their week’s summer holiday. A big part of the holiday-making experience for people visiting Scarborough was a  fish and chip dinner. It was part of the seaside experience, particularly given the majority of tourists at the time were staying for at least several days.</p>

<p>Stephen’s was just incorporating something he saw as an essential part of the ‘holiday’ experience  into his theatre to make it more attractive to tourists.</p>

<p>Then there’s the innovation. It’s a theatre in the round – Stephen’s passion was for promoting ‘new theatre forms’ such as theatre in the round which Stephen passionately believed was an exciting form of theatre, which not only rejected the predominant and, as he saw it, staid form of proscenium arch, but through its intimacy and immediacy could compete against cinema and the emerging popularity of television.</p>

<p>This can be particularly seen in the lounge / bar on the gallery level. This would be equipped with earphones, so people could watch the production from the bar if they were hard of hearing. But beyond that, there’s also a taste of the future.</p>

<p>Stephen proposed live feeds from the stage to TVs in the lounge / bar so you could watch the show whilst enjoying a drink. This is 1965, technologically it wasn’t possible (or if it was, it would be beyond the budget of a regional theatre and the quality would not be great). Stephen is essentially proposing a live stream of the play. Now Stephen enjoyed technology and undoubtedly knew that what was on the edge of the possible then would become affordable reality relatively quickly. He was offering a vision of what could be.</p>

<p>It might not have been practical for the time, but the ‘Fish and Chip Theatre’ offered points for discussion. How do we democratise theatre and draw more people for more walks of life in, how do we utilise technology to make the experience better or, again, more accessible? </p>

<p>It’s a fascinating insight into Stephen Joseph’s mind and his ambitions for theatre. It must not be forgotten that, broadly speaking, Stephen Joseph is credited as being an influence on practically every theatre in the round/ adaptable theatre built in the UK during the 1960s and 1970s – which all then comes back to his experiences in Scarborough from 1955 until his death in 1967; Scarborough is thus in the DNA of many other theatres outside the town that were inspired and influenced by Stephen Joseph’s ideas and ambitions.</p>

<p>The ‘Fish and Chip Theatre’ plans are now on display as part of The Ayckbourn Collection at Scarborough Art Gallery until early 2024.</p>
