<p><em>The blog continues it’s year-by-year exploration of Alan Ayckbourn’s home theatres the Stephen Joseph Theatre, looking at significant events, people and plays.</em></p>

<p><em>We move today onto 1961 – which marked another key turning point in the career of Alan Ayckbourn as he turned his hand to the second of the professions we most associate him with.</em></p>

<p><strong>1961: Debut Of A Director</strong></p>

<p>In 1961, another significant step was taken by Alan Ayckbourn in his theatrical career, which would also have a lasting impact on Scarborough’s Library Theatre.</p>

<p>Having joined the company as an assistant stage manager and <a href="/career/actor">actor</a> in 1955, Alan had gone on to <a href="/career/playwright">playwriting</a> in 1959 and two years later, would add <a href="/career/directing-career">directing</a> to his string of achievements.</p>

<p>The company’s founder and Artistic Director <a href="/life/stephen-joseph">Stephen Joseph</a> encouraged Alan to take the steps into writing and directing, with Alan himself acknowledging that Stephen saw his long-term talents lay somewhere other than acting.</p>

<p><em>“Stephen Joseph gradually encouraged me to direct in order to put a spoke in the wheels of my acting career, but that is a poisoned chalice for an actor; if they get the taste for directing, they slowly tire of acting because directing is global and you have a view of the entire production.”</em></p>

<p>His first production as a director was Patrick Hamilton’s classic Victorian thriller <em><a href="/career/directing-career/gaslight">Gaslight</a></em> , which opened at the Library Theatre on 29 June 1961. As Alan’s programme note shows, the playwright was already plunging into the deep end with an attempt to prove that a very traditionally proscenium arch play could work effectively in-the-round.</p>

<p><em>“Certainly, at first sight, a Victorian thriller did not seem ideal ‘round’ material…. It took only one or two rehearsals to dispel this illusion.”</em></p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/1961-gaslight-interior-w686.jpg" alt="" width="686" height="798" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Alan Ayckbourn’s programme note for his directorial debut, Gaslight<br>(© Haydonning Ltd)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>That he attempted such a challenging play at the outset is instructive as it demonstrates how comfortable Alan Ayckbourn was working in-the-round. By 1961, Alan had been acting in-the-round for five years and it is considered he was one of the most experienced in-the-round actors in the UK at that time, having performed in around 25 different productions.</p>

<p>Most notably, the play was reviewed by the influential <em>The Stage &amp; Television Today</em> newspaper which wrote “the production, by Alan Ayckbourn, is polished, building with authority from its quiet opening to the chilling of spines as the tension mounts towards hysteria.”</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Hazel Burt in Alan Ayckbourn’s directorial debut, Gaslight<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>That this was a promising start to a directorial career is obvious, but few would have realised this was to launch a career that – as of writing – has seen Alan Ayckbourn direct more than 400 productions in the UK, USA and Europe.</p>

<p>This move into directing would also have a huge effect on Alan Ayckbourn’s professional career as, although primarily thought of as a writer, he considers himself first and foremost a director; for most of his professional life, Alan has spent far more time in the rehearsal room than in front of a keyboard.</p>

<p><em>“I always consider myself as a director who writes rather than a writer who directs, because directing takes up so much of my time.”</em></p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Stanley Page, Hamel Burt &amp; David Jarrett in Gaslight<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>That Alan’s talents and contributions to the company were beginning to be recognised can be seen from another interesting item from the archive published in the Yorkshire Post on 10 June 1961 and held in The Bob Watson Archive at the SJT.</p>

<p>Written by theatre critic Desmond Pratt, it looks at the impact of theatre-in-the-round since Stephen Joseph opened the Library Theatre in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/1961-06-10-yorkshire-post-library-theatre-w911.jpg" alt="" width="910" height="1024" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>The article about the Library Theatre from the Yorkshire Post (10 June 1961)<br>(© JPI Media Publishing)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Somewhat contradictorily – as can be seen from the accompanying article – whilst extolling the virtues of in-the-round and its future, the author also sees it merely as a platform for more traditional and commercial theatre ventures.</p>

<p>Within the article Alan Ayckbourn’s place and rise within the company is mentioned with a brief appraisal of his progress.</p>

<p>“Alan Ayckbourn, for instance, is only 22, but he started work in the theatre on leaving school and joined Studio Theatre four years ago as an assistant stage manager with a happy knowledge of lighting and sound effects. He has developed into a most sensitive actor, has become a playwright whose works have been staged by Mr Joseph and is now to produce for the first time.”</p>

<p>I’m not sure Alan even would know what a ‘happy knowledge’ of something is! But this is then followed by advice as to where Alan Ayckbourn should be heading with his career.</p>

<p>“It will be a loss to theatre-in-the-round but Mr Ayckbourn, for instance, is ready for the commercial theatre and should progress to the proscenium arch. The extra rewards will be both his and this of the larger audiences to which he can appeal.”</p>

<p>Thank goodness Alan avoided Mr Pratt’s advice and chose not to ‘progress’ into proscenium arch nor to abandon regional theatre; both being such crucial aspects of his six decade career in theatre.</p>

<p>What all this demonstrates is all the pieces were now in place for Alan: a playwright and director beginning to attract attention.</p>

<p>Abandoning his acting career in 1964, Alan would soon concentrate on the two careers for which he is now world-renowned for and which would subsequently have had a huge impact on British theatre.</p>

<p>And for the Library Theatre? It would provide a natural successor to ensure that Stephen Joseph’s legacy would continue his legacy after his untimely death in 1967 which led to Alan Ayckbourn becoming Artistic Director of the company in 1972.</p>
