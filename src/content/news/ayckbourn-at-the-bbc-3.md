<p>Seventy years ago this month, Alan Ayckbourn began work as a radio drama producer for the BBC.</p>

<p>It’s a five year period of his professional career which is neither widely discussed nor well documented. Yet it undoubtedly played a very significant role in his future work as writer, director and artistic director in the theatre.</p>

<p>In <strong><a href="/life/influences/bradley-alfred">Alfred Bradley</a></strong>, the famed northern radio producer, he also found someone who would have a profound impact on his life and career.</p>

<p><em>“In the mid-sixties, I worked alongside Alfred Bradley for five years as a fellow radio drama producer at the BBC Woodhouse Lane Studios in Leeds. Alfred was, I believe, instrumental in me getting the job. Indeed the post was only created in response to the enormous backlog of unproduced new play scripts that had accumulated on his desk, a victim of his own successful, self-generated, one man new play policy.</em></p>

<p>“<em>Alfred was hugely influential and encouraging to this fledgling producer, who prior to his arrival in Leeds had never even set foot inside a sound studio. Although only a dozen or so years older than I, Alfred turned out to be what I later referred to as one of my ‘guardian uncles’; those remarkable people whom I was lucky enough to meet in my early years who subsequently shaped and informed my life. I remain indebted to him.”</em></p>

<p>Alan had no intention nor dream of ever working for the <strong><a href="/career/bbc-career">BBC</a></strong> or in radio, but then – in 1964 – he had come to the conclusion he no longer wanted to work in theatre either and was looking for an alternative career path.</p>

<p>He had been working in theatre since leaving school at the age of 17, initially as an actor. Since 1959 he had been successfully writing for <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong>, Scarborough, and then the <strong><a href="/career/victoria-theatre">Victoria Theatre</a></strong>, Stoke-on-Trent, as well as directing since 1961.</p>

<p>He was looking for his big break and it appeared tangible when, in 1964, the theatre impresario Peter Bridge took Alan’s play <strong><em><a href="/plays/mr-whatnot">Mr Whatnot</a></em></strong> into the West End. It was, he hoped, going to be the next rung in his journey up the theatrical ladder.</p>

<p>It wasn’t. It was a disaster with Alan receiving some of the harshest reviews of his entire career. The production barely lasted two weeks before closing, leaving Alan upset and fully intending to never write for theatre again.</p>

<p> Knocked back by the experience, he phoned his agent <strong><a href="/life/influences/ramsay-margaret">Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay</a></strong> who – coincidentally – happened to be in a meeting with Alfred Bradley. He knew of Alan through the Library Theatre and – desperate to get Alan off the phone and Peggy’s attention back – urged Peggy to tell Alan to buy The Spectator and apply for a job advertised within.</p>

<p><em>“In 1965, licking my wounds from my first West End disaster, it was Alfred who suggested I apply for the job alongside him, the newly created Assistant Radio Drama Producer’s post at Leeds. I found myself in the office next door climbing a mountain of new scripts, inspired by the man who, virtually single-handedly, had launched the Northern writing explosion.</em></p>

<p><em>“From Alfred I learnt a little of how to encourage talent when it was apparent and how to say no as kindly as possible when it clearly wasn’t; how to enjoy and celebrate, just occasionally, the achievement and success of others.”</em></p>

<p>Alan began work in Leeds in January 1965 and moved his family – his wife, Christine, and their two sons – to nearby Adel. The announcement of the appointment had been carried in The Stage newspaper in November 1964 – which also managed, not for the last time, to misspell Alan’s name.  </p>

<p>His first day at the office must have been a surprise because he didn’t really have much idea about what he was going to do for Alan and it certainly wasn’t what he anticipated.</p>

<p><em>“I didn’t know what the job was when I applied to join the BBC. I thought I was going to be sorting our Alfred Bradley’s filing…. When I got there I found that, far from sorting out Alfred’s filing, I was going to be doing my own programmes and running with a great deal more responsibility than I’d had in the theatre.”</em></p>

<p>Alfred had built a fearsome reputation for championing new northern writing and would travel the north relentlessly looking for new talent and work. He is credited with helping launch the careers of Alan Plater, Keith Waterhouse, Alun Own and Stan Barstow among others.</p>

<p>But such was his success, that the BBC office in Leeds was being overwhelmed by new submitted scripts and Alfred was unable to handle them all. This became part of Alan’s responsibilities. As a script editor, he had to read the new work, evaluate them and to produce the work that got selected.</p>

<p>It was all a bit of a surprise given Alan had no experience of producing radio drama and he was thrown into the deep end within weeks of starting work, expected to cast, organise, direct, edit and produce these  plays for the radio.</p>

<p>Despite this lack of formal training, Alan believes he produced more than 50 radio plays during his first year at the BBC. They ranged from 30 to 90 minutes in length and were broadcast across the BBC Home Service and BBC Light.</p>

<p>Alan had no fear of asking for help and it is this, he believed, which helped him cope with the stress and pressure of those early months. The stage managers, other directors, secretaries and other staff at the BBC all helped Alan as he found himself quickly becoming an important and established part of the team.</p>

<p><em>“I had to work to find out what it was all about. I produced, as I say, my 50 shows, but found out the hard way through all the knocks. And Alfred guided me a bit but the studio managers carried me most of the time in that first year.”</em></p>

<p>Alan describes himself as a ‘mad man’ during the first year and loving the work and the challenge of it all. Her was also earning considerably more than at any previous point in his career receiving £38 a week, which made a substantial difference to both him and his family.</p>

<p>More importantly, Alan was learning skills which would soon inform his role as artistic director of the Library Theatre in Scarborough. The media may have been quite different, but the skills involved were highly transferable.</p>

<p><em>“As a director who was previously from theatre, I learnt the virtues of speed and economy. With two or three days to produce a finished product for broadcast, you can’t afford to hang about! It gave me a great opportunity to do far more plays – I did more plays in a year than I’d done in ten years in the theatre.”</em></p>

<p>Equally important was how what he learnt about new writing and working with new writers.  Alan had been encouraged to write in Scarborough by <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> – easily the most influential man in his life. Stephen was passionate about encouraging new writers and new writing and Alan would eventually step into Stephen’s shoes.</p>

<p>But it was largely from Alfred that Alan learnt the skills of how to work with, encourage and deal with new writers;  which would be of enormous benefit in his 37 years as artistic director of the Scarborough theatre,</p>

<p><em>“I’d already been working with actors, of course, and I suppose I had learned the hard way about directing them. But now I learned something about writers. I learned from Alfred a certain amount about how to treat writers, and how to draw them out.”</em></p>

<p>As a script editor, Alan read hundreds of new plays with Alfred insisting every writer deserved a written response. The responsibility of which demanded – in Alan’s opinion – objectivity and articulacy, which he felt fed through to his own writing.</p>

<p>Alan worked with a number of significant writers at the radio, but probably none more so than Roy Clarke. He would on to become a phenomenally successful writer for television responsible for writing such hits as&nbsp;<em>Open all Hours</em>,&nbsp;<em>Keeping Up Appearances&nbsp;</em>and&nbsp;<em>Last of the Summer Wine</em>.</p>

<p>Alan directed Roy’s two debut works,&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/career/bbc-career/bbc-radio-productions/the-events-at-black-tor">The Events at Black Tor</a></em></strong>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/career/bbc-career/bbc-radio-productions/the-17-jewelled-shock-proof">The 17-Jewelled Shockproof Swiss-Made Bomb</a></em></strong> with the impact of each illustrated by the fact the BBC released the original productions commercially for streaming in 2022.</p>

<p>He would also work with the writer Don Haworth and his two productions of Don’s work led to nominations for the  prestigious Prix Italia Award. The first nomination was for&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/career/bbc-career/bbc-radio-productions/theres-no-point-in-arguing-the-toss">There’s No Point Arguing The Toss</a></strong></em> (first broadcast in April 1967) and&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/career/bbc-career/bbc-radio-productions/we-all-come-to-it-in-the-end">We All Come To It In The End</a></strong></em>&nbsp;(first broadcast in July 1968).</p>

<p>It wasn’t only writers Alan met though. As a director, he worked with hundreds of actors, many of whom would go onto significantly successful careers on stage and screen.</p>

<p>Most famously, he was the first person to employ Bob Peck as a professional actor having met him in Leeds within the amateur dramatic circuit. He would also work with actors such as Ben Kingsley, Robert Powell, Joan Sims, Eileen Derbyshire, Stephanie Turner, Elizabeth Bell, Wilfred Pickles, James Bolam, Elisabeth Sladen and Leslie Sands among many others. Many of them would later come to work for him when he returned to the Library Theatre in Scarborough.</p>

<p>Over five years, it’s estimated that Alan directed more than 200 radio plays and read into the thousands of new plays. It was an incredibly five year period during which he learnt many of the skills which would inform his decision to return to Scarborough.</p>

<p>Alan left the BBC on 23 June 1970 and, two years later, took on the role of artistic director in Scarborough, following in his mentor Stephen Joseph’s footsteps and ensuring his theatrical legacy survived. There is no doubt that his success in the role was in no small part as a result of his work at the BBC.</p>

<p>And whilst Stephen Joseph has always been at the forefront of Alan’s mind, he has never forgotten his other ‘guardian uncle’ Alfred Bradley and how he contributed to his success and growth.</p>

<p><em>“If you’re lucky in life, you get to meet the right people at the right time who put you right about the world – and about yourself. Alfred was one. I shall always be grateful to him.”</em></p>
