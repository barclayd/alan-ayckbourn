<p>With the exciting news that Alan Ayckbourn’s classic play <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> is to be revived at <strong><a href="https://www.oldvictheatre.com/stage/other-half/">The Old Vic</a></strong> from 29 July to 19 September, here’s a quick primer to one of the most successful plays in the Ayckbourn play cannon,</p>

<ol>
<li><em>How The Other Half Loves</em> is considered a hugely innovative play and the first to utilise a composite set with two rooms overlaid each other, allowing simultaneous action in both rooms with actor in different ‘locations’ sharing the same performance space.</li>

<li>Further to that, it is considered the first play to depict two events on two different days in two locations simultaneously with two characters present at both events!</li>

<li>It was written whilst Alan Ayckbourn was working full-time as a Radio Drama Producer for the BBC in Leeds; its success is one of the reasons he left the BBC in 1970 after five years to return full-time to the theatre.</li>

<li><em>How The Other Half Loves</em> premiered at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, on 31 July 1969, directed by Alan Ayckbourn.</li>

<li>It was Alan Ayckbourn’s ninth play and written when he was just 30 years old.</li>

<li>It marked his final on-stage appearance as a professional actor – when the actor playing Frank Foster injured himself, Alan had to take to the stage for several performances as the company could not afford understudies. Alan had previously acted professionally between 1956 and 1964.</li>

<li>It was written for performance in-the-round and the composite set works best in that format. While the vast majority of performances subsequent to 1969 have been in the end-stage / proscenium arch, Alan Ayckbourn does not believe they work as well as in-the-round. The Old Vic performance will mark the first time it has been performed in-the-round in the West End.</li>

<li>The original production was stage-managed by Bob Peck – better known as the award-winning actor who would go on to fame on stage and screen, most notably in the BBC thriller <em>Edge of Darkness</em> and the Steven Spielberg’s <em>Jurassic Park</em>.</li>

<li>Its West End premiere took place at the Lyric Theatre, London, on 5 August 1970 starring Robert Morley as Frank Foster. It was a financially successful but hugely unsatisfying production as Morley changed it from an ensemble piece into a star vehicle for himself.</li>

<li><em>How The Other Half Loves</em> ran for more than two years with 869 performances and is the second-longest contiguous (i.e. unbroken) run of an Ayckbourn play in the West End after the 1973 production of <em>Absurd Person Singular</em>.</li>

<li>The West End production immediately toured to Canada and Australia to great success.</li>

<li>It was the first Ayckbourn play to have a West End revival and has been produced in the West End more times than any other Ayckbourn play: In 1969 (Lyric Theatre); 1988 (Duke of York’s Theatre); 2016 (Duke of York’s Theatre); 2026 (The Old Vic).</li>

<li><em>How The Other Half Loves</em> was the second full-length Ayckbourn play to be published and has never been out of print since 1972.</li>

<li>Its Broadway premiere was on 29 March 1971 at the Royale Theatre, New York, starring Phil Silvers as Frank Foster alongside Sandy Dennis as Teresa Phillips.</li>

<li>The play was famously ‘Americanised’ by Alan Ayckbourn and the production’s director Gene Saks for the Broadway production; Alan later regretted this particularly as the ‘compromised’ script was published and produced for many years in North America.</li>

<li>Largely dismissed as a farcical, star vehicle (it is neither of these things) due to Morley’s alterations in the West End, the play was re-evaluated following a production by the Actors Company in 1976 starring Simon Callow and Stephanie Turner. Performed as an ensemble with the original script, critics re-evaluated the play, praising its technical ingenuity and social commentary.</li>

<li> It has been performed around the world and been translated into more than 20 different languages.</li>

<li>Alan Ayckbourn has directed it twice for the world premiere in Scarborough in 1969 and for its 40th anniversary, also in Scarborough, in 2009.</li>

<li>It is the Ayckbourn play which, arguably, had the oddest request for a revised production. In 1976, a North American producer seriously requested he be allowed to stage a production which replaced two of the three heterosexual couples with same sex couples and the off-stage baby of one with an on-stage chimpanzee. The request was, obviously, denied!</li>

<li>It is considered to be one of Alan Ayckbourn’s most successful and produced works over the past six decades and he considers it to be the play which established his credentials as a successful writer.</li>
</ol>
