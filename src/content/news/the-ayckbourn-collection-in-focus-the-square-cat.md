<p><em>In August 2023, The Ayckbourn Collection was donated to Scarborough Museums and Galleries by Sir Alan and Lady Ayckbourn and the playwright’s Archivist, Simon Murgatroyd.</em></p>

<p><em>It is a collection of approximately 60 significant items drawn from Sir Alan’s Archive and Simon’s personal collection exploring the contribution of Alan Ayckbourn, Stephen Joseph and theatre in the round to Scarborough’s cultural heritage and history.</em></p>

<p><em>Currently, items from the exhibition are on display at Scarborough Art Gallery until early 2024. To mark the exhibition, Simon Murgatroyd – who curated the Collection and facilitated its donation – is taking a weekly look at some of the individual items within it, their significance and why they were chosen.</em></p>

<p><strong><em>The Square Cat</em> Manuscript</strong></p>

<p>This is where The Ayckbourn Collection began, for where else to start but the playwright’s first commissioned and produced work. This is the point where it all really begins for Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p>There are actually several original manuscripts for <em>The Square Cat</em> held between Scarborough Museums &amp; Galleries, the Ayckbourn Archive in the Borthwick Insitutue for Archives at the University of York, the John Rylands Library at the University of Manchester and the British Library.</p>

<p>But this one is special. Because it’s the only manuscript held in Scarborough, where the play premiered on 30 July 1959 at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre on the first floor of the public library.</p>

<p>This is definably a copy used within the original production too – unlike, say, the British Library’s copy which was a clean copy intended only for archive. The manuscript with Scarborough Museums &amp; Galleries was the property of William Elmhirst, an actor with the  Studio Theatre Ltd company (Stephen Joseph’s company which performed the theatre in the round seasons) during the 1959 summer and winter seasons, who appeared in seven plays including Alan Ayckbourn’s first two plays, <em>The Square Cat</em> and<em> Love After All</em>.</p>

<p>Rather implausibly, William played the son Steve, an ‘inane athletic youth of 19’, despite the fact he was 30 years old in reality; the actor who played his father, David Campton, was 35! You can see William in <em>The Square Cat</em> in the final photo accompanying this article with his ‘father’ which is reproduced from the Ayckbourn Archive at the Borthwick Institute for Archives.</p>

<p>You’ll also see from the accompanying photos that the manuscript is credited to Roland Allen, that’s because Alan’s ambition at the time was to be an actor and he had been acting with the Scarborough company since 1957. To differentiate between the roles, he chose a writing pseudonym which combined his name (Alan / Allen) with that of his soon-to-be-wife Christine Roland, who notably helped him write the play.</p>

<p>So why is <em>The Square Cat</em> so important and essential to The Ayckbourn Collection? Obviously, this is the start of Alan Ayckbourn’s playwriting career which – as of writing – spans 64 years and 89 full-length produced plays as well as numerous other works.</p>

<p>It is the first step in a career which by the late 1970s will see Alan become one of the most successful and well-known theatre practitioners in the UK whose work essentially originates in Scarborough before going on to be performed in the West End, around the UK, then Europe and even the world. Frequently they are also adapted for radio, television and film along the way.</p>

<p>And that’s important too. Because of Alan’s 89 plays – as of 2023 – <em>The Square Cat</em> is the first of 83 plays to premiere in Scarborough. If you can think of a famous Ayckbourn play, the chances are it premiered in his adopted home town, directed by him with a hand-picked company and was probably the definitive production, performed in-the-round for which it was conceived.</p>

</div>

<p>It’s also important because it’s one of the few material ties between Alan Ayckbourn and Stephen Joseph. Alan joined Studio Theatre Ltd at 18 and found both a mentor and father figure in Stephen. Alan frequently acknowledges Stephen Joseph is the most influential person in his entire life.</p>

<p>Despite this, there’s next to nothing in archive linking them. There’s about six photos with them together, there is no surviving correspondence between them in either the Ayckbourn Archive at York or the Stephen Joseph Archive in Manchester. We largely just have Alan’s fond memories.</p>

<p>And <em>The Square Cat</em>. For the only reason this play exists is because Alan complained to Stephen about the quality of the roles he was performing. Stephen countered, if he wanted better, write a play and give himself the lead role. If it was any good, Stephen said he’d produce it.</p>

<p>The rest if history, Alan wrote the play and Stephen staged it with Alan in the lead role. It was a very successful production – extended for a week during the 1959 summer season – and led to Stephen immediately commissioning a second play, <em>Love After All</em>. It shows the confidence Stephen had in Alan as well as his desire to encourage new playwrights.</p>

<p>Stephen sadly died before he saw Alan gain his extraordinary fame and success, but in the months prior to his death, Alan’s break-out play <em>Relatively Speaking</em> opened in the West End and Stephen must have had some sense his protégé was going to achieve great success. Not least in ensuring Stephen’s legacy was kept alive in Scarborough by taking on the role of Artistic Director of the company in 1972.</p>

<p><em>The Square Cat</em> manuscript is now on display as part of The Ayckbourn Collection at Scarborough Art Gallery until early 2024.</p>
