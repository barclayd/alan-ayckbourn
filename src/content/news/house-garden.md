<p>Twenty-five years ago this week, an extraordinary production opened any the National Theatre in London.</p>

<p>On 9 August 2020, Alan Ayckbourn’s <strong><em><a href="/plays/house-and">House &amp; Garden</a></em></strong> opened in The Olivier… and The Lyttelton… and across the theatre’s foyer.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn essentially took over the NT for that summer with his ambitious and profoundly theatrical piece of event theatre. The play, premiered in Scarborough the previous year, consists of two plays running simultaneously in two auditoria with a single cast moving back and forth between each play and stage.</p>

<p>It tells of a weekend event at a manor house from the perspective of, obviously, the house and garden. What is off-stage in one play is on-stage in the other. And, after the show, the fete featured in the play came to life in the NT foyer with artificial turf, a beer tent, stalls and actors in and out of character blurring the lines between where theatre starts and ends.</p>

<p>It was, for anyone who experienced it, a remarkable event and the success of which was later credited by the Artistic Director Trevor Nunn as essentially helping to save the NT at a very precarious financial moment.</p>

<p>To mark the 25th anniversary of this remarkable play, the blog is reproducing Alan Ayckbourn’s programme note for the National Theatre production offering an insight into  the creation and challenges of the piece.</p>

<p><strong>House &amp; Garden</strong> by Alan Ayckbourn</p>

<p>Generally, I write a new play each year for the theatre I run in Scarborough. Last year was my sixtieth birthday and it’s become a bit of a custom that whenever I reach these significant landmarks I try to come up with something rather more than a play, what I term an event.</p>

<p>In 1999 I produced <em>House &amp; Garden</em>.</p>

<p>At the Stephen Joseph Theatre we have two auditoria, a 400 seat theatre-in-the-round and a smaller 167 end stage auditorium. I decided to try and write a piece that would be performed in both spaces simultaneously, <em>House</em> playing in one whilst <em>Garden</em> was going on in the other. The plays would use the identical cast who would be required to move (or in some cases to run) between the two spaces as and when the action required. They would be performing essentially the same play albeit seen from two very different viewpoints.</p>

<p>I decided not to ‘cheat’ by having characters go off and hang around for hours before coming on again in the other theatre – after all that would have been far too easy. Instead it was planned that in many cases actors would have only just enough time to leave, say, <em>House</em> in order to turn up in <em>Garden</em> in time to play their next scene.</p>

<p>It was, as they say, nail biting stuff. After all, who can predict how a live audience will affect a play?</p>

<p>More important, it’s a fact that no two audiences are alike. In this case, as we found, not even two audiences seeing the same play on the same night are alike. How much would the critical timings vary from night to night? The answer was that in the end not as drastically as I’d feared.</p>

<p>Moreover, thanks to three “catching up” points – a scene change in the middle of both acts and the interval itself – we were able to bring the shows down at the end, generally within 5 to 10 seconds of each other, due to some very clever cue-ing and close collusion between the two DSM’s [Deputy Stage Managers] actually running the shows. The most difficult moment was, in fact, the curtain calls which took a good deal of orchestrating.</p>

<p>For the actors it was, I think, a unique experience. A stage actor is used to playing their audience for the evening, to building a rapport, an understanding between the two of them, be they hero or a villain. But in <em>House &amp; Garden</em> they frequently found themselves being perceived as both, depending on the perspective from which they were being seen. Moreover, the leading actor in one play became necessarily the small part player in the other – thus pursuing a theme I had long wanted to explore, namely that we are all of us walk-on players in other people’s lives.</p>

<p>And the audience? Well, they soon joined in the fun of it, too. Indeed it was interesting how, when they saw their second play (which could be either <em>House</em> or <em>Garden</em> – they can be seen in either order), they were seen to be eagerly joining together the missing pieces of the jigsaw they had been given from the night before.</p>

<p>Then there was also the little matter of an additional surprise which we threw in afterwards… But you’ll need to see the plays to experience that.</p>

<p>It’s a huge venture, occasionally nerve wracking but always exciting. I suppose all that live theatre’s meant to be, really. Now we’re about to do it all again. We must be mad. Olivier and Lyttelton, here we come.</p>

<p>Danger! Actors in transit! Please don’t block the stairways.</p>
