<p>Here on the Ayckbourn Blog, we’re celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in July 1955.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn was a vital and significant presence at <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> and these articles celebrate the plays he wrote for the company between 1959 and 1976. They offer an in-depth look at the creation of and background to some of the playwright’s most famous – and most obscure – works.</p>

<p>In the previous article, we looked at his fourth play – <em><strong><a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/2025/12/09/the-library-theatre-at-70-the-ayckbourn-plays-standing-room-only/">Standing Room Only</a></strong></em> (1961) – we’re now skipping forward to his eighth play, <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-sparrow">The Sparrow</a></strong></em> (having covered <em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></strong></em> in-depth for its 60th anniversary last year <strong><a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/2025/07/08/relatively-speaking-at-60-the-facts/">here</a></strong>, <strong><a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/2025/07/17/relatively-speaking-at-60-the-author/">here</a></strong> and <strong><a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/2025/08/05/relatively-speaking-at-60/">here</a></strong>).</p>

<p><strong>The Sparrow (1967)</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>For <em>The Sparrow</em> (1967), we take quite a leap forward in time since the previous article in December 2025 where we looked at Alan Ayckbourn’s fourth play, <em>Standing Room Only</em> (1961), and what the playwright might plausibly have thought could be his final play for Scarborough.</p>

<p>In 1962, Stephen Joseph opened the UK’s first professional theatre-in-the-round venue with The Victoria Theatre in Stoke-on-Trent; Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre being a seasonal venue was home to the UK’s first professional in-the-round company.</p>

<p>That summer, Alan – alongside most of the The Library Theatre company – upped sticks and left Scarborough Theatre for the Potteries.</p>

<p>Alan would work at the Victoria Theatre as writer, director and actor until 1964, premiering his fifth and sixth plays, <em><strong><a href="/plays/christmas-v-mastermind">Christmas V Mastermind</a></strong></em> (1962) and <em><strong><a href="/plays/mr-whatnot">Mr Whatnot</a></strong></em> (1963) at the venue.</p>

<p>Alan left the Victoria in 1964 because <em>Mr Whatnot</em> had been optioned for the West End by the producer Peter Bridge and this promised to be Alan’s big break – no more small town theatres such as Scarborough or Stoke-on-Trent. This was the promise of the big time….</p>

<p>It was, of course, a disaster. <em>Mr Whatnot</em> opened in 1964 at the New Arts Theatre and closed within two weeks on the back of damning reviews.</p>

<p>Depressed and with no job to speak of, Alan decided to quit playwriting and, thanks to his agent <strong><a href="/life/influences/ramsay-margaret">Margaret Ramsay</a></strong>, applied for a job at the <strong><a href="/career/bbc-career">BBC</a></strong> as a Radio Drama Producer based in Leeds working for the noted producer <strong><a href="/life/influences/bradley-alfred">Alfred Bradley</a></strong>.</p>

<p>Theoretically, that could well have marked the end of Alan’s writing career as he was very successful at the BBC and worked there for five years. But at the same time as he began work at the BBC, Stephen Joseph commissioned him to write a life-changing play, <em>Meet My Father</em> for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. We now know the play as <em>Relatively Speaking</em>.</p>

<p>This play was picked up for the West End in 1967 – again by Peter Bridge. Unlike <em>Mr Whatnot</em>, this was a tremendous success and re-launched Alan’s playwriting career, marking him as one of the country’s most notable young writers. Despite this, Alan was committed to the BBC and established a notable career as a radio producer between 1965 and 1970.</p>

<p>From this situation came <em>The Sparrow</em> in 1967 and, strange as it might seem, its fate is irrevocably intertwined with <em>Relatively Speaking</em>.  Arguably, the latter informed the direction the former took and also helped consign it to obscurity.</p>

<p>Having been closed by Stephen Joseph in 1965, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was re-opened and re-launched in Scarborough in 1967 with a new Artistic Director, Rodney Wood; Stephen Joseph having stepped down the previous year after being diagnosed with terminal cancer.</p>

<p>The future of the company was uncertain and although both the Arts Council and Scarborough Council had restored previously withdrawn funding, it was not built on the most solid of financial foundations and would only operate for a 14 week summer season between 1967 and 1974.</p>

<p>For the 1967 summer season, four plays were presented including the world premiere of Alan Plater’s <strong><em><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-2/page-108/page-144/page-253/page338.html">Hop, Step and Jump</a></em></strong>, alongside productions of J.B. Priestley’s <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-2/page-108/page-144/page-255/page340.html">Eden End</a></strong></em> and Anouilh’s <strong><em><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-2/page-108/page-144/page-256/page341.html">Romeo and Jeannette</a></em></strong>. For the fourth play, Alan was asked to write and direct a new play for the company; this would mark the first time Alan would direct one of his own works in Scarborough and, subsequently, he would direct every Scarborough premiere of his work.</p>

<p>Alan has never spoken about why he accepted the commission, yet it is notable. It had been 18 months since he wrote his last play, <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, and he was now firmly established in his job at the BBC. It was also the first time he had been commissioned for a play by someone other than Stephen Joseph.</p>

<p>Perhaps he accepted because he felt a loyalty to both the company and what Stephen had created. Perhaps it was a favour to his boss, Alfred Bradley, who was now part of the new Scarborough Theatre Trust board or that Rodney Wood had introduced Alan to both the company and Stephen Joseph in 1967. Whatever the case, it wasn’t an obvious decision given the direction his career was apparently going.</p>

<p>And this is important, because the events of 1967 will influence the course of all that follows. Alan was commissioned to write <em>The Sparrow</em> in early 1967, at which point, he was still a little known writer with a West End flop behind him and several successful regional productions in Scarborough, but who had only written two plays in the previous four years.</p>

<p>Meanwhile, he had already become an established voice at the BBC having directed more than 100 radio plays in two years and, that April, his production of Don Howarth’s <em>There’s No Point Arguing The Toss</em> would be nominated for the prestigious Prix Italia award. At this time, <em>Relatively Speaking</em> had just begun rehearsals for its pre-West End tour.</p>

<p>All this will change during the next six months, during the writing and production of <em>The Sparrow</em>.</p>

<p>Whatever the reason for accepting the commission, it was on unusual terms. The contract for <em>The Sparrow</em> exists in archive – the earliest of Alan’s contracts to survive – and he was not paid an upfront fee, receiving instead seven-and-a-half per cent of the gross profit, guaranteed at a minimum of £100. He also received no pay as director of the production. The company was so financially strapped, it instead offered him free accommodation during rehearsals – whilst he was still ostensibly working in Leeds at the BBC. </p>

<p>In hindsight, this is also a key moment in Alan’s relationship with the company. For between 1967 to 2009, Alan would never draw a wage from the company, initially as director and later as Artistic Director.</p>

<p>True to form for the majority of Alan’s writing from the 1960s to the mid 1980s, Alan submitted a title for the play far in advance of writing it. In this case, he submitted the title <em>The Silver Collection</em>&nbsp;and this featured in early discussions and correspondence about the summer programme as well as in the earliest advertising for the season. The summer season opened on 10 July and the play was being referred to as <em>The Silver Collection</em> at least as late as 28 May when it was announced in a press release which included – astonishingly – a mis-spelling of Alan’s name as “Ayckebourn” twice in the same official document!</p>

<p>Now we can only speculate why the title changed. But <em>The Silver Collection</em> has no relevance to anything within the play he wrote – although to be fair, that’s not entirely unusual for Alan, <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em> anyone? But given this disparity between a rather specific title and the play itself, it seems plausible Alan had a different idea in mind when he submitted the title. By the time he came to write the play – probably five months after submitting the title – the play could have become something else entirely.</p>

<p>Why might this be? If we postulate the play changed during those intervening months, we can find a very cogent reason why Alan might have suddenly focused his mind on writing something different.</p>

<p>By the time Alan came to put pencil to paper – all Alan’s drafts until the 1980s are all handwritten in pencil – in June 1967, his circumstances were very different to January 1967. <em>Relatively Speaking</em> had opened at the Duke of York’s Theatre on 29 March and had been an immediate and resounding hit. By the following week, the critics had proclaimed it a must-see show and performances was frequently selling-out. On 2 May, he received a telegram from Noël Coward congratulating him on the play and on 22 May, Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth II saw the play. He would also have received his first not insubstantial royalty cheque from the production too.</p>

<p>Alan was suddenly writing a follow-up to the hottest ticket in the West End and one can only imagine the pressure this might have put on him to create a worthy successor – or perhaps more accurately, that difficult ‘second album.’ A point he made during an interview in 1969.</p>

<p>“I suppose every writer who writes a hit play has a ghost chasing him. If people like your play you are, after all, faced with a decision. You can either write the same play again under a different title and probably enjoy at least three-quarter of your previous success over again. Or you can keep faith with yourself and try to do something different.”</p>

<p>And Alan certainly went for something completely different. From the high-comedy of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, he went for almost Pinteresque, grim urban realism with <em>The Sparrow</em>. The lightness and technical trickery of <em>Relatively Speaking</em> was replaced with, as the biographer Ian Watson describes “a straight chronological narrative, devoid of deft plot manipulation, about four distinctly unglamorous young people in a grotty flat.”</p>

<p>Alan also altered the title to the more appropriate and plot-relevant <em>The Sparrow</em> shortly before the summer season began, although he would later express dis-satisfaction with this title too!</p>

<p><em>The Sparrow</em>‘s plot – as such – revolves around a young couple, Ed and Evie and the efforts of Ed’s flatmate, Tony, to disrupt the relationship as revenge on Ed for a one-night stand with his estranged wife, Julie.  It could not be further in style and structure from <em>Relatively Speaking</em> and there is a strong argument that Alan may well have been more inspired by Harold Pinter’s writing – having been directed by Pinter in his directorial debut of <em><strong><a href="/career/actor/acting-alan-ayckbourn-in-harold-pinters-the-birthday-party">The Birthday Party</a></strong></em> in 1959.</p>

<p> Biographer Michael Billington makes the point: “I assume that Ayckbourn, who in his job as a BBC drama producer was exposed to a lot of plays, was merely subconsciously offering his own variation on the kind of themes that were floating around English drama, and life, in the 1960s: themes to do with territory, domination and domestic power being aired not only in plays by Pinter and Jellicoe but in the anthropological works of Robert Ardrey and Konrad Lorenz. The play is also definably Ayckbourn’s in its disenchanted view of marriage and in its sympathy for the victims of marital power games who finally strike a blow for independence. Ayckbourn is fascinated by the relationship between the exploiter and the exploited; and, from first to last, he is emphatically on the side of the underdog.”</p>

<p>As well as by Albert E Kalson in his critical study <em>Laughter in the Darkness</em>: “[<em>The Sparrow</em>] approaches, if it does not achieve, the claustrophobic shape and menacing air of a Pinter play.”</p>

<p>Intriguingly, in correspondence from 1986, Alan makes reference to a play that was directly inspired by Pinter, but does not name it. Objectively, it could only be <em>The Sparrow</em>: “I wrote a very good Pinter play years ago. Nobody wanted to do it because Pinter was currently writing them better, but it taught me a lot about playwriting and the use Pinter made of language or the lack of it.”</p>

<p><em>The Sparrow</em> opened at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre on 13 July 1967 and ran for three weeks in rep, directed by Alan in-the-round. A mid-season press release states that both it and <em>Hop, Step and Jump</em> had been playing to capacity audiences.</p>

<p>The cast, with hindsight, was remarkable and consisted of Robert Powell, John Nettles, Heather Stoney and Pamela Craig. It has generally been noted that this marked the professional stage debut of John Nettles, who would go onto considerable success on stage and screen, although this is bending the truth slightly. John was also in <em>Hop, Step and Jump</em> which premiered three days before <em>The Sparrow</em>; for the sake of a better story, those three days have obviously been conveniently ignored!</p>

<p>Reviews were mixed and potentially suffered from comparisons with <em>Relatively Speaking</em> as it continued to storm the West End. Five reviews survive in archive – and judging from other productions of this period, that probably was the sum total of published reviews.</p>

<p>John Stevenson in the Daily Mail was the most effusive, writing: “This play will be running in the West End in a year or two’s time making packed audiences laugh very loud and very often. It’s what is now happening to the author’s comedy&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>. Not that the two plays have much in common, apart from the author’s likely talent. He has shrewdly avoided the lure that has led many a playwright to disaster – the temptation to re-write an earlier hit.”</p>

<p>Eric Shorter at the Daily Telegraph called Alan out on the same point, disappointed by comparison with the earlier work: :”No point in expecting miracles twice over. Just because Alan Ayckbourn had a new play coming on at Theatre in the Round, Scarborough, was no reason for supposing that we should get another comedy as deliciously theatrical and cleverly contrived as the successful&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>… His writing is worthy of Pinter at his funniest. But the piece begins to show its contrivance at half time and the pleasant hold it has had on our attention, we have witnessed no more than a skilful diversion.”</p>

<p>Elsewhere, The Stage described it as ” full of laughs from slick and witty dialogue”, the Northern Echo as a “slick and sophisticated comedy… It reminds one of Shaffer’s&nbsp;<em>Private Ear</em>, but the comedy is much more witty and original” whilst the Scarborough Evening News merely regurgitated the plot without actually stating an opinion.</p>

<p>It’s inserting to wonder what the Scarborough audience – probably evenly split between tourists and residents – made of the play. Yes, it was very different to his last play, but then, so had each of his plays previous to that in Scarborough. Alan was still, arguably, finding his voice as a playwright at this point. The Scarborough audience would not have been able to define an Ayckbourn play other than perhaps as an entertaining evening. We’re a decade away from someone being able to say a play was ‘Ayckbournian’.</p>

<p>And that’s worth considering, Alan is still experimenting with form and structure – although as Watson rightly points out, <em>The Sparrow</em> is probably the least structurally interesting of any Ayckbourn play. We are not at the point where anyone could rightly predict what Alan would write next or even what type of writer he was going to be, except in the very broadest of senses as a writer of comedies. Consider his previous plays: contemporary farce (<em>The Square Cat</em>); period farce (<em>Love After All</em>), children’s ballet / play (<em>Dad’s Tale</em>); science-fiction situation comedy (<em>Standing Room Only</em>); semi-improvised panto-type (<em>Christmas V Mastermind</em>); silent surreal comedy (<em>Mr Whatnot</em>); high comedy (<em>Relatively Speaking</em>); suburban comedy / theatre of menace (<em>The Sparrow</em>). </p>

<p>With no way of ascertaining how the audience received it – aside from it being successful at the box office – the only other insight into the play we have is several quotes from a document from the Arts Council . This contains readers’ quotes from a script reading in regard to whether it justified a financial guarantee. The extracts are of limited use as the play was not seen only read, but – unlike the reviews – they are not written in relation to the success of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>.</p>

<p>“The author finishes the play with no finish. He literally leaves us exactly where we came in with the air of having had enough of it himself – so that’s that. I think this is exacerbated because amusing, facile and witty as the dialogue is, the meeting of the four characters throughout the acting of the comedy produces no develop-ment, change or, in the main effect upon each other at all. In terms of character, the piece is absolutely static. One wishes it had just that further development of insight and character that the quality of the dialogue and the author’s gift for character justifies.</p>

<p>“It’s only a slight little comedy, but it has a touch of quality. Dialogue and characterisation ring absolutely true. Mr. Ayckbourn tells us everything about his people and their relationships that he wants us to know.”</p>

<p>“A simple tale, of one basic situation. It is told with competent, unthrilling dialogue, but it doesn’t get us anywhere. The characters arrive, move, speak, and leave, but neither they nor the situation develops…. It is, probably, a custom-made playlet for four actors known to the author, and they may enjoy it for a week.”</p>

<p>The latter quote may actually have been closer to the mark than the author realised. Unlike the majority of Alan’s plays, the character descriptions in <em>The Sparrow</em> are surprisingly detailed and accurate to the rather notable group of actors playing them, all of whom Alan was familiar with from his work at the BBC.</p>

<p>If anything – and with regards to the history of the play – these quotes seem to be the most relevant as to what the play was really like and why it was never going to make a mark like <em>Relatively Speaking</em>.</p>

<p>Back in Scarborough, Peter Bridge, the producer of <em>Relatively Speaking</em> in the West End, visited the production alongside its director, Nigel Patrick – who reportedly was not terribly impressed. Bridge did option the play but contemporary correspondence suggests this more was to keep a promising playwright on side.</p>

<p>The play never made it into the West End and Bridge’s excuse was that he couldn’t find young stars who could both carry the show and draw notice in the West End; a reasonable excuse especially if the characters had been so specifically drawn, but ironic given the trajectories Robert Powell and John Nettle’s careers were soon to take.</p>

<p>Famously, it was also Bridge that all but spiked the play anyway when Alan recalled Bridge comparing it to Ann Jellicoe’s 1962 play <em>The Knack</em> – essentially because both had a central female character and were set in London. <em>The Knack</em> had been a hit at the Royal Court and had recently enjoyed success in a 1965 film starring Michael Caine. It was an inappropriate comparison which, Alan believes, essentially killed the play’s prospects.</p>

</div>

<p>“At the time, the only reason it was suppressed was it was a bit like&nbsp;<em>The Knack</em>, somebody said [likely to be Peter Bridge]. Since I hadn’t seen&nbsp;<em>The Knack</em>, I didn’t realise. I’ve seen&nbsp;<em>The Knack</em> since. It is a bit like&nbsp;<em>The Knack</em>&nbsp;– it’s got a girl in the lead, that’s what it was. But then, so has&nbsp;<em>Antony &amp; Cleopatra</em>.”</p>

<p>Alan later expressed the view that Bridge’s real reason for not producing the play was he wanted a safe play in a similar mould to&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>&nbsp;and into which he could put a bankable star name –&nbsp;<em>The Sparrow</em>&nbsp;fulfilled neither of these criteria.</p>

<p>The play closed at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre on 6 September 1967 and it was never produced professionally again. There were interested parties and Alan was certainly open to it being produced into the 1970s, but these came to nothing. As he noted <em>The Sparrow </em>“only had three weeks in its life, those three weeks at Scarborough. It’s probably worth a little more than that.”</p>

<p>Alan has said in subsequent years, he wonders how&nbsp;<em>The Sparrow</em>&nbsp;would have developed as a play had it been given more opportunity, but it was soon forgotten amidst the success of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>&nbsp;in the West End during that summer of 1967 and by the equally extraordinary success of his next play, <em><strong><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves">How The Other Half Loves</a></strong></em> (1969), which cemented Alan’s place as a successful West End comedy writer. </p>

<p>Unproduced, unloved and at direct odds with the playwriting path he would take, by the 1980s Alan had withdrawn <em>The Sparrow</em> and, like many other of his early plays, made a largely ineffectual attempt to destroy existing copies. For many years, it was believed <em>The Sparrow</em> was essentially lost but since the early 2000s, three extant copies have been located, all of which are now in public archive at the Ayckbourn Archive at the <strong><a href="https://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page-14/page18.html">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a></strong> at the University of York, in <strong><a href="https://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page-13/page18.html">The Ayckbourn Collection</a></strong> at Scarborough Museums and Galleries and in the Lord Chamberlain’s Collection in the British Library.</p>

<p>These are the only places where Ayckbourn fans have an opportunity to evaluate <em>The Sparrow</em> as the playwright has made it clear it is now considered part of the early body of his work which he will never give permission to be performed again. Realistically – given Alan’s stated wishes – the only chance of it being performed is when the copyright runs out, seven decades after the playwright’s death.</p>

<p><em>The Sparrow</em> stands as an interesting oddity, sandwiched as it is between the to plays which would make him a star and launch one of the most remarkable and successful playwriting careers of the late 20th century. It stands in direct contrast to both – and pretty much anything else he has subsequently written.</p>

<p>Yet it is not a play to be ignored because it has its points of interest – not least in its attempt to do something completely different to anything else the playwright was writing. It shows his versatility as a young writer – he was 28 at the time – and his desire to experiment and move forward in his craft.</p>

<p>It also emphatically deals with many of the themes which will dominate his later writing – not least with regard to relationships and marriage – as well as offering a glimpse of a darker edge to Ayckbourn’s writing which would not appear again for several years to come.</p>

<p><em>The Sparrow</em> may not be considered an essential or even an important Ayckbourn play, but its is arguably a significant work and one which helps illuminates the speed of his development as a writer.  Within the space of four plays and five years, he will have moved onto  <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em> (1972) and the string of remarkable plays and successes which follow.</p>
