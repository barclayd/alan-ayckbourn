<p>As visitors to the blog will be aware, we’ve been celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>With the anniversary year in full swing, the blog comes closer to a home with a look at all the plays Alan Ayckbourn wrote for <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> between 1959 and 1976.</p>

<p>Over the coming months, we’ll be looking at each of his Library Theatre plays in-depth, providing some insight and background into the creation of some of the playwright’s most famous – and obscure – works.</p>

<p>Alan’s third play – and his first failure as a playwright – premiered in 1960 with <strong><em><a href="/plays/dads-tale">Dad’s Tale</a></em></strong>.</p>

<p><strong>Dad’s Tale (1960)</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>In 1960, Alan Ayckbourn laid the seeds for one of the most interesting threads of his career with his first significant failure.</p>

<p>That Christmas, Alan was commissioned by Stephen Joseph to write a Christmas play for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre’s winter season. It was his first attempt to write a play for a family audience and it was beset with issues – a couple of which are quite extraordinary in hindsight.</p>

<p>Its abject failure as a play with audiences was his first major taste of theatrical disappointment but would also be his earliest foray into what would become a passionate advocacy of encouraging young people to visit the theatre and the writing of his substantial canon of what are dubbed the ‘family plays’, such as &nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/mr-as-amazing-maze-plays">Mr A’s Amazing Maze Plays</a></em></strong> and <em><strong><a href="/plays/invisible-friends">Invisible Friends</a></strong></em>.</p>

<figure><figcaption><sub>Rosamund Dickson &amp; Stanley Page in Dad’s Tale (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Although it was a long time coming – it took 28 years after <em>Dad’s Tale</em> for Alan to overturn his belief  he had no talent for writing for young people!</p>

<p>Back in 1960 though and the seeds of the play’s failure were planted long before Alan got to work on it – for it was conceived in the most unusual way and Alan found himself working in a way he would never do again. <em>Dad’s Tale</em> is highly unusual in that it owes its origins and structure to several different people. It is practically the only time Alan was writing a play to order that largely incorporated other people’s ideas and needs; all this can be clearly seen in the resulting piece.</p>

<p>Alan’s first two plays,&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/the-square-cat">The Square Cat</a></em></strong>&nbsp;(1959) and&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/love-after-all">Love After All</a></em></strong> (1959), had been popular and financial successes for&nbsp;Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, and for his third play, the Artistic Director&nbsp;Stephen Joseph&nbsp;asked Alan to collaborate with the company’s writer-in-residence,&nbsp;<strong><a href="http://articles.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-75/page-7/styled-16/">David Campton</a></strong>, to create a Christmas play aimed predominantly at children.</p>

<p>This was perhaps an unusual suggestion in itself as Alan and David’s writing styles and interests were totally divergent. David Campton had already begun an adaptation of Mary Norton’s novel&nbsp;<em>The Borrowers</em>&nbsp;and Stephen suggested this was a good starting point. Alan read the play but this became the sole extent of the collaboration as, discovering he was not a natural collaborator, he was left to write the piece alone. The only remnant of&nbsp;<em>The Borrowers</em>&nbsp;in the play is the Tinies, little people who steal things throughout the sparse plot.</p>

<p>The second unusual decision to affect the play was this was to be a joint production between Studio Theatre Ltd and the British Dance Drama Theatre. The company had performed daily at the Library Theatre prior to the evening’s main performance from 11 July – 6 August that summer and Stephen had been impressed with the company’s work. As a result, he had suggested the two companies combine forces for a Christmas play.</p>

<p>It might have seemed like a good idea at the time, but it seems doubtful Stephen fully thought out all the practicalities of such a decision.</p>

<p>The first major difficulty was the lack of budget, which meant the two companies could not rehearse together. As a result, the Studio Theatre company rehearsed their scenes in Scarborough and British Dance Drama Theatre, under the direction of Gerard Bagley, rehearsed in Birmingham. The two companies were only brought together for the dress rehearsal. Obviously not an ideal way to mount a production!</p>

<p>The co-production also meant dance had to be substantially incorporated into the script; which apparently took Alan by surprise as Stephen Joseph only told him about the ballet scenes after he had accepted the commission…</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Rosamund Dickson with dancer Tonya Burcka as a Christmas Tree in Dad’s Tale (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p> Alan had no experience of dance and certainly not of writing scenes for a ballet. His solution to this hurdle was by having separate dance sections which illustrated the dreams of his characters. In essence, the dance scenes and the drama scenes were totally separate and the dancing could just be dropped into the play when the British Dance Drama Theatre arrived in Scarborough. Although Alan had no experience as a choreographer, he gave the dance company detailed notes of what he wanted and how and when they should interact with the Scarborough company</p>

<p>The acting company included Stanley Page making his debut at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre; the Australian born actor would become a regular member of the Studio Theatre company and appear in the world premieres of a number of Alan Ayckbourn plays including&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></em></strong>,&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/confusions">Confusions</a></em></strong>,&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></em></strong>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/sisterly-feelings">Sisterly Feelings</a></em></strong>. Alan Ayckbourn was also in the company and played seven roles; apparently during one performance he heard a woman exclaim: “Oh no! Not him again”.</p>

<p><em>Dad’s Tale</em>&nbsp;was notably directed by Clifford Williams – who had previously directed Alan’s second play <em>Love After All</em> and who would go onto a significant directing and influential career with the Royal Shakespeare Company. He successfully brought the two companies together for the dress rehearsals at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre two days before the show was due to open. There is nothing to indicate there were any major problems with the integration of the companies nor the dance with the drama – Alan’s extensive notes to British Dance Drama Theatre and the choreographer Gerald Bagley had obviously been an asset.</p>

<p>The play starred Hazel Burt, Rosamund Dickson, Philip Clifford, David Jarrett, Stanley Page and Alan Ayckbourn. The dancers were Tonya Burcka, Jennifer Wright, Frances Harper, Brenda Elder and Robert Horobin</p>

<p>The play opened on 19 December 1960, although its success as a family play is very hard to judge as, according to Alan, very few children attended the play. This was not necessarily a reflection on the play, but certainly a reflection on the company’s inexperience of staging family plays.</p>

<p><em>Dad’s Tale</em>&nbsp;was scheduled for the school holidays, thus depriving it of the large potential school party audience these shows appeal to. It also had very little marketing, particularly as the company not only did not have a maerktingf or press department but also no experience promoting family plays.</p>

<p>As a result it ended up with particularly small audiences in both Scarborough and Newcastle-under-Lyme’s Municipal Hall, which it toured to in January 1961. The latter was part of a tour including other venues at Southampton, Hemel Hempstead, and Ealing, but it is not known whether&nbsp;<em>Dad’s Tale</em>&nbsp;was actually produced at these venues; given it’s initial lack of success and a lack of any evidence to suggest otherwise, it seems likely the play was quietly withdrawn from the tour schedule.</p>

<p>Although no-one would ever argue that&nbsp;<em>Dad’s Tale</em>&nbsp;is a milestone in Alan’s playwriting career, several observers have noted there is one innovation in the plot which gives a clear hint of Alan’s burgeoning talent and his desire to break the mould.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>A scene from Dad’s Tale with Alan Ayckbourn (centre) (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>In one scene, Martin and his Auntie tell the story of how the neighbours have responded to Dad stealing their Christmas hamper. Although they are ostensibly telling the same story, their interpretations are radically different from each other as Ayckbourn biographer Michael Billington has pointed out.</p>

<p><em>“This combination of narrative and ballet sounds like a recipe for disaster. But although the show never got an audience, it does have a blend of free-flowing imagination and robust comedy. It is the kind of play that only someone deeply in love with the theatre could have written…. But what is interesting is how, even at this early stage, Ayckbourn experiments with different ways of telling it [the story]. Long before David Halliwell began to explore the possibilities of multi-viewpoint drama, Ayckbourn shows us a single incident from several points of view.”<br>Michael Billington</em></p>

<p>For a children’s play – or indeed for any play of this period – the idea of breaking the narrative structure to present alternate viewpoints is quite a radical idea. Of course, alternate scenes will become a common feature of Alan’s later work in plays such as&nbsp;<em>Sisterly Feelings</em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/intimate-exchanges">Intimate Exchanges</a></em></strong>.</p>

<p>The play was also notable for&nbsp;marking the first time Alan broke the ‘fourth wall’ (if it’s possible to have a fourth wall in theatre-in-the-round) with the lead character Martin speaking directly to the audience. Alan would go on to use this device a number of times in his future ‘family’ plays.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn wrote&nbsp;<em>Dad’s Tale</em>&nbsp;under the pseudonym of Roland Allen and it is now considered to be the final play to be credited to that name. Although his next play,&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/standing-room-only">Standing Room Only</a></em></strong>, was originally credited to Roland Allen, when it was later revived the play was – and has since been – attributed to Alan Ayckbourn.&nbsp;</p>

<p><em>Dad’s Tale</em>&nbsp;has never been published and is not available for production – understandably given its lack of success; it is among the playwright’s early plays which have been withdrawn as he believes it reflects a period when he was still learning the craft of playwriting. </p>

<p>Unlike its preceding play, <em>Love After All</em>, which Alan seems to have been determined to destroy, <em>Dad’s Tale</em> is more easily found for research. Original copies of the manuscript are held in the Ayckbourn Archive in the <strong><a href="https://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a></strong> at the University Of York, The Ayckbourn Collection at <strong><a href="https://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Scarborough Museums and Galleries</a></strong> and in the Lord Chamberlain’s Collection in the British Library as well as in several private collections.</p>

<p>Although&nbsp;<em>Dad’s Tale</em>&nbsp;was Alan’s first taste of theatrical failure following the success of his first two plays, it was an important lesson for Alan. Stephen Joseph believed in the right to fail and that writers had to make mistakes if they were to learn and move forward. <em>Dad’s Tale</em> was Alan’s first major playwriting mistake, but he would learn from it and move forward – his next play was a huge success in Scarborough.</p>

<p>And perhaps the most important lesson he learnt is whilst he initially believed he had no talent for writing plays for families, the lesson he took from it albeit three decades later, was he certainly could and that it was more the circumstances surrounding the play and a then inexperienced playwright that were the issue. Not his writing talents.</p>
