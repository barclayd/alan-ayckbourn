<p>Twenty years ago, Alan Ayckbourn’s acclaimed play <em><strong><a href="/plays/private-fears-in-public-places">Private Fears in Public Places</a></strong></em> premiered at the <strong><a href="/career/ayckbourn-and-the-sjt/sjt-venues/stephen-joseph-theatre">Stephen Joseph Theatre</a></strong> in Scarborough.</p>

<p>It is regarded as one of his most significant works of the past two decades and, the year after opening in Scarborough, it went on to an extraordinary run at the 59E59 Theatres, New York, both selling out whilst being acclaimed and named as one of the best plays of the year by several prestigious publications.</p>

</div>

<p>But did you know, that thirty years ago, an entirely different play by Alan Ayckbourn with the same title of <em>Private Fears in Public Places</em> was due to premiere at the Scarborough company’s former home, the <strong><a href="/career/ayckbourn-and-the-sjt/sjt-venues/stephen-joseph-theatre-in-the-round">Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round</a></strong>? </p>

<p>Indeed, it was even advertised, publicised and tickets went on sale for a production which never took place.</p>

<p>It’s a very rare example of an Ayckbourn play being announced and officially advertised before being replaced by a completely different play. The other prime example of this being <strong><em><a href="/plays/the-jollies">The Jollies</a></em></strong>.</p>

<p>So what happened to <em>Private Fears in Public Places</em> and why, a decade later, did a different play with the same title surface? Here’s Alan Ayckbourn to explain.</p>

<p>“Basically, I had two ideas bouncing around my head [in 1993]. So the final piece could have emerged from either one of them. And the play I absolutely thought I would write is a rather gruelling piece set in an airport departure lounge – so that is the one which went in to the brochure. When I actually started to write the advertised play, <em>Private Fears In Public Places</em>, it all rather alarmingly began coming to pieces in my hands. It wasn’t ready to be written. Certain parts were intact, but it was like crafting a piece of furniture without legs.”</p>

<p>Having decided this “pretty bleak” and gruelling piece was to be his 46th play, he gave the press office at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round the title so it could be included in the winter 1993 / 1994 brochure. Alan also wrote the advertising copy for the as-yet unwritten play, ironically noting in the memo: “By the time I get round to writing it, it’ll probably be about eight obstetricians trapped in a lift.”</p>

<p>Alan went on holiday to Scotland to write the play in late August / early September. But disaster struck as he soon realised that a) he had no play and b) he was staying somewhere without a telephone to warn the theatre, which was about to announce the winter season!</p>

<p>Back in Scarborough, press officer Jeannie Swales recalls the tumultuous day and the call which eventually came just after the brochure had been received by the printer for publication…</p>

<p>“That damned phone call! Alan’s wife,&nbsp;Heather, in a phone&nbsp;box (this was before the days when everyone had a mobile), in Scotland, on a Friday evening! After three or four attempts, I managed to get someone to pick the phone up at the printers (it was probably 7.30pm by then), who basically said he was security, and it was nothing to do with him, and he didn’t know how to get my message to anyone who could help…. it was so frustrating! Still, it makes for a good story…”</p>

<p>Too late to stop the print run, the brochures came out with <em>Private Fears in Public Places</em> advertised as Alan’s 46th full-length play, to be directed by the playwright and running from 27 January to 26 February 1994. The copy, which Alan had written about the play, described it thus:</p>

<p>“At the airport, Jessica waves a fond farewell to her husband. Then a chance encounter changes her life. How well does she know, how far she can trust herself?”</p>

<p>Very little else is known about Alan’s original concept for <em>Private Fears In Public Places</em> other than it was to be set in an airport and there seems to be the possibility the play might have featured an escalator or travelator.</p>

<p>Even more alarming was the fact that the press had got wind of the new season and brochure and they too had gone to print. On 9 September 1993, the Scarborough Evening News announced: “Among them is the new Alan Ayckbourn play <em>Private Fears in Public Places</em>, which he has written and directed.”</p>

<p>The big problem was there was nothing to replace <em>Private Fears in Public Places</em>. Alan had a reserve idea – which appears to have been a non-musical version of <em><strong><a href="/plays/a-word-from-our-sponsor">A Word From Our Sponsor</a></strong></em> – but that too amounted to nothing.</p>

<p>In desperation, it was decided the theatre would probably need to revive a classic Ayckbourn work to cover the gap in the schedule. But then Alan had a revelation for a time-travelling thriller inspired by the likes of the film <em>Psycho</em> and <em>Back to the Future</em>. He rapidly wrote what came to be <em><strong><a href="/plays/communicating-doors">Communicating Doors</a></strong></em>, which was a huge success – despite all the stress which had preceded it.</p>

<p>And what of <em>Private Fears in Public Places</em>? Well, Alan was never one to throw a good title away.  Back in 1971, he apparently had an idea for a play which would be called <strong><em><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></em></strong>. He abandoned the play but thinking the title was too good to waste – and so vague it could cover a multitude of plays – he decided to keep it for his next play, which had a completely different concept to that which he initially imagined.</p>

<p>Likewise, a decade after he’d shelved <em>Private Fears in Public Places</em>, he pulled the title back out and wrote an entirely different play. No airports, no Jessica, just an incredibly clever and moving piece about the lonely existence of 30-somethings in London seeking to make connections.</p>

<p>So – to all you writers out there – never throw away a good title, you never know when you might find it useful!</p>
