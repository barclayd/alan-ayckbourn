<p>As fans of Alan Ayckbourn are no doubt aware, the playwright’s archive is held in the Borthwick Institute for Archives at the University of York.</p>

<p>It is just one part of the Samuel Storey Writing and Performance Collection, which includes other luminaries such as the comedy writers Laurence Marks and Maurice Gran and the York-born Comedian Frankie Howerd.</p>

<p>One of the joys of archiving is discovering the links between people that may not previously be known. Marks and Gran (creators of such TV hits as <em>Birds of a Feather</em> and <em>Goodnight Sweetheart</em>) got an early break writing for Frankie Howerd. They in turn were later advised and encouraged by Alan Ayckbourn when writing their first professionally produced play, <em>Playing God</em>, which premiered in his home theatre in Scarborough.</p>

<p>When it acquired the extensive Frankie Howerd Collection in 2021, the Borthwick Institute even discovered a tenuous link between Frankie Howerd and Alan Ayckbourn as was reported at the time.</p>

<p>Within the collection is a three page, typed manuscript called <em>Introduction for Frankie Howerd by Peter Caulfield</em>; this being a pseudonym used by Alan between 1965 and 1970.</p>

<p>During this time, Alan was working as a Radio Drama Producer for the BBC and his contract prohibited him writing for other television companies. </p>

<p>However, despite working for the Corporation, Alan was raising a family with two children, living in a council flat in Leeds and was overdrawn on his bank account. Between 1965 and 1967, he recalls writing surreptitiously for ‘all manner of outlets back then desperately trying to make ends meet (unsuccessfully)’ – including both Frankie Howerd and Tommy Cooper.</p>

<p>In order to do this, Alan did the obvious thing and pretended to be someone else!</p>

<p>Around 1966 – the playwright is unsure of the exact date – Alan recalls being contacted by David Monroe, the deputy stage manager for the unsuccessful West End transfer of his play <em>Mr Whatnot</em> in 1964. Monroe by then was connected to David Paradine Productions, which had been founded by David Frost in 1966 and had connections with Frankie Howerd.</p>

<p>He wondered whether Alan was interested in writing material for Frankie Howerd’s television show and commissioned him, alongside  several other writers, to ‘audition’ by writing a suitable sketch. Alan needed the money, so agreed.</p>

<p>He recalls the brief was to write a monologue for Howerd who would be in a pulpit, preaching a sermon, which would introduce an episode of the TV show. Alan wrote the three page monologue and submitted it under the pseudonym Peter Caulfield.</p>

<p>He doesn’t recall anything else about the script other than he had no contact with Frankie Howerd himself and that the script itself wasn’t ever used by Howerd. His then partner, now wife, Heather Stoney, believes he did receive some feedback about it in which Howerd criticised Alan for writing his famed ‘ad-libs’ into the script rather than leaving them for Howerd to ‘improvise’.</p>

<p>These three pages were then forgotten until they were discovered in 2021 amongst Frankie Howerd’s Collection at the Borthwick Institute; given Alan’s pseudonym of Peter Caulfield is quite obscure, it was fortunate someone had written ‘alias Alan Ayckbourn’ beside it.</p>

<p>It was a fascinating mini discovery – as the playwright himself had not kept a copy of the monologue – but which also illustrated where Alan was at that time of his life just prior to finding success as a playwright. Following the disastrous West End production of <em>Mr Whatnot</em> in 1964, which closed after two weeks despite starring Ronnie Barker, Alan announced he was giving up playwriting and ‘fled’ to the BBC.</p>

<p>Alan’s financial situation was not alleviated until 1967 when <em>Relatively Speaking</em> opened in the West End to enormous success. Alan recalls soon afterwards being called into his bank for a meeting with the manager who informed him his account had gone from one of the bank’s poorest to the richest almost overnight!</p>

<p>Alan’s only notable achievement using the Peter Caulfield pseudonym came after the success of <em>Relatively Speaking</em> and only because Ronnie Barker asked him for help as he had enjoyed working on <em>Mr Whatnot</em> playing the character of Lord Slingsby-Craddock. Barker later admitted this character was essentially the template for one of his most memorable creations, Lord Rustless, and for <em>Hark At Barker</em>, the comedian commissioned Alan – credited as Peter Caulfield – to write the Rustless bridging material where the character would pontificate on a chosen subject interspaced with sketches written by other writers.</p>

<p><em>Hark at Barker</em> ran between 1969 and 1970 and Alan (or should that be Peter?) and the writing team were nominated for a BAFTA in 1970; Alan not being able to attend the ceremony as that might have given the game away that he was writing for someone he shouldn’t.</p>

<p>As a footnote to all this, Alan actually did meet Frankie Howerd once, briefly, in the strangest of circumstances. On 2 September 1959, the Mayor of Scarborough held a ‘midnight matinee’ at Scarborough’s Futurist Theatre to raise money for World Refugee Year. It involved stars from the town’s theatres presenting material from the summer shows, headlined by Frankie Howerd, who was starring in <em>The Big Show</em> at the Futurist. </p>

<p>Alan was an actor with the Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre company, but had had his first play, <em>The Square Cat</em>, premiered in the town earlier in the summer. The company shared the same dressing room as Howerd, who apparently arrived after his own show, saw how late the charity production was running – he would not take to the stage until past 1.30am – and noted not even a higher power could make an audience laugh at this time of night!</p>

<p>Rather bizarrely, this performance – which was headlined by Howerd and featured such contributions as the Futurist Lovelies, Martin Granger’s Puppets, Bill Maynard and Mollie Sugden – saw Alan and the Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre company present the opening scene of Harold Pinter’s <em>The Birthday Party</em>, which Pinter himself had directed for the company earlier in the year.</p>

<p>Quite how this went down in an evening otherwise dominated by fun and frivolity is not recorded!</p>

<p><em>Introduction for Frankie Howerd by Peter Caulfield</em> is held in the Frankie Howerd Collection at the Borthwick Institute for Archives. Whilst it can be read on site, it has never been published nor performed, nor will permission ever been given for either. It sits alongside many other little-known treasures by both Alan Ayckbourn and Frankie Howerd waiting to be explored and found in their wonderful respective archives.</p>

<p><em>Both The Alan Ayckbourn Collection and The Frankie Howerd Collection are held in the Borthwick Institute of Archives at the University of York and are open to the public to explore and research. Further details can be found <a href="https://www.york.ac.uk/borthwick/">here</a>.</em></p>
