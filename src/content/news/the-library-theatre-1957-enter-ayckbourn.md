<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1957 when an aspiring actor called Alan Ayckbourn joined the company…</p>

<p><strong>1957: Enter Ayckbourn</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>1957 was a notable year for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, although it’s unlikely anyone would have realised that at the time; not least the person involved.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn, aged 17 (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>A young, aspiring actor called Alan Ayckbourn joined the company that summer. He was 18 and had previously been working at the <strong><a href="/career/actor/connaught-theatre">Thorndike Theatre</a></strong> in Leatherhead when his stage manager, Rodney Wood, asked him if he would like to join him working in Scarborough that summer.</p>

<p>Alan had never seen theatre in the round before and was intrigued by the offer. As a result, he and Rodney attended a production by the Scarborough company of Sartre’s <em>Huis Clos</em> at the Mahatma Gandhi Assembly Hall in Fitzroy Square, London, in Spring 1957. The result was profound and, ultimately, life-transforming.</p>

<p><em>“[It] sticks out still in my mind as one of the most exciting things I’d ever seen in the theatre…. It was an absolute knockout. It was a pretty racy play, for its time, you know. And I thought, ‘This is terrific.’ I also liked it because theatre-in-the-round had no scenery and that meant less work.”</em></p>

<p>He agreed to take on the job as an Acting Stage Manager and spent two weeks in rehearsal in London before heading up north to North Yorkshire. He was predominantly employed as a stage manager – with lighting and sound responsibilities – but his role allowed for occasional acting roles, which was the path he aimed to ultimately follow.</p>

<p>The first of these was playing Eric Birling in J.B. Priestley’s&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-19/page-3/page28/page28.html">An Inspector Calls</a></strong></em>. The role did not attract much attention, although Alan was mentioned in passing in several reviews.</p>

<p>More successful was his second role at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre during that same season, playing Jack Bensted in Catherine Prynne’s&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/career/actor/the-ornamental-hermit">The Ornamental Hermit</a></strong></em>. The Times noted: “Mr Alan Ayckbourn at the end gave a lively account of a young country-bred poet of an engaging dishevelment,” The Guardian also said he had made a “good showing.”</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan (right) in An Inspector Calls (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Clearly big things were ahead for this young man.</p>

<p>Though not, as he might have anticipated, as an actor….</p>

<p>In context, Alan Ayckbourn never set out to be the writer and director for which he is now renowned. He left school at 16 and walked straight into a role in <strong><a href="/career/actor/donald-wolfit">Donald Wolfit</a></strong>’s company at the Edinburgh Festival. Wolfit was the last of the great theatre managers and Alan remembers being in utter awe of the man, who apparently dispensed advice on theatre and life in equal measure. Sometimes both at the same time such as the gem, “Drink the Guiness before the show and the gin afterwards.” Sage advice there.</p>

<p>Alan played a soldier for the three weeks the company were resident at Lauriston Hall in&nbsp;<em>The Strong Are Lonely</em>&nbsp;and was employed largely because he could stand to attention for the duration of the play, thanks to his experiences in Haileybury Cadet Force!</p>

<p>From that heady experience during the summer of August 1956, Alan was employed by Melville Gillam at the <strong><a href="/career/actor/connaught-theatre">Connaught Theatre</a></strong> in Worthing as a voluntary assistant stage manager. Although unpaid, Alan spent six months acquainting himself with many aspects of working in the theatre and even had a couple of small roles.</p>

<p>After six months and without any money to speak of – having largely been dependent on the generosity of his mother whilst at Worthing – Alan was quickly employed by Hazel Vincent at the Thorndike Theatre in Leatherhead. There he performed in five roles and believed he had found his vocation as an actor.</p>

<p>Whilst at Leatherhead, Alan became friendly with a stage manager called Rodney Wood, who brought him to Scarborough and – eventually that summer – to meet the founder of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong>. </p>

<p>And the rest was history.</p>

<p>Or nearly not.</p>

<p>For at the end of the summer season, Alan did not stay in Scarborough as he had been offered work by Milos Volankis for the winter season at the <strong><a href="/career/actor/connaught-theatre">Oxford Playhouse</a></strong>. Alan joined the company, happy to be employed just as an actor and fondly remembers his time at the Playhouse; not least something which obviously had a lasting impact on the 20 year old.</p>

<p>“I remember having to help Mai Zetterling, nightly, into the lowest cut evening dress l’ve ever had the good fortune to jam the zip on.”</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>The 1957 company at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>At the end of the winter season, Milos was keen for Alan to stay on at the Oxford Playhouse as he thought he was a promising young actor. But Stephen Joseph had also contacted him and offered him a stage management with increased acting responsibilities in Scarborough for summer 1958.</p>

<p>Despite his fondness for Oxford, Alan returned to Scarborough where he would stay until 1962, when he helped Stephen Joseph found the Victoria Theatre in Stoke-on-Trent. Of course, he would later return to Scarborough where he became inextricably associated with the company.</p>

<p>Had it not been for that decision to leave Oxford, who knows what direction Alan Ayckbourn’s life would have taken? Certainly without the influence of Stephen Joseph, Alan would probably have remained an actor rather than being encouraged to write and direct and discovering his true theatrical vocation.</p>

<p>And in its 70th year, we mustn’t lose sight of that fact. Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre launched Alan Ayckbourn as one of the most successful British playwrights of the 20th century – and all because he thought theatre in the round meant an easier life as a stage manager!</p>
