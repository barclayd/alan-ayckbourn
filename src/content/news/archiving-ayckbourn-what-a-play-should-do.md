<p><em>It’s been a while since we’ve had an Archiving Ayckbourn article on the blog, but this recent discovery is too good not to share.</em></p>

<p><em>Whilst filing old correspondence I came across this interesting response to a question posed to the playwright.</em></p>

<p><em>What impact do you want your plays to have?</em></p>

<p><em>Alan responds by saying there is no simple answer to this as each different play is bound to have a different expectation. But he concludes the letter with what he hopes every play should bring to an audience.</em></p>

<p><em>This is reprinted here for the first time since he sent it in 1987.</em></p>

<p><strong>My Plays</strong> by Alan Ayckbourn</p>

<p>All plays, I feel, however lighthearted, should seek to say something about human nature – the human condition, if you like. Why we behave like we do to each other, hurt people we profess to love and, perversely, fall in love with people we profess to hate.</p>

<p>My canvas like, say, Jane Austen’s is small. But she is a writer  like Chekhov whom I admire in that they say so much with such economy.</p>

<p>My plays are theatre plays and at the end of each performance the audience should leave feeling they have attended a splendid party where they’ve not only met new people but also discovered fresh things about those they already knew – including on occasions themselves. And they should feel lifted by that knowledge.</p>

<p>Of course, theatre being theatre it never works out quite like that all the time. There are dreadful performances when no-one seems to have wanted to be there in the first place – least of all the actors!</p>

<p>But that’s the joy and the frustration of live theatre. Never quite the same unlike film, but again unlike film, always having the possibility of rising to fresh heights at each performance.</p>

<p><em>Alan Ayckbourn, 1987</em></p>
