<p>Alan Ayckbourn’s latest<em> </em>play, <em>Show &amp; Tell</em>, is currently playing at the <strong><a href="https://sjt.uk.com/events/show-and-tell-by-alan-ayckbourn">Stephen Joseph Theatre</a></strong>, Scarborough, before heading out on tour next week.</p>

<p>On 20 September, a post-show Q&amp;A was held with Alan Ayckbourn and the <em>Show &amp; Tell</em> company answering questions about the playwright’s 90th play.</p>

<p>The Ayckbourn Blog is delighted to present some highlights of the evening in this edited transcript offering the playwright and actors’ insights into the play. On the night, Sir Alan was joined by his company of Bill Champion, Paul Kemp, Frances Marshall, Richard Stacey and Olivia Woolhouse along with the stage management team of Fleur Linden Beeley, Sue Volans and Anna Reid.</p>

<p>For context, <em>Show &amp; Tell</em> is based around a theatre company, established in the ’60s, which performs plays in people’s homes. They are hired to perform a French farce – <em>A Friend Indeed</em> – by a man with a deteriorating mental condition who hallucinates his wife is still with him and who he believes he is buying this as a birthday present for.</p>

<p><strong>Show &amp; Tell – Q&amp;A</strong> (Edited transcription compiled by Simon Murgatroyd)</p>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn:</strong> So that’s my 90th, <strong><em><a href="/plays/show-and-tell">Show &amp; Tell</a></em></strong>. They don ’t get any easier to write! The easiest play to write is obviously the first one, which nobody expects to go well!</p>

<p>I’m crafty enough these days to always stay a play ahead. So those of you who’ve read the newspaper articles will know that I’m already preparing number 91, which is written and it should be here next year.</p>

<p>The point about writing – for me – is to always try not to repeat yourself. Just try and always keep ahead of the game. The nicest thing people can say to me is, ‘I didn’t see that coming.’ So that people who say, ‘Oh, I saw that coming a mile off”, is a very disappointing result from a play. But if I can keep the narrative spinning enough to surprise people with enough twists and turns that you don’t anticipate what’s coming – I love that game of playing with a narrative.</p>

<p><em>[Enter company]</em></p>

<p>We have a forum now. So it’s our chance to hear from you. In a play, there’s two vital elements. One of the vital elements is you, the audience – which is said in a speech in <em>Show &amp; Tell</em> – the two vital elements of the theatre are the actors and the audience. There’s a lot of me, my philosophy about theatre, in this piece.</p>

<p>When I first started to approach this play I thought, what am I going to write for number 90? And I thought, what am I most fond of, what’s been occupying me my whole life? And I came to the conclusion that the most beloved thing – not people – in my life has been theatre. And I’ve never been far away from the theatre, thank goodness, since I was 17 years old.</p>

<p>I’ve been in various roles – a bit like Ben in the play – I started as an acting assistant stage manager and worked my way through various branches of theatre, including acting and the beloved directing, of course, which I really enjoyed. And then the writing just developed with the directing.</p>

<p><strong>Audience:</strong> Is it inspiring or intimidating as an actor to have a playwright direct a play?</p>

<p><em>[Actors look to Alan and themselves. Laughter]</em></p>

<p><strong>Bill Champion:</strong> What more need be said? I’d say it’s not intimidating, because one of the many extraordinary things about Alan is that you forget he wrote it. Because he’s a director who’s not dictatorial. He doesn’t impose on you. He’s employed you for a reason and he wants to see what you’re going to do with the play.</p>

<p>And also – I’m assuming this is genuine – he sort of discovers the play along with you, which is extraordinary, really. Occasionally, you’ll remember who he is and go, ‘oh, OK’. But for the most part, it’s not intimidating at all, because he’s just a director of the play.</p>

<p><strong>Paul Kemp:</strong> But at least when you get a note, you know that it’s what the author intended!</p>

<p><strong>Audience:</strong> Has the play changed much as you’ve been doing it? Did you devise any of it?</p>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn:</strong> No. I’m not very good at changing things in rehearsal. When you start writing – early in your career – everybody has a good idea except you. When I started my first play, the cleaners came in and were suggesting good lines! And so I succumbed to that. I was very new and inexperienced. The first few plays of mine, I think probably a tenth were written by other people! Their lines were the funny ones that just popped in. But as I grew older, I stopped listening to them. The later plays are 99% me.</p>

<p>We don’t change much in rehearsals, if at all. Experience has shown me that when I get it wrong, I get it seriously wrong.</p>

<p>The mistakes you make as a young writer – you make technical, practical mistakes. You don’t give a person enough time on stage to make a cup of tea and to pour it. And so, as you get on, that becomes quite obvious – I need so many lines to do that. So the practicalities of the playwriting become quite easy to master as you get on.</p>

<p>However, if there’s something inherently wrong in the play, my instinct is to want to throw it straight out of the window – the whole lot. Because if I make a mistake, it’s a serious one. It’s one that the play will not survive. But tea and drinks are poured confidently all the way through!</p>

<p><strong>Audience:</strong> Which part of <em>Show &amp; Tell</em> did you like best? Which was most fun? The first part of the play or the second part of the play? I like the second act, it looked like fun to do.</p>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn:</strong> I liked it all!</p>

<p>I said something rather pretentious by the time we got to the end of rehearsals. This play is a bit like a piece of music. The first scene is allegro moderato. And the second scene is an adagio. And then you get to the first scene of the second act, which is a scherzo. And then you get to the final scene which is an allegro vivace – this is when the farce <em>A Friend Indeed</em> is played.</p>

<p>So all the scenes are different, but they’re all related and you couldn’t have part two without part one because most of the work, most of the references, in part two stem from what happens in part one.</p>

<p>There’s a lot of exploration of the theme of Jack’s world being an invented world. And he populates his world with invisible characters, as we see in the last scene. And it’s an interesting parallel to the way that the actors within the play also create the world. Those two parallel strands reflect each other quite strongly.</p>

<p>That said, I like the first act better.</p>

<p><strong>Audience:</strong> Olivia, I believe from the programme that you’ve trained at the Guildford School of Acting, and you are making your professional debut. What would you take away from your experience?</p>

<p><strong>Olivia Woolhouse:</strong> I think working with Alan and seeing his burning passion for theatre has been so inspiring at this point in my career. I always think ahead a lot, and I’m like, “Oh, what’s the longevity of a career in theatre?” And seeing someone like Alan who’s written a lot, it makes me excited going forward, whatever happens, if I choose to, I can find ways to stay here for as long as I can.</p>

<p>And working with all of the actors here – everyone’s been around for a while, who again have lots of experiences that they’ve brought to this. Because it’s a play about theatre, what better way to come into this than with people who have experiences of their own to share?</p>

<p>And with the style of direction and hearing so many stories from everyone about their experiences, it just fuels what this is about, really.</p>

<p><strong>Paul Kemp:</strong> She’s had to put up with a lot of stories.</p>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn: </strong>The longer you stay in this company, the more you’ll hear my stories.</p>

<p><strong>Frances Marshall:</strong> During rehearsals, we were doing that game of ‘six degrees of separation’, and you – Alan – were telling a story about <strong><a href="/career/actor/donald-wolfit">Donald Wolfit</a></strong>, who you worked with. I thought, you’re telling Olivia this, and she’s just starting out, and it’s one degree away from Donald Wolfit, and that’s fantastic, that’s amazing! To have that, I think that’s just unbelievable, brilliant.</p>

<p><strong>Audience:</strong> I was wondering, are there still theatre companies like the one in the play that sort of turn up at people’s houses and rock walls?</p>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn:</strong> We’ve got one coming next Friday – we’ve got some people who do this as a thing. They wrote to me and said, ‘I think you must have read about us. We’d like to come and see your play.’ I hope they’re not going to sue me! But, I made it up, really.</p>

<p>But the Arnold Wesker references about Theatre 42 are real. There’s no word of a lie. I remember, in 1961, we – the Scarborough company – were all plonked into a van at Regent’s Park station. We went to Wellingborough, which was, at that time – I don’t know what it’s like now – but at that time was an arts desert. We performed three anti-nuclear plays by David Campton; pretty grim pieces about babies being affected by nuclear fallout and things like that. It was very dark stuff – Comedies of Menace as they were dubbed.</p>

<p>Meanwhile, there was a very hearty folk group actually going around driving people out of the pubs. Because they’d go, ‘Whoa! What’s this?’ And there was this sight of this folk group pursuing their audience from pub to pub! And there was a whole raft of experimental films being done in the local cinema, which was totally empty. And I thought, ‘This isn’t working. We’re never going to get an audience.’</p>

<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/2024-show-tell-03-c2a9-tony-bartholomew-w350.jpg" alt="" width="350" height="233" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Paul Kemp &amp; Frances Marshall in Show &amp; Tell (© Tony Bartholomew <a href="http://www.bartpics.co.uk">www.bartpics.co.uk</a>)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>I was a bit like the actor, Peter, in <em>Show &amp; Tell</em>, saying ‘this is rubbish’. And then, of course, this damn great coach load of people came down from Hampstead. And so we had this imported audience, but not a single Wellingborough-er was there to watch a single thing!</p>

<p>So it was a very strange and, I think, well-meaning, but in the end a disastrous experiment to bring art to the people. It’s a small step from patronage to patronising, as I say in the play too. Saying to people, ‘this will do you good. You may not enjoy it, but this will do you good. And the nastier the play is, the better it is for you.’</p>

<p>Whereas I think that if we’d have done something like <em>A Friend Indeed</em> from <em>Show &amp; Tell</em> or something like that, they might have enjoyed that.</p>

<p><strong>Audience: </strong>If, as you nearly said earlier, the main point of theatre is to entertain, how important is it to say something important?</p>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn: </strong>I think you can say important things if you entertain. I hope this play says important things. And I hope most of my plays say important things. But you don’t have to nail them to or fire them at the audience. You don’t have to point a large accusatory finger at the audience and say, ‘You are responsible for this!’</p>

<p>But you must, in the end, say something. You must put some meat in the stew. It’s very important to say something important.</p>

<p><strong>Bill Champion:</strong> My favourite quote that somebody ever said about Alan’s plays – somebody stopped Alan in the bar and said, “If I’d known what I was laughing at, I wouldn’t have laughed.”</p>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn:</strong> Thank you all very much.</p>
