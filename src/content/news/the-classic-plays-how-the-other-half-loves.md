<p>With the <strong><a href="https://www.oldvictheatre.com/stage/other-half/">The Old Vic</a></strong>‘s imminent revival of Alan Ayckbourn’s classic play <em>How The Other Half Loves</em>, the blog takes a look at the history of the play and its place in the Ayckbourn canon.</p>

<p><strong>The Classic Plays: <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> </strong>by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>“<em>How The Other Half Loves</em> really sort of established me. Everyone always says, if you’re doing your first play and it’s a success, ‘Oh well, he’s maybe a one-play wonder’, but once you’ve got a second one in, they say, ‘Oh he’s got some legs’.”</p>

<p><em>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p><em>How The Other Half Loves</em> is Alan Ayckbourn’s ninth play and is regarded as the second of his classic works after <em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></strong></em> (1965).</p>

<p>The latter had established Ayckbourn as playwright to watch when it opened in the West End in 1967 to rave reviews and had subsequently gone on to success both nationally and internationally with its first UK tour, regional productions and international productions all taking place in 1968.</p>

<p>But there was no immediate follow-up to this success. Alan’s next play, <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-sparrow">The Sparrow</a></strong></em> (1967) premiered in Scarborough and vanished without trace and it would be 1969 before he wrote his next play with <em>How The Other Half Loves</em>.</p>

<p>This play marked a pivotal moment in his career and the point when theatre would begin to dominate his professional career and life.</p>

<p>But this was not a guaranteed path. In early 1969, Alan – despite the success of <em>Relatively Speaking</em> – was living in a council flat near Leeds with his young family. He had been working full-time for the <strong><a href="/career/bbc-career">BBC</a></strong> as a Radio Drama Producer since 1965 and was well regarded, working particularly closely with the renowned northern producer <strong><a href="/life/influences/bradley-alfred">Alfred Bradley</a></strong>.</p>

<p>Alan was at a crossroads. He had a desire to return to theatre, where he had begun his professional career in 1956 at the age of 17 and where he worked through to 1964. But after the West End failure of <em><strong><a href="/plays/mr-whatnot">Mr Whatnot</a></strong></em> in 1964, Alan had retreated to the BBC and done relatively little theatrical work, writing just two plays and directing only two productions between 1965 and 1969.</p>

<p>In 1968, Alan had worked tirelessly at the BBC producing some of his most renowned work including acclaimed productions of Roy Clark’s &nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/career/bbc-career/bbc-radio-productions/the-events-at-black-tor">The Events at Black Tor</a></em></strong>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/career/bbc-career/bbc-radio-productions/the-17-jewelled-shock-proof">The 17-Jewelled Shockproof Swiss-Made Bomb</a></em></strong> as well as being nominated for the prestigious Prix Italia for his production of Don Haworth’s <strong><a href="/career/bbc-career/bbc-radio-productions/we-all-come-to-it-in-the-end">We All Come To It In The End</a></strong>.</p>

<p>In contrast, his only involvement with theatre that year was directing an adaptation of <em><strong><a href="/career/directing-career/the-wizard-of-oz">The Wizard of Oz</a></strong></em>, written by Alfred Bradley, for children that summer at York Theatre Royal.</p>

<p>However, a window of opportunity was about to open that would draw Alan firmly back into the very theatre which had launched him as both writer and director and would set the stage for his most notable success yet.</p>

<p>Alan’s major influence in his early life has been the theatre pioneer <strong><a href="/life/stephen-joseph">Stephen Joseph</a></strong>, who Alan met when he joined his company, Studio Theatre Ltd, at <a href="https://www.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-187/page-14/"><strong>Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</strong></a> in Scarborough in 1957. Stephen had encouraged this aspiring young actor to both write and direct and Alan had come to regard Stephen as his mentor.</p>

<p>But Stephen had died in 1967 and The Library Theatre was struggling in the wake of this. It was only operating during summer seasons with an annually appointed Director of Productions / Artistic Director. In late 1968, the Scarborough Theatre Trust board approached Alan about taking on the role for summer 1969 as well as writing a new play for the season.</p>

<p>There was no money to pay him – accommodation in the town would be produced for free – and no guarantee it would lead to a more permanent role in Scarborough. That Alan took the job speaks highly of his loyalty to Stephen and desire to keep his legacy alive.</p>

<p>In hindsight, Alan admits that this marked the beginning of the end of his work at the BBC and it’s likely he would have returned full-time to a theatrical career regardless of whether it was in Scarborough or not. Although Alan resigned from the BBC in early 1970, he revealed in 2026 he was essentially absent without leave through the summer of 1969 and his guilt at this fed heavily into his decision to resign.</p>

<p>Alan had begun programming the Scarborough season in early spring 1969 intending to produce five plays, of which he would direct four including his then untitled new play. In fact, the season consisted of just four plays</p>

<p>Rehearsals began on 16 June with the repertory season opening on 23 June. Alan directed the first and third productions – <strong><em><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-2/page-108/page-146/page-262/page347.html">The Dynamic Death-Defying Leap of Timothy Saturn the Great</a></em></strong>, which opened the season, and <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-2/page-108/page-146/page-263/page348.html">A Little Stiff Built Chap</a></strong></em> by Leonard Barras, which opened on 3 July; rehearsals were generally between one to two weeks per production. The second production, <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-2/page-108/page-146/page-264/page349.html">Ask Me Tomorrow</a></strong></em> by Stan Barstow and Alfred Bradley, opened on 26 June directed by Pamela Craig.</p>

<p>The title of Alan’s new play – <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> – was finally announced in The Stage on 3 July and he began writing it soon after this date. He completed it a week later before directing it over a two week period.</p>

<p>If this seems quick, it was. But to appreciate Alan’s working pattern at the time, it has to be acknowledged that his plays gestated in his head for several months and he would only write them when fully formed. Never one who enjoyed the writing process, Alan needed a strict deadline to motivate him to put pencil to paper. No deadline was arguably more motivating that the start of rehearsals a week ahead!</p>

<p>The play is also quite unusual in that it has autobiographical elements with its ground-breaking structure significantly informed by Alan’s own experiences living in Leeds.</p>

<p>“My secretary had just got married and wanted a flat, and I said ‘There’s one going two doors down’. So she got that one. Of course the flats were identically designed and unless you were some weird eccentric you had to put your furniture in a certain way – your television there because of the plug in the wall there, and the dining hatch was there. Often I’d go for a drink with her and her husband and one would say ‘I wonder which flat we’re in’. And I started to think that if you actually ripped the lid off those boxes and looked in, you’d see people treading these identical little patterns, wearing out the same bit of carpet. I was also thinking ‘I don’t think I can write a full-length play this year. I’m a bit exhausted’. So I thought ‘I’ll try writing two at once. Let’s be clever’. So I got this family going this way, and this the other, and eventually they tied up.”</p>

<p>This led to the idea of a composite set with two spaces overlaid each other allowing action to occur simultaneously in two different places. To this, he then added the memorable twist for one scene of having one couple simultaneously attend two events at two different locations on two different days. It is widely credited as a theatrical innovation by Alan, having never been attempted before.</p>

<p>What is also hugely significant and frequently ignored is the play was written and conceived for performance in-the-round. To this day, Alan is adamant, the play is nowhere near as successful when seen end-stage or proscenium arch. The play works by the entire audience looking down – as it were – on this composite set and seeing the locations and characters overlaid each other. To his mind, whilst the play has been very successful in the proscenium, most designers lose the elegant simplicity achievable in-the-round and overly complicate the play to communicate the idea of the shared space.</p>

<p>It is perhaps astonishing to note that The Old Vic’s forthcoming production will mark the first time the play has been performed as intended in-the-round in the West End.</p>

<p>Other influences in shaping the play, according to Alan, were: “being drawn into the comet’s tail of somebody else’s breaking marriage”, being “fascinated by the uses of distortion of time and space; especially the comic potentialities” and a desire to “write a play which highlighted different and contrasting social lifestyles.”</p>

<p>The play was written for an ensemble of six, all drawn from the rep company Alan had chosen for The Library Theatre season. It consisted of Elizabeth Ashton, Colin Edwynn, Jeremy Franklin, Brian Miller, Elsabeth Sladen and Stephanie Turner with Alan directing the play.</p>

<p>‘Ensemble’ is the significant term here as the perception of the play quickly became something it was never intended to be. <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> is, objectively, an ensemble piece and no one character takes precedence or has a greater amount of lines than any other.</p>

<p>However, when Robert Morley was cast as Frank in the West End production in 1971, he came to utterly dominate the play, altering lines and placing all the emphasis on him. It did extraordinary damage to the piece and how it was perceived until the early 1980s when it received a welcome re-evaluation and it became obvious this was not a play centred on or about the character of Frank Foster.</p>

<p>Back in Scarborough, <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> opened on 31 July 1969 at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, performed in-the-round and the next day, the Scarborough Evening News declared the play “hilarious.” In the same review, Alan was quoted with an eye to pulling in fans of its famed predecessor.</p>

<p>“I suppose it’s inevitable that comparisons will be drawn between this and&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>. In fact, it is an exploration of the same sort of theme, but with a few decided differences.”</p>

<p>Other reviews of the play are scarce, but in correspondence Alan noted: “Reviews have been pretty good – Guardian (aside from a rather snide sentence), Yorkshire Post, Express and the locals.” The premiere was also attended by Alan’s London producer Peter Bridge who felt it had “all the makings of another&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speakin</em>g”; although later actions would betray his discomfort with certain aspects of the play.</p>

<p>Bridge’s reaction to the play was largely predicted by Alan’s agent&nbsp;<strong><a href="/life/influences/ramsay-margaret">Margaret Ramsay</a></strong>&nbsp;– better known as Peggy – after she had read the script. She liked the play but wondered whether it was suitable for the proscenium arch and, as a result, whether Peter Bridge would be really interested in the piece. In hindsight, this was perhaps the first indication that Bridge’s tenure as Alan’s producer was not going to be a long term affair. In fact, it would unravel throughout the course of&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em>‘ original London production and it was the final Ayckbourn play he would produce.</p>

<p><em>How The Other Half Loves</em> was a huge success for Scarborough and, essentially, marked the start of a new phase of Alan Ayckbourn’s career. He was now essentially embedded with the Scarborough company and in 1970 would again accept the role of Director of Productions for the summer season. Two years later, he would be offered the full-time position of Artistic Director of the company, which he would hold until 2009.</p>

<p>The original production also marked the end of a previous career which he had not thought to resume…</p>

<p>Soon after the play opened in Scarborough and Alan had returned to Leeds, he received a phone call from his stage manager. Jeremy Franklin, the actor playing Frank Foster, had slipped a disc and was in Scarborough Hospital in traction for two weeks. Alan rushed back to Scarborough and took to the stage for that evening’s performance, ironically as the author, having to read from the book. All seemed fine until, alarmingly, as the play reached its climax Alan discovered his script was missing pages.</p>

<p>“I distinguished myself… by actually losing several pages during the action and having to ad-lib a scene, My fellow actors confronted by the sight of the author-director in full-flow, spouting fresh dialogue, stood uncertainly about convinced that these must be new re-writes about which they hadn’t been told. I later rushed off, jotted it down and it’s in there somewhere to this day.”<br><br>The next day, Alan learnt the part afresh and performed it for the remaining three performances that week. Fortunately, the play was in repertory and was not due to return for another two weeks, by which point Jeremy Franklin had recovered and returned to the role. It was only four performances, but five years after he had officially retired from acting,&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em> proved to be Alan’s ultimate on-stage acting swan-song.</p>

<p>The success of <em>How The Other Half Loves </em>in Scarborough did not, however, guarantee its future. Incredible as it may be to consider, given this is one of the most popular and perfumed Ayckbourn works, there initially was no commitment by Peter Bridge to take it any further.</p>

<p>By the time the play closed at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in September 1969, Peter Bridge had not optioned the play and seemed very wary of it. Given that Bridge had previously optioned <em>The Sparrow</em> and, two years on, there was no movement on it at all, Alan was pessimistic that <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> was going to be consigned to history too. Alan wrote to Bridge that month to express concern.</p>

<p>“People I know reckon it could be a winner and I’m very anxious it shouldn’t go the way of&nbsp;<em>The Sparrow</em>,&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/standing-room-only">Standing Room Only</a></em></strong>&nbsp;or even&nbsp;<em>Mr Whatnot</em>!”</p>

<p>In an attempt to mollify Alan, Bridge signed a contract but was then ominously silent about his intentions, leading Alan to despair in November that he could “only assume that he’s [Bridge] cooled off on this one as well…. I must say I find the ecstatic almost festive air of congratulations which surrounded&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>&nbsp;in bewildering contrast to the total disinterest for the latest one.”</p>

<p>It’s odd to think that a play so closely associated for more than six decades with Alan was not viewed as a potential success. It later transpired, the problem was Bridge could not work out how it would transfer successfully into the proscenium.</p>

<p>By the time <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> stormed to success in the West End in 1970, Peter Bridge’s days as Alan’s London producer were rapidly coming to a conclusion. His lack of faith in Alan’s work or vision in the potential of <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> never forgotten by either Alan or Margaret Ramsay even once the royalties began pouring in.</p>

<p>For <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> would go on to phenomenal success, eclipsing <em>Relatively Speaking </em>in the West End and truly cementing Alan’s reputation as one of the hottest young playwriting talents in the country.</p>

<p>Within three years, it had become a repertory staple in this country, had transferred to Broadway and was being performed internationally as far afield as Australia. It had become an instant classic and has continued to be popular ever since.</p>
