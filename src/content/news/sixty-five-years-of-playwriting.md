<p>Sixty-five years ago today, Alan Ayckbourn’s first play premiered at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in Scarborough.</p>

<p>It’s fair to say, he would have no concept then that 65 years and 90 plays he would have become one of the country’s most successful living playwrights – especially when at the time, his focus was entirely on his acting career!</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn in 1957 (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>To mark the occasion, here’s a look at how Alan Ayckbourn’s professional playwriting career began.</p>

<p>Alan had never had any grand intentions to become a playwright. In 1959, he’d been acting with the <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> company for two years and working its way up its ranks. At the time, there was no conscious desire to become anything other than an actor. </p>

<p><em>“I have never made any decisions; they have always been made for me. I could look back on my life and say I planned it that way, but I didn’t plan to be an actor, nor a director, nor a writer. They ran out of writers!”</em></p>

<p>That’s not to say Alan suddenly became a playwright. He admits he had been interested in writing for many years – inspired by his mother, who was a very successful writer in her right and had bought Alan his very first typewriter and a child.</p>

<p><em>“Story-telling was part of my upbringing – probably the result during my early years of having a mother as a writer who also spent most of her time writing in order to feed our single parent family of two! What choice did I have?”</em></p>

<p>Since joining the Library Theatre, Alan had been writing plays inspired by some of his favourite writers. The company’s founder, <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> – who would had an inordinate influence in Alan’s life and career – encouraged everyone in the company to try their hand at writing. Alan, with his background, was keen to give it a go even if his focus was on acting. However, he had occasionally shown his mentor the results of his efforts.</p>

<p><em>“Certainly I was tapping out plays in my early teens and had written a good half dozen before I had my first one professionally produced.”</em></p>

<p>When it came to commissioning Alan’s first play, Stephen was well aware of Alan’s nascent ability and had the perfect riposte for him when the opportunity presented itself. It was the winter season of 1958 and Alan was starring in David Campton’s <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-19/page-3/page44/page44.html">Ring of Roses</a></strong></em>; a role he did not enjoy. He made clear his displeasure to Stephen Joseph.</p>

<p><em>“Stephen had the complete, some would say lunatic, disregard in allowing me to write for him. The story goes that I came off stage one night and said that I could write much better than what I had just acted in and he told me to get on with it then.”</em></p>

<p>There was a catch though – Stephen told him to write a play and he would consider staging it, but on the understanding that Alan would take the lead role. A form of quality control as Stephen gambled that Alan would not write a play which did not show him off in a good way.</p>

<p>Having created a company dedicated to new writing, Stephen was well aware there were inherent advantages in encouraging members of the company to write. Not least they were already on contract – the cost of commissioning a playwright to write a new play would have been beyond the company’s means at the time., but if there were already on the payroll…</p>

<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn in The Square Cat (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Interestingly, Stephen’s challenge was made during the winter season when Alan was also in rehearsals for the company’s brief winter tour.</p>

<p>The play he was rehearsing was by and being directed by a relatively unknown writer who had just suffered his first West End flop. Harold Pinter had been invited to Scarborough to direct <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-19/page-3/page68/page68.html">The Birthday Party</a></strong></em> following its critical mauling in London. This would only be the second time it had been professionally staged and featured Alan in the role of Stanley with Pinter making his professional directorial debut.</p>

<p>The play may not have directly influenced Alan’s first play, but its author certainly inspired Alan.</p>

<p>“I got fascinated by Pinter’s use of dialogue, his use of words, the structure of sentences. You can see even now what’s actually rubbed off on me from him.”</p>

<p>Alan probably wrote his first play,<em><strong><a href="/plays/the-square-cat"> The Square Cat</a></strong></em>, during the course of the tour, but it was not a sole effort. Alan’s partner and soon to be wife, Christine Roland, worked together with him, offering advice on the play.</p>

<p><em>“It was untypical of me in that firstly, it was written over quite a long period, and secondly, it was written with a great deal of help from Christine, structurally, not dialogue-wise. She was very helpful. We talked out: ‘What if … ? What if … ? What if … ? How about trying it this way round?’ And it was very much an exercise, in the sense that we were trying to get effects.”</em></p>

<p>The pseudonym Roland Allen (Christine Roland / Alan Ayckbourn) was used by Alan for his first four plays, although Christine was only involved in helping the creation of <em>The Square Cat</em> and <strong><em><a href="/plays/love-after-all">Love After All</a></em></strong>.</p>

<p><em>The Square Cat</em> was unashamedly a showcase for Alan as an actor. His part involved two roles which he would quick-change between: the rock ‘n’ roll star Jerry Wattis and the mild-mannered Arthur Brummage. In fact, most of the first act is spent building up this character who then rarely leaves the stage. It was a showcase for Alan, not as writer but as an actor.</p>

<p><em>“I came on in act one and stayed on, with all the best lines, until the end, and I danced and sang and played the guitar – none of which I was very good at. It was an immensely practical way to start. I learned a great deal from seeing the same bits die every night.”</em></p>

<p>In fact, one of the great mysteries is why Alan the writer wrote something for Alan the actor which centred own things he knew he couldn’t do! Singing, acting and playing the guitar. He had absolutely no experience with the guitar and didn’t even have one to play in the run-up to the production.</p>

<p><em>“I did actually set myself as an actor an impossible task, yes. I did originally play a guitar, and sing a song, and dance – none of which I do at all well. A bloke called Donny taught me about five chords, in order that I could sing&nbsp;‘I Gave My Love A Cherry’, which seemed a nice, extremely boring, morose song which goes on for ages. But even that I used to have very great difficulty with. I used to sing it on some … but on other nights, I would nod vaguely in the direction of the lighting box and they’d take the lights out rather swiftly; so I would just play one open chord – sploing – and the lights would go. And other nights I would sing, excruciatingly,&nbsp;I Gave My Love A Cherry&nbsp;to a rather flat guitar, because I hadn’t actually learnt to tune it.</em>“</p>

<p><em>The Square Cat</em> opened at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, on 30 July 1959 and was a big hit with the summer audiences. The surprise success and demand for tickets – apparently it was booked to capacity for all performances – led Stephen Joseph to cancel a week’s performance of David Campton’s adaptation of <em><strong>Frankenstein</strong></em> (which had not been well received) and scheduled a second week for <em>The Square Cat</em>’s second repertory run the following month; the first time a play in repertory had run for two consecutive weeks at the venue.</p>

<p>For Alan, the over-riding memory of the experience was the pay-cheque at the end.</p>

<p>“It made me forty-seven quid, I remember, more than I earned in several weeks. It proved very popular because it was what it was – a farce, with no pretensions to anything else – and it did give people quite a laugh.”</p>

<p>But it had a far more long-lasting effect because Stephen Joseph had found a young writer who could apparently write a play which attracted audiences – he was not about to let that go. Almost immediately after the season ended, Stephen commissioned Alan to write a follow-up for the company’s winter season giving him less than two months to come up with and wrote a new play.</p>

<p>Which he did with <em>Love After All</em>, which was also a success for the company and playwright. And with that, in 1959, it kickstarted an unexpected career.</p>

<p>By 1964, Alan had retired from acting and in 1967 he had his first major West End hit with <em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></strong></em>. 65 years on from <em>The Square Cat</em>, he has written 90 full-length produced works, numerous other works including revues, adaptations, plays fore children, a novel and a book on playwriting.</p>

<p>And all because of a play, which opened 65 years ago today, which he hoped would propel his acting career to a new level.</p>

<p>Here’s to 90 plays and 65 years of playwriting!</p>
