<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1965 and the decision to close Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre by its founder, Stephen Joseph.</p>

<p><strong>1965: The Final Year?</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>In 1965, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre – home to the UK’s first professional theatre-in-the-round company – closed its doors for apparently the final time.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Stephen Joseph, founder of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Stephen Joseph, who had founded the company in 1955, to his mind firmly brought to a close his theatre experiment after 11 years. The closure was neither a threat, a bluff nor actually a surprise. The writing had been on the wall for several years, ever since 1963 when Stephen had written an ultimatum to Scarborough Town Council stating the future of the company was in danger if better facilities at the library were not provided or better support offered.</p>

<p>It was always a source of frustration to Stephen that none of these issues were either acknowledged or addressed by the council in subsequent months, as he notes in his book <em>Theatre In The Round</em> (1967, Barrie &amp; Rockliff) looking at how the company had fared since 1955.</p>

<p><em>“The new company in Scarborough immediately tried to put the summer seasons on a firmer footing, by negotiating a regular booking at the Library, by making improvements to the theatre and its facilities, and by seeking co-operation with the local authority in the shape of a regular financial grant, of help with publicity, ticket sales and drawing attention to the existence and place of the theatre. The corporation were prepared to give a grant, but only on an annual basis, without any undertaking to repeat it; and they blew hot and cold on each and every other proposal.</em>“</p>

<p>In context, Stephen appears to have run out of reasons to keep the Library Theatre running. In 1962, he had opened the UK’s first professional theatre-in-the-round venue at the <strong><a href="/career/victoria-theatre">Victoria Theatre</a></strong>, Stoke-on-Trent. That was now establishing itself well and, in itself, had caused issues as Scarborough had lost its arts council funding with the opening of the Victoria.</p>

<p>The Scarborough company was also, arguably, moving backwards not forwards. By 1961, it had both a thriving summer and winter season at the library plus an extensive winter touring season. By 1965, this had been reduced to just a shortened summer season which ran for not much longer than its first two seasons.</p>

<p>The lack of financial support from both the arts council and the Town Council meant the 1965 season was significantly affected in two ways. Firstly, a production was dropped and the season began in July, three weeks later than recent summer seasons.</p>

<p>Secondly, to save money – or to make the most of what little was available – the season was produced in association with the University of Manchester. Half of the company was recruited from the student ranks of Stephen’s drama course – an incredible opportunity for the students who were involved in every aspect of the operation from acting and stage management to promotion and front of house. But this wasn’t sustainable or viable for the long term of a professional company.</p>

<p>And Stephen was increasingly in poor health, which would lead to an imminent diagnosis of terminal cancer and, ultimately, his untimely death in 1967.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Promotional postcard for Meet My Father (held in the Borthwick Institute for Archives)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>The 1965 summer season opened on 5 July with <strong><em>Granite</em></strong> by Clemence Dane followed on 8 July by Alan Ayckbourn’s latest play, <em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Meet My Father</a></strong></em>. </p>

<p>A day later, Stephen addressed the Scarborough Theatre Trust board and announced there would be no performances in 1966 and that, unless an alternative venue could be found, the company would close – as the minutes to the meeting show.</p>

<p><em>“It was, he [Stephen Joseph] said, only the fact that the actors and staff worked for very low wages that it was possible to run the theatre in Scarborough. This year they could not run the full season (not commencing until 5 July). They had not received full support from the local authority; there was no chance of any expansion at the library and unless other premises were found it was doubtful the company would return in 1966.”</em></p>

<p>There’s a strange irony in that the play which opened the day before this announcement we now know as <em>Relatively Speaking</em> – the first of Alan Ayckbourn’s major West End hits and which would throw a financial lifeline out to the company in 1967.</p>

<p>As far as Stephen was concerned though, the Scarborough project had ended. In his book, <em>Theatre In The Round</em>, he wrote about the decision to close Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p><em>“The odd thing about Scarborough is that the corporation runs its own theatres and puts on its own entertainments, and they have never decided if we are a rival company that should be driven out of town, or a good addition to the wide range of attractions that a seaside town wants to boast of. Somewhere between these two extremes is the position from which no action is taken…. It became clear that a place that was admirable for the first steps, had been outgrown entirely; and just as there is discomfort in wearing clothes that are too small, and embarrassment in wearing fashions that have just gone out, so the company felt increasingly uncomfortable and unhappy at the Library. We wanted to develop in every way, and, if this was not possible, the sensible alternative seemed to be to abandon Scarborough altogether.”</em></p>

<p>The sad aspect of this was Stephen hadn’t fallen out with the library itself. He remained good friends with the Director of Libraries, Mervyn Edwards – who was highly supportive of Stephen’s work and decisions. But the library was, at this point, run by the Town Council. Mervyn had helped the company to the extent of his powers and influence, only the Town Council could have made the significant decisions that were needed to save the company.</p>

<p>They didn’t and, on 27 August, the news of the theatre’s closing was made public by the Scarborough Evening News in an interview with Stephen, who lambasted the council’s lack of support for the venue.</p>

<p><em>“This theatre is now totally inadequate for our company’s needs. It has no storage space, no proper dressing rooms, no kitchen for making refreshments and washing up crockery etc. For several years, we have repeatedly asked for help from Scarborough Council, but they have done nothing to improve facilities at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre…. Therefore, it seems we have no choice but to find our own theatre, even if it means – as it most probably will – that we must move from Scarborough at the end of this season.”</em></p>

<p>The news was further reported on a national level by The Stage newspaper on 2 September 1965. </p>

</div>

<p><em>“It seems probable that this year’s 11th season by Stephen Joseph’s ‘in the round’ company at the Library, Scarborough, will be the last. Frustrated by lack of facilities at the Corporation owned Library and the improbability of improvement, Mr Joseph has decided to give up production there.”</em></p>

<p>And what of the season itself? How had that gone? Despite the dire situation the company was in, Stephen never compromised and it was as challenging a season as he had ever staged. There were seven productions which included a triple bill resulting in nine plays, five of which were world premieres by Alan Ayckbourn, David Campton, Alan Plater, Denys Ives and Mike Stott.</p>

<p>There were no well-known plays or guaranteed box office hits and yet the summer season was a record-breaking one with the highest receipts for any summer season at the Library Theatre. A report in The Stage noted audiences had rarely fallen below 94% and there were full houses for many performances.</p>

<p>It was a point not lost on Stephen either,  “[it is] perhaps ironical that we must close the Library Theatre after a season of record returns.”</p>

<p>But good audiences and box office receipts would not solve the underlying issues that Stephen felt were holding the company back and could only be addressed by both involvement and investment by the Town Council.</p>

<p>The season closed on 18 September 1965 and, to all intents and purposes, that should have been the end of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>In November, Scarborough Theatre Trust published a document in which the reasons for the closure of the venue were made clear.  The main reason for the closure was the company had outgrown the Library Theatre, but Stephen also hit out at the Town Council, the Libraries Committee and, separately, the Arts Council for not supporting a winter tour which might have seen the company move to a new home at the University of Manchester.</p>

<p>The decision to close Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was confirmed by the board on 17 December, when it was made absolutely clear there would be no professional season in 1966.</p>

<p>Stephen’s great theatre experiment had ended and there was seemingly no hope for the future….</p>

<p>Except, waiting in the wings, was an unlikely saviour in the shape of an insurance agent and keen amateur theatrical, Ken Boden.</p>
