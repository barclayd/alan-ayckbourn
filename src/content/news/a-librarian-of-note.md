<p>In the history of theatre in the round in Scarborough, there are several figures who are inextricably linked to its early fortunes.</p>

<p>Its founder <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong>, his protégé <strong><a href="/">Alan Ayckbourn</a></strong>, the theatre manager of three decades Ken Boden. All essential figures in the 70 year history of what began in 1955 as <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong>.</p>

<p>But there’s one person who very rarely figures or is acknowledged in the history of the company, but without whom there, arguably, would be no Stephen Joseph Theatre today. Who is far more significant than he has ever really been given credit for.</p>

</div>

<p>He is William Home Smettem who, in 1955, was the outgoing Director of Public Libraries, Museums and Art Gallery in Scarborough and responsible for, among other things, running Scarborough Library. Traditionally, William’s role in the story of theatre in the round is that Stephen Joseph contacted him and, as William was due to retire later that year, felt he had nothing to lose in agreeing to let Stephen rent the Concert Room for the summer for the inaugural season of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>This view is expressed by Alan Ayckbourn in <em>Conversations with Ayckbourn</em> by Ian Watson (Faber, 1988) in which he notes: “Smettem, who was obviously a man of some vision and imagination, but who was also on the verge of retirement, so probably had less to lose than a man who was coming into the job, said, ‘Fine!’ And Stephen moved in there.”</p>

<p>As might be expected, it’s not really that simple though and does an injustice to William and all he contributed. He wasn’t just the man who approved and made the booking of the library for Stephen. His involvement was far more substantial than has previously been acknowledged.</p>

<p>One of the problems is that William has always been described in relation to the history of theatre-in-the-round in Scarborough as the chief librarian. But this substantially reduces and under-estimates his role. As Director of Public Libraries, Museums and Art Gallery, he was – essentially – responsible for overall running of all Scarborough Council’s civic cultural venues and had a powerful and influential voice in the town.</p>

<p>He had led something of a cultural revolution in Scarborough and was associated not just with the town’s libraries, but also its museums and art gallery; all of which had essentially come to the fore during his tenure. Like Stephen, he was a pioneer and he re-shaped the town’s civic cultural services.</p>

<p>It’s interesting to note that, prior to 1930, Scarborough did not have a civic library. Whilst boroughs were given the power to establish free public libraries with the 1850 Libraries Bill, Scarborough only adopted it in 1925.</p>

<p>Four years later, William was appointed as the town’s first Borough Librarian in 1929 – although. as noted in the press at the time, he had no books at this point! He did have a building though as, in 1924, the Mechanic’s Institute (formerly the Oddfellow’s Hall) was offered to the council on the condition it was used as a public library and the council took up the 1850 Bill.</p>

<p>On 30 June 1930, the town’s first civic library opened with demand for it being unexpectedly huge. In its first year alone, more than 15,000 people applied for a library card. They’d have had to battle it out for books as the library only held 9,500 of them in its first year!</p>

<p>William immediately oversaw an ambitious development of the site which saw a rapid expansion. In 1936, a junior library and the Scarborough Room were added on the ground floor as well as lecture hall space upstairs. The Harrison Room – an exhibition space on the first floor which had opened in 1930 and was the town’s first civic exhibition space as the town had no civic art gallery – was later converted into the Concert Room where Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre would open 19 years later.</p>

<p>William rapidly took on even more responsibilities and, in 1937, was involved when the council took over the town’s museum from Scarborough Philosophical Society – this being the Rotunda which is regarded as one of the UK’s earliest purpose-built museums. He had responsibility for the cleaning, sorting and cataloguing of its collections.</p>

<p>In 1934, the council had also acquired Woodend, the former home of the famed Sitwell family. Woodend was transformed into the town’s natural history museum from 1947, opening in 1951, with its conservatory for tropical plants and birds (added by Lady Louisa Sitwell in 1879) made into a vivarium and snake-house. It also housed the town’s natural history collection – including the Harrison Collection, which had previously been displayed in the Harrison Room at Scarborough Library – as well as a space honouring the Sitwells for which William gathered a collection of the family’s literary works, which are still held in the building – now Woodend Gallery &amp; Studios.</p>

<p>The council also came into possession of the noted Crescent House next door to Woodend in 1942 and, in 1947, this was converted into the town’s first civic art gallery. By which point, William had the title of Director of Public Libraries, Museums and Art Gallery. All had come into existence in just the eighteen years since he was appointed Borough Librarian and, now, he was responsible for them all. An extraordinary achievement with William helping to guide the town’s culture output.</p>

<p>So when, in his retirement year of 1955, he was approached by Stephen Joseph about the idea of doing something pioneering in the former Harrison Room, now Concert Room, on the first floor of Scarborough Library, it seems a stretch to say that William was only interested because he had nothing to lose.</p>

<p>He had spent much of the past quarter of a century helping to build Scarborough’s civic cultural offerings – and what was the one thing missing in Scarborough? A civic theatre and here was someone with a radical new idea for a form of theatre which could, potentially, make an affordable and exciting new theatre space, who was interested in trying out his ideas in the library.</p>

<p>I would argue that this is what attracted William’s attention more than anything else. The opportunity to add yet more to the cultural life of Scarborough and potentially pave the way for a civic theatre.</p>

<p>Now, we don’t know with certainty when Stephen met William for the first time. It’s a strange mystery that has – like the hydra of legend – evolved many heads over the decades. There are at least five different variations of how Stephen came to Scarborough (which include those by Stephen himself, Alan Ayckbourn, an education officer for the North Riding, William’s widow as well as the family of his successor, Mervyn Edwards); all of which are variations on a theme but with different people taking centre-stage in first bringing Stephen to Scarborough Library and all in slightly different circumstances.</p>

<p>What we do know for certain is that Stephen first formally wrote to William on 17 February 1955 enquiring about the use of the Concert Room as a home for his proposed theatre-in-the-round project; the letter is held in archive at Scarborough Library.</p>

<p>William’s response, on 21 February, is very encouraging and he adds he thinks it could be “very successful.” This begins regular correspondence between the two in which William goes above and beyond what we might expect of such a high-placed figure in order to help Stephen realise his dream for the UK’s first professional theatre-in-the-round company.</p>

<p>On 25 February, when responding to Stephen’s plea that the library hire for eight weeks is too expensive, William responded he has no doubt a compromise can be reached. Indeed it is, rather than the £600 hire cost initially quoted, William arranged a fee of just £100!</p>

<p>And in context, that’s essentially for the first floor of the library for the summer. Stephen was given not just the Concert Room, but also use of the ‘crush area’ – which became the foyer, the small lecture room – which became the dressing room and wardrobe / administration area as well as the large lecture room – which was the equivalent of the Concert Room and was used for box office, refreshments and an exhibition. It’s equivalent to more than half the space on the first floor of the library at just £12.50 a week (that’s approximately £290 adjusted for inflation).</p>

<p>Throughout the correspondence, the impression is of William driving the negotiations with the Libraries Committee and basically being able to, within reason, get what he wanted.</p>

<p>Stephen visited Scarborough on 12 March – this is the first verifiable and documented meeting between Stephen and William, although they may have met earlier – during which they formalised a plan and agreement for what Stephen dubbed the Library Theatre. William also set about obtaining a play license for Stephen as the Concert Room at that point was only licensed for music and dancing, not plays.</p>

<p>By 30 April, with plans and dates for the summer season firmly in place, Stephen also asked William for help in being introduced to significant local figures such as the hotelier Tom Laughton – brother of the famed actor, Charles – as well as for help with accommodation for the company that summer.</p>

<p>Stephen returned to Scarborough on 25 May for several nights and from June 16 for a week, during both of which he had meetings with William, who – being based in Scarborough – was actively involved in arranging many aspects of the inaugural season such as organising the seating arrangements. The level of support Stephen received can be ascertained from a letter to the Huddersfield Daily Examiner in 1967 in which he wrote: “Thanks to the imagination of W.H. Smettem, the Librarian here, our novel schemes last year were given full scope.”</p>

<p>Prior to arriving in Scarborough for rehearsals on 11 July 1955, Stephen was in Scarborough for no more than four weeks that year. Whilst he obviously did a lot of work on the project, correspondence shows that the man who was his main contact in Scarborough and arranging many of the necessary details to enable the theatre to be viable was William.</p>

<p>Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre opened on the first floor of Scarborough Library on 14 July 1955 with a civic night for dignitaries, the press and members of Scarborough Theatre Guild. Amongst the guests were William H Smettem, supporting this exciting new venture for the library which also marked a first for British theatre.</p>

<p>It is no push to say that without William’s enthusiasm and support for the project, it might never have happened in Scarborough. And, in no small part, he was also responsible for helping the company’s survival. By reducing the cost of the venue hire from £600 to £100, William ensured Stephen Joseph’s losses at the end of the summer were manageable rather than insurmountable. If he hadn’t have arranged this – and in doing so set a precedent which his successor Mervyn Edwards continued – it seems unlikely Stephen would have been able to bear the financial loss and Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre would not have survived its first season.</p>

<p>On 28 August, it was reported in the press that William Smettem was to retire from his post. By this point, Stephen was already in correspondence with Mervyn Edwards with whom, on 16 August, he had made a provisional booking for a second season in 1956.</p>

<p>It seems likely that William’s support and enthusiasm contributed to the smooth transition with regard to Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre and its relationship with Mervyn Edwards, who was – if anything – even more supportive of the project over the coming years than William and played a significant part in its development and success.</p>

<p>As we celebrate the 70th anniversary of theatre in the round in Scarborough, it seems only right to acknowledge and return William Smettem into the story of its creation. Not just as someone who arranged the booking and then retired, but as pro-active figure who encouraged and supported Stephen Joseph and whose actions, ultimately, made it possible for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre to make history when it opened as the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in 1955.</p>
