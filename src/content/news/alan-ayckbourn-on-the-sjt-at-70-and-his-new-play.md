<p>Earlier this month, the Stephen Joseph Theatre celebrated its 70th anniversary having opened on 14 July 1955.</p>

<p>It was on that date theatre pioneer <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> opened the UK’s first professional theatre in the round in the UK on the first floor of Scarborough Library. Two years later, Alan Ayckbourn joined the company, where he first met Stephen – the single most influential figure in his life and career.</p>

<p>As Alan prepares to go into the rehearsal room for the world premiere of his 91st play <em><strong><a href="/plays/earth-angel">Earth Angel</a></strong></em>, running at the SJT from 13 September – 11 October, he spoke to his archivist <strong><a href="http://www.simon-murgatroyd.com">Simon Murgatroyd</a></strong> about his play as well as the 70th anniversary and what Stephen Joseph would have been made of it.</p>

<p><strong>Simon Murgatroyd: Seventy years on, what do you think Stephen Joseph would have made of the SJT today?</strong></p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2025/square-2019-c2a9-tony-bartholomew-01.jpg" alt="" width="350" height="350" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Sir Alan Ayckbourn CBE (© Tony Bartholomew <strong><a href="http://www.bartpics.co.uk">www.bartpics.co.uk</a></strong>)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn: </strong>I think Stephen Joseph would think it’s sliding slightly backwards from his original thought. But Stephen had very definite views that, in his words, theatre should be blown up every seven years!</p>

<p><strong>That sounds rather drastic!</strong></p>

<p>I think what Stephen Joseph meant was not that the bricks and mortar should be exploded but the thinking within them should be totally rethought every seven years. I think his view was, ‘If it ain’t broke, then fix it!’</p>

<p>He hated routine and safe options – he felt that wasn’t exciting for either him or the audience. If you got into a routine during his day, he’d break that routine. He would have felt there’s nothing more boring that saying, this is when we do the Shakespeare, this is when we do a period play, this is when we do a comedy. Audiences quickly find that routine boring and it’s not like they don’t have enough alternatives that are exciting to chose from today.</p>

<p><strong>Did Stephen’s thoughts influence how you ran the theatre for your 37 years as Artistic Director?</strong></p>

<p>While I was here, we weren’t actually self-destructing every seven years but I certainly bore that idea in the back of my mind. Whether it was at the library, <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-188/">Westwood</a></strong> or the SJT, we never took anything for granted and didn’t believe there was any safe formula for success. Because there isn’t.</p>

<p>I changed things radically and we always tried just to keep the audience surprised really. That was the key. </p>

<p><strong>How did you achieve that?</strong></p>

<p>There has to be reinvention, a sort of consistent reinvention and we’d drop some big bombs in it to shake things up. </p>

<p>Event theatre like <strong><em><a href="/plays/intimate-exchanges">Intimate Exchanges</a></em></strong>, <em><strong><a href="/plays/house-and">House &amp; Garden</a></strong></em>, <strong><em><a href="/plays/damsels-in-distress">Damsels in Distress</a></em></strong>, <em><strong><a href="/plays/sisterly-feelings">Sisterly Feelings</a></strong></em> – all the way back to <strong><em><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></em></strong>. You’re always trying to do different things up to and including flooding the theatre! Which, of course, brought the audience in in droves when we did <em><strong><a href="/plays/way-upstream">Way Upstream</a></strong></em> in 1981 and then as a revival in 2003.</p>

<p><strong>Do you think what Stephen Joseph said still holds true today? That his thoughts are still relevant to theatres?</strong></p>

<p>Yes. You’ve really got to keep moving, but to do that takes enormous effort, enormous energy and enormous commitment. Many theatres look for safe options now – but the one thing Stephen Joseph never had was safe options.</p>

<p>He would always be looking to see what he could do differently – even if at times it was ridiculous and dreadful! But, as a result, those early years were so exciting and unexpected for both the company and the audiences.</p>

<p><strong>Which, of course, led to the theatre becoming such an important venue for new writing for much of its history</strong>.</p>

<p>That has undoubtedly changed from Stephen’s day. The established plays are now the major productions, but I remember when I joined the company, the established plays – <em>The Glass Menagerie</em> and <strong><em><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-19/page-3/page28/page28.html">An Inspector Calls</a></em></strong> – were just there to subsidise and to support the new stuff, which is where the money went. They weren’t the headline act nor the main attraction, they were there largely to just draw people in and hopefully to help establish an audience for the new writing.</p>

<p><strong>Talking of new plays, in its 70th year the Stephen Joseph Theatre is premiering your 91st play, <em>Earth Angel</em>, which – I believe is the 349th new play by the company to premiere since Stephen Joseph founded the company in 1955! An extraordinary figure and more than half the total number of plays produced during the company’s entire history.</strong></p>

<p><strong>So what can you tell us about your new play, <em>Earth Angel</em>, before it opens in September</strong>?</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/earth-angel.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="388" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Earth Angel poster</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>Alan Ayckbourn:</strong> <em>Earth Angel</em> is a sort of love story and it’s also about the world today really.</p>

<p>The play begins with a widower in his sitting room following the end of the wake for his wife. Neighbours are washing up and amongst them is this younger bloke, Daniel – in his twenties – and everyone assumes he must be either a relation or a friend.</p>

<p>But he’s not a neighbour, nor a friend of a neighbour nor a relative of the wife. In fact, he’s never even met the widower before.</p>

<p>Which leads people to ask, what are you doing here? Why are you here? And he’s quite evasive on that score, so we – and the characters – are left wondering who he is. And all Daniel keeps saying, “I was passing and there was a service and I thought you probably needed someone to talk to.”</p>

<p><strong>Which takes us to…</strong></p>

<p>Everyone – except the widower – is inherently suspicious of Daniel. And the conspiracy theories build around him from there. I was trying to get a tiny microcosm of society into the play and they’re all part of this suspicious world and then there’s the two main protagonists, who just swirl along on this tide of nonsense remaining more or less buoyant.</p>

<p>It’s up to us, the audience, to decide whether to get caught up in the tide of the people’s suspicions of Daniel or whether there is a point anymore when we say, ‘oh no, this is ridiculous.’</p>

<p>And it really comes back to, who do we trust in this world?</p>

<p><strong>It sounds fascinating and I look forward to seeing it. Thank you, as always, for your time Alan.</strong></p>
