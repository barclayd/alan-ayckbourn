<p>One of the enduring Ayckbourn related questions is, what is the fate of the television adaptations of Alan Ayckbourn’s plays?</p>

<p>Between 1967 and 1990, there were approximately 20 television adaptations of the playwright’s work produced in the UK. The majority off which have rarely if ever been seen again.</p>

<p>For although most of the significant TV adaptations are held in archive – I was fortunate during 2019 to host the first cinema screening of a splendid remaster of the BBC’s 1985 adaptation of <em>Absurd Person Singular</em>, whilst the British Film Institute holds the BBC’s acclaimed 1986 adaptation of <em>Season’s Greetings</em> – several of these TV works have fallen between the gaps and are now presumed lost forever.</p>

<p>For this article, I’m looking at these lost TV adaptations of Alan Ayckbourn’s plays, which we’re sadly never going to experience or be able to judge in the future.</p>

<p>The earliest Ayckbourn television work was actually an abridged live recording of the West End production of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, screened on 21 July 1967 on BBC1, not long after the play had opened to tremendous success in the West End.</p>

<p>This is quite a big loss as it provided a rare insight into what Alan’s breakout West End hit looked and played like. It featured Celia Johnson, Michael Hordern, Richard Briers and Jennifer Hilary and although it only ran for 50 minutes – possibly the first act of the play – it was significant historically given how little comparatively survives regarding the West End production.</p>

<p>Sadly, it has not survived in the BBC Archives, much like its successor. There was little press coverage of it and we are left just to wonder what it was like and how well it reflected the actual production.</p>

<p>The first adaptation of an Ayckbourn play made specifically for television was also <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, shown on BBC1 on 2 March 1969 and running for 90 minutes. Directed by Herbert Wise – who would later direct <em>The Norman Conquests</em> for Thames Television – it starred Celia Johnson, Donald Sinden, John Stride and Judy Cornwell.</p>

<p>All that definitely survives of the adaptation is a number of production publicity photographs held in the Ayckbourn Archive at the Borthwick Institute for Archives at the University of York. Nothing else has survived and we have very little idea what the adaptation was like as the playwright can’t remember anything other than his agent wasn’t terribly impressed by it!</p>

<p>Research by the BBC led to the conclusion this had also not survived in archive and was now lost. Fortunately, the BBC’s third version of <em>Relatively Speaking</em> from 1989 has survived.</p>

<p>The next lost work is, arguably, the most obscure. It’s a performance of the short play <em>Countdown</em> – part of the <em>Mixed Doubles</em> anthology of plays. It was performed live on the BBC2 Arts programme <em>Full House</em> on 16 December 1972 and starred Clive Dunn and Sheila Hancock.</p>

<p>Sadly, like so many of the live broadcasts of this period, it hasn’t survived in archive and practically nothing else is known about it.</p>

<p>There is one other major adaptation that has sadly been lost to time and that is Anglia TV’s 1976 adaptation of <em>Time and Time Again</em>. This was quite a major production and was, potentially, going to be the first of a series of Ayckbourn works for television.</p>

<p>Directed by Casper Wrede, it featured the majority of the 1972 West End production reprising their roles including Tom Courtenay as Leonard alongside Cheryl Kennedy, Michael Robbins and Bridget Turner. This was quite a high budget filmed work which expanded the play with location shooting.</p>

<p>Whilst it sounds fascinating and – tantalisingly – the final 20 minutes of it have been found, the rest of it appears to have been lost and all efforts to recover have had no positive result. The playwright himself thought it was an interesting attempt to film the play but which did itself no favours by deciding to ditch the final scene!</p>

<p>The final Ayckbourn works missing in action are two curiosities. BBC North recorded two of Alan Ayckbourn’s revues with Alan Ayckbourn directing his Scarborough company. The impetus for these was to act as a test bed for live recordings of Alan Ayckbourn’s world premiere productions, but – sadly – these never came to fruition. From the playwright’s recollection, ambition far outweighed practicality on the side of BBC North.</p>

<p><em>Men on Women on Men</em> was shown on BBC1 (North) on 2 and 9 February 1979 and was split into two parts – as was the original stage revue – although each part of Alan and composer Paul Todd’s revue was still slightly abridged. Alan directed it with Malcolm Hebden, Alison Skilbeck, Lavinia Bertram and Robin Murphy.</p>

<p>This does not appear to have survived in the BBC Archive, although it is known to be held privately from a black / white video recording – it’s not known whether the original broadcast was in colour or black and white.</p>

<p>This was followed on 2 March 1984 by <em>Deadly Virtues</em>, an abridged version of Alan and Paul Todd’s revue, <em>The Seven Deadly Virtues</em>. Next to nothing is known about this production. The playwright can’t remember anything about it – despite directing it – and the only evidence for its existence is a listing in the Radio Times. I’ve never come across any footage, photographs nor anyone who recalls watching it on TV; like <em>Men on Women on Men</em>, it was shown only once on BBC1 (north). It is a complete mystery and unlikely ever to come to light.</p>

<p>So these then are the lost Ayckbourn television works. Whilst the playwright notoriously has little to do with the filmed adaptations of his plays, it is still a loss that these interpretations of his plays have not survived for either posterity or research.</p>
