<p><em>In the midst of the recent series of <a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/2023/01/18/changing-spaces-the-library/">Changing Spaces</a> articles, the blog republished <a href="https://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/2023/03/09/celebrating-20-years-at-westwood-pt-1/">Alan Ayckbourn’s memories</a> of life at the second home of his Scarborough company.</em></p>

<p><em>To accompany this, I’ve found in archive another article by Alan Ayckbourn in which he gives a potted history of his entire association with theatre-in-the-round in Scarborough until his retirement as Artistic Director of the company in 2009.</em></p>

<p><em>This is reprinted here over two parts to give another insight into Alan Ayckbourn and his thoughts on theatre in the round in Scarborough over the decades. In the first part, the playwright recalls his experiences between 1957 and 1975 at the first home of the Scarborough company, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</em></p>

<p><strong>Theatre-in-the-Round Memories (part 1)</strong> by Alan Ayckbourn</p>

<p>I’ve been asked to jot down a few memories over the decades. Here goes then. It’s by no means comprehensive, I’ve probably left out more than I’ve included here, indeed I know I certainly have, but it’ll do for starters.</p>

<div>

<p><strong>1959:</strong> <em>The Square Cat</em> produced. I appear onstage in my very first play. Singing and dancing and playing the guitar, none of which I can do. Nonetheless earning £33 in royalties – the equivalent of over a month’s wages! Could try this again.</p>

<p><em>Love After All</em> (by the smash hit author of <em>The Square Cat</em>) follows swiftly. Featuring the author in four roles, including one in drag. Similar results financially.</p>

<p><strong>1960:</strong> <em>Dad’s Tale</em> – a third attempt, this time a Christmas show which I write for two separate companies – our own and a dance troupe from Manchester. And in which I played no less than eight roles. The companies only meet up at the dress rehearsal. Disastrous – there are four people in the audience at the first performance including Artistic Director Stephen Joseph (who directed), Mrs PB (his housekeeper), David Campton, (on the box office) and the local critic. Disappointing financially. Note to self: avoid writing Christmas shows in future.</p>

<p><strong>1962:</strong> The “Stoke years”. A year round theatre-in-the-round is established in a converted cinema in Stoke-on-Trent, the Victoria Theatre. Stephen Joseph’s dream. Though once built, he typically distances himself from it and takes no further part in it.</p>

<p>Under the Artistic Directorship of Peter Cheeseman, we play to pitifully few in the freezing cold. The regulars, such as they are, bring rugs and blankets and huddle together in the front rows for warmth. Despite this we remain as a company for 18 months or more, till tempers start to fray and scripts are thrown.</p>

<p><strong>Christmas 1962</strong>: Appear as an actor for the last time in one of my own plays, <em>Christmas Vs Mastermind</em>. Disastrous. I meet a woman coming out at the end of the performance vowing never to return to the theatre, any theatre, again. Must remember not to write Christmas shows.</p>

<p><strong>1963:</strong> I write my sixth play, <em>Mr Whatnot</em> – a huge success at the Victoria. Later on it’s my first play to transfer to the West End in 1964. A dismal failure….</p>

<p><strong>1965:</strong> Very depressed, I join the BBC and take my first ‘desk job’ as Radio Drama Producer, alongside noted radio producer Alfred Bradley. I am issued with a secretary for the first time, who sits expectantly in the other corner of the office like some expensive piece of machinery ready for immediate use. But I have no idea what to use her for, so start taking her out to drinks at lunchtime and later on to the theatre in the evening.</p>

<p>Whilst at the BBC, I’m persuaded by Stephen Joseph to come up with another play. Which I write in long hand, in the space of a few nights with next door’s cat on my lap. The result is <em>Meet My Father</em> for him to direct for the Scarborough 1965 summer season. On the fifth night without sleep, I declare it to be worthless and send it to him anyway due to pressure of time.</p>

<p><strong>1967:</strong> I change the name of <em>Meet My Father</em> to <em>Relatively Speaking</em> (‘so much more West End sounding, darling’) and suddenly have a West End hit. After the disappointment of <em>Mr Whatnot</em> a few years earlier, I decide not to celebrate too early, so resolve to stay at the BBC and stick with the day job. My caution is justified when I write <em>The Sparrow</em> for the summer season which, despite starring Robert Powell and John Nettles (admittedly in his first professional job), sinks with all hands and is never produced again.</p>

<p>It’s a bad year, because it is also the one when Stephen dies, leaving a huge hole in my life.</p>

<p>I don’t go to the funeral, though, as I’m unfamiliar with the protocol having never been to one and believe, like weddings, funerals are by invitation only. Rather hurt that I never got an invitation, actually. Glad Stephen lived to see the success of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, though.</p>

<p><strong>1969:</strong> I write another play, <em>How the Other Half Loves</em>, which transfers to the West End. Starring Robert Morley, who warns me in advance that, during his life, he’s ‘left a trail of sadder but richer playwrights behind him’. He was absolutely right. But at least it establishes that I’m not a ‘one hit wonder’ and I’m regaining faith in the theatre.</p>

<p><strong>1970:</strong> I quit the BBC after five years. No gold watch or leaving party as I go under a slight cloud of disapproval since I haven’t been in the office much in the last 18 months. I have farewell drink with the secretary instead.</p>

<p><strong>1971:</strong> I go to America for the first time and on to Broadway with <em>How the Other Half Loves</em>. This time starring Phil Silvers who, though undoubtedly a comic genius as <em>Sergeant Bilko</em>, is hardly ideal as Frank Foster. The production leaves a sadder, chastened, not much richer dramatist behind it when it closes prematurely.<br>I hurry back to the safety of Scarborough.</p>

<p><strong>1972:</strong> I take over as Artistic Director of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre summer company and set about enlarging the length of our playing season from 13 weeks to virtually year round with a semi-permanent company of six.</p>

<p><strong>1973:</strong> I write a clutch of plays, <em>The Norman Conquests</em>. Ironically no one initially wants to do <em>The Norman Conquests</em>. The West End gets nervous at the unusual and so, although there’s interest in the plays individually, as a trilogy they are something of a dead duck.</p>

<p>I dig my heels in (not for the first time!) and the three languish on a shelf for some time before the directorEric Thompson, who’d had such success with <em>Time &amp;Time Again</em> and <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> in the West End, phones me to say he is going into hospital for a day or two for a minor op and ‘Had I something he could take in to read?’</p>

<p><strong>1974:</strong> We subsequently launch the trilogy over two week gaps at Greenwich Theatre with the legendary ‘golden’ cast – most of them, apart from Tom Courtenay, largely unknown at the time. I remember, unaccountably, feeling that T<em>able Manners</em>, the first of them we open, had gone terribly badly on press night. I recall walking out to Greenwich Park (which was locked) feeling dreadfully depressed and gazing through the gates in the pitch dark. Unfounded, as it happens. The reviews are spectacular. But I remember the following day as we started rehearsals for <em>Living Together</em>, Eric saying “Everyone, congratulations. But you realise we’ve now got to repeat that. Twice!” Thank God we did.</p>

<p><strong>1975:</strong> Less memorably, I successfully conspire with Andrew Lloyd Webber to create the most highly heralded, subsequently much derided musical of the decade, <em>Jeeves</em>. Rather shame-facedly, seeing the disaster which is approaching, I remember avoiding the West End press night altogether and taking refuge with Andrew in a restaurant, only returning to the theatre to hear the booing from the Upper Circle.</p>

<p>I go backstage to console the star, David Hemmings, who is sitting in the shower half undressed and crying. I remember crouching with him and trying to reassure him that it wasn’t his fault. Nobody thought in the heat of the moment to turn the shower off. I remember going home drenched to the skin. Note to self: along with Christmas shows, in future avoid all musicals.</p>

<p>By the mid-decade in Scarborough, we are dividing our time over the winter between the Filey Sun Lounge (in-the-round) on Tuesdays, Whitby’s Spa Theatre (proscenium) Wednesdays / Thursdays and back in the Library (three-sided as we are unable to use our usual room) Fridays / Saturdays. Madness!</p>

<p><strong>1975:</strong> <em>Bedroom Farce</em> becomes my first National Theatre play, the first time I’m allowed to direct in London (the world premiere is in 1975, the National Theatre production in 1977). Though only then to co-direct with Peter Hall. Which is fine by me. We don’t get under each other’s feet that much since he’s busy in the next door rehearsal room directing <em>Volpone</em>.</p>

<p><strong>1976: </strong>With <em>Just Between Ourselves</em>, we finally quit Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. And re-open a few weeks later, miraculously – in the circumstances – in our second ‘home’ at Theatre in the Round at Westwood.</p>
