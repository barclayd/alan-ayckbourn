<p><em>On this day in 1959, Alan Ayckbourn made his professional playwriting debut with The Square Cat.</em></p>

<p><em>The play opened at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, on 30 July 1959 and would launch an extraordinary playwriting career.</em></p>

<p><em>To mark the anniversary, I’ve dug up some facts about the plays which you may not know…</em></p>

<p><strong>The Square Cat – Did You Know?</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>○&nbsp;Alan Ayckbourn wrote&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/the-square-cat">The Square Cat</a></em>&nbsp;when he was just 19 years old. It was written whilst on the Studio Theatre Company’s 1959 winter tour, which included Harold Pinter’s self-directed second production of his play&nbsp;<em><a href="http://careers.alanayckbourn.net/styled-3/page140/index.html">The Birthday Party</a></em> – which featured Alan playing the role of Stanley.</p>

<p>○&nbsp;Alan had written approximately a <a href="/plays/childrens-plays-index/early-writing-plays">dozen plays</a> before&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>, most of which are now lost. By all accounts though, none of these were full-length and none were farces. <em>The Square Cat</em> was entirely new territory for the budding playwright.</p>

<p>○&nbsp;In a letter dated 19 March 1959, the <a href="http://careers.alanayckbourn.net/styled-6/styled-7/">Library Theatre</a>’s manager Rodney Wood discussed the coming season with Scarborough Library and no mention is made of&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>; it must have been a very late addition to the season.</p>

<p>○<em>&nbsp;The Square Cat</em>&nbsp;was co-written by Alan with his fiancée Christine Roland – they married later that year in May 1959. As a result, it was credited to the pseudonym Roland Allen (combining both author’s names) – although the first print of the production’s programme mis-spelt Allen as Allan.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn &amp; Christine Roland (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>○&nbsp;Someone heads off for a clandestine meeting at a country-house, not knowing their partner has decided to follow them but who arrives first…. Sound familiar? It’s the set-up for both&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>.</p>

<p>○&nbsp;Alan says he wrote the main role of a rock ‘n’ roll star for himself, despite knowing he couldn’t sing, dance or play the guitar. The original manuscript acknowledges this by ending the first act: “Wattis gives a triumphant twang on his guitar.” Unfortunately, someone – either writer or director – decided to be more ambitious as a handwritten note next to it reads “breaks into a number”. In desperation, Alan turned to a local guitar teacher who taught Alan the chords for that – ahem – ‘classic’ rock ‘n’ roll tune, <em>I Gave My Love A Cherry…</em></p>

<p>○&nbsp;Alan played two roles in the play, Jerry and his mild-mannered alter-ego Arthur Brummage, who was brought up in a “dull seaside town” – a sly dig at Scarborough perhaps?</p>

<div><figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn as Jerry Wattis &amp; Arthur Brummage in The Square Cat (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>○&nbsp;Memorable quote: “If people carried on like they do in songs, the delinquency rates would get out of hand.”</p>

<p>○&nbsp;Sidney Glover – the protagonist Alice’s husband – is definitely the prototype ‘Ayckbourn Man’; a husband who obsesses on DIY – and explains in detail the difference between water and gas pipes – and who is clueless as to how he treats his wife. He is almost a proto-Dennis from&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/just-between-ourselves">Just Between Ourselves</a></em>&nbsp;and, of course, in name a pre-cursor to Alan’s most famous Sidney – Sidney Hopcroft from&nbsp;<em><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></em>.</p>

<p>○<em>&nbsp;The Square Cat</em>&nbsp;was the first play at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, to run for two consecutive weeks.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn in The Square Cat (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure></div>

<p>○&nbsp;According to financial accounts for the 1959 summer season,&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat&nbsp;</em>had a total attendance of 3,340 people. It was the second highest attended show of the year behind John van Druten’s&nbsp;<em><a href="http://careers.alanayckbourn.net/styled-3/page141/index.html">Bell, Book &amp; Candle</a></em>&nbsp;with 3,349 people attending.</p>

<p>○&nbsp;The play made £695, 8 shillings and 6 pence as compared to £696, 2 shillings and 6 pence for&nbsp;<em>Bell, Book &amp; Candle</em>.</p>

<p>○&nbsp;Alan estimates he earned £47 from&nbsp;<em>The Square Cat</em>&nbsp;– the most money he had ever earned in his life at that point!</p>

<p>○<em>&nbsp;The Square Cat</em>&nbsp;was revived for the Studio Theatre Company’s 1960 winter tour with Barry Boys playing the role of Jerry Wattis due to Alan being called for a short-lived (3 days) National Service. It was never performed in its entirety again.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Barry Boys in a publicity image for The Square Cat tour (© </figcaption></figure></div>

<p>○&nbsp;During the 1970s and 1980s, Alan insisted he had destroyed all copies of the play. However, original manuscripts are held in the <a href="http://ayckbourn-archive.alanayckbourn.net/page-5/page-10/">Ayckbourn Archive</a> at the University Of York, the <a href="http://ayckbourn-archive.alanayckbourn.net/page-5/page-17/">John Rylands Library</a> at the University Of Manchester and the <a href="http://ayckbourn-archive.alanayckbourn.net/page-5/page-18/">Lord Chamberlain’s Collection</a> at the British Library.</p>
