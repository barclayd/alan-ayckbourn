<p>Today is World Theatre Day and if there’s one thing worth celebrating during these febrile times, it’s the joy of theatre and the uplifting experience it has on our lives.</p>

<p>It’s a good time to reflect on treasured moments in the theatre, favourite performances, fond memories and all those wonderful experiences we’ve all had either visiting the theatre or working within theatre.</p>

<p>So I’m going to celebrate the day by taking you back to the beginning. The play which arguably led me to where I am today and which still remains one of my fondest theatre memories more than thirty years on…</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/1987-henceforward-poster-c2a9-scarborough-theatre-trust-w400.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="570" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>A rare surviving example of a poster for the world premiere of Henceforward… at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round in 1987.<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p><strong>Looking Back at Henceforward…</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>If there is one play to blame for where I am today, it would be Alan Ayckbourn’s classic 1987 play <a href="/plays/henceforward"><em>Henceforward…</em></a>. This was the playwright’s first serious foray into speculative fiction (or sci-fi if you prefer) and his first play to explore the nature of creativity.</p>

<p>It opened in Scarborough in July 1987 and would transfer to the West End during November 1988 with Ian McKellen and Jane Asher starring; the only Ayckbourn play to have featured McKellen.</p>

<p>But for this slice of history, we’re back in Scarborough in 1987. I was not much of a theatre-goer at that point. Not that I didn’t love theatre, but at 16 years of age, I was an aspiring ballet dancer and that was the be all and end all. Dramatic theatre was, well, just not ballet really. All that acting seemed a bit wet compared to the exuberance and joy of dance.</p>

<p>But things were about to change.</p>

<p>For that I had to thank my Dad, Ian, who decided for whatever reason (and I really must ask him some day) to take me to the town’s famed theatre, the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round run by some chap called Alan Ayckbourn. I don’t believe, at the time, I had any clue who he was and certainly hadn’t come across any of his plays. He certainly hadn’t choreographed any ballets.</p>

<p>The first play I saw was <em>Educating Rita</em> starring Robin Herford and Bernadette Foley, directed by Malcolm Hebden (later of <em>Coronation Street</em> fame). I was a fan of the movie, but the play blew that one out of the water. Stripped of anything unnecessary down to a single set and two actors, it transformed the piece and was so immediate. That thrill of live theatre which we all treasure.</p>

<p>This was also my first experience of theatre-in-the-round and, like a certain Mr Ayckbourn many years earlier, it transformed my view of what theatre could be. Subsequently the vast majority of my theatre experiences have been in the round and it has always felt the natural, most vibrant and exciting space for drama. I still have to fight a vague sense of disappointment when attending a pros arch theatre and can’t quite shift the feeling that it’s just a big TV screen with the glass removed. But, I digress.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Constructing the set for the world premiere of Henceforward… during 1987. The man on the right being the theatre’s future Master Carpenter Frank Matthews – an ardent Ayckbourn fan and extraordinary craftsman.<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Thrust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Having scored a hit and proved there was more to theatre than ballet, my father followed it up with a couple of knockout punches. That winter saw the world premiere at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round of Stephen Mallatratt’s adaptation of Susan Hill’s novella <em><a href="http://thewomaninblack.alanayckbourn.net">The Woman In Black</a></em> – you might have heard of that one, it went on to do rather well for itself!</p>

<p>Now that was amazing. I don’t believe I slept for several months afterwards as I had recurring nightmares about the Woman in Black. To this day, my 1987 copy of the novella has to be kept placed under several other books as it features the eponymous woman on the cover. Laugh you may, but has she got me yet? There you go…</p>

<p>So, heading into 1988 and I’ve discovered theatre can lick cinema at certain things (<em>Educating Rita</em>), it can scare you witless with absolutely no budget just an amazing script, actors and tech team (<em>The Woman in Black</em>). What could possibly seal the deal for me?</p>

<p>Enter <em>Henceforward…</em></p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Barry McCarthy &amp; Jenny Funnell during rehearsals for the 1988 revival of Henceforward… in Scarborough.<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Now, small disclaimer here as it helps explain an awful lot. I am and always have been a geek. Particularly a sci-fi geek. I am proudly of the <em>Star Wars</em> generation and that has never left me. It can safely be said, that if I’m still that enthusiastic 42 years on, then we can presume I’m taking this one with me all the way.</p>

<p>And <em>Henceforward…</em> was a sci-fi play (not that Alan ever called it that as he felt nothing would put off the general public quicker back in the ’80s than labelling something sci-fi). My interest was piqued.</p>

<p>My Dad took me to what was the 1988 revival of the play – it had been so successful in 1987, that it was revived in early 1988 before going onto an extensive tour of Germany, Poland, Egypt and Turkey. I had little idea what to expect, but nothing could have prepared me anyway.</p>

<p>I’m still not sure I will ever successfully convey just how much of an impact the play had on me. It was a magical experience. Yes, it was science-fiction and featured clever spins on tropes of the genre. But taken away from the big-budget cinema creations or the unbounded imagination of a book, it concentrated on what theatre does best. What, I was to learn, Alan Ayckbourn did best.</p>

<p>In a small part of a convincingly created dystopian world, <em>Henceforward…</em> concentrated on its characters and their relationships. Like most good speculative fiction, it reflected on universal ideas and themes which are familiar to us all but through the prism of a world slightly removed. At its heart, <em>Henceforward…</em> is a play about the act of creativity, what inspires us to create, the relationships between men and women and how destructive love can be. As relevant now as they were then. As relevant in the future as they were then.</p>

<p>I didn’t know that at the time, but there was a back – and forward – catalogue of equally good works by the same playwright ruminating on these themes. Riches waiting to be discovered.</p>

<p>I was gripped and astonished by <em>Henceforward…</em>.  What it was saying and how it was presented. Central to the work was an extraordinary performance by Barry McCarthy (latterly seen as Professor Dumbledore in <em>Harry Potter and The Cursed Child</em> in the West End). Jerome is a far from likeable man, he makes some appalling decisions and he absolutely makes the wrong decision at the end allowing art to have a brief moment of inconsequential triumph over love. But you can’t take your eyes of him, you certainly couldn’t take your eyes of Barry. Despite all the character’s many faults, he was complexing, fascinating and – despite it all – you empathised with this man. But this was also an ensemble (which I was to learn was so important to Alan Ayckbourn’s writing) and everyone single actor was memorable and there was nothing extraneous or unnecessary.</p>

<p>The original production ended with an extraordinary piece of music by Paul Todd which took advantage of the nascent technology of sampling (not something the average man in the street had much experience of at that point). I remember this music created solely from the word ‘Love’ filling the theatre-in-the-round space. New, exciting, thrilling to watch being ‘created’ and to hear. And from that emotional high, the playwright dropped his only surviving character and the audience off the wall.</p>

<p>It’s the ultimate dying fall.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Barry McCarthy as Jerome in the world premiere of Henceforward…<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>I remember walking back to the car with my Dad, full of excitement talking about the humour, the clever staging, the theatrical effects. And then it hit me. What I’d been laughing at. The darkness that lay beneath the surface of the play. The realisation that at times I was torn between laughing or being appalled – during subsequent viewings, my reactions have changed regularly to the situations presented.</p>

<p>This was the ‘A’ Effect – as it would later be dubbed by the academic Sidney Howard White. Ayckbourn’s ability to move us from laughter to tears at the drop of a hat. The guilty-laugh when we laugh at something we know we shouldn’t be and feel guilty for doing (the second act of <em><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></em> being a masterclass in this). His skill at making us laugh – but not cheap, easy laughs. These were earnt laughs that – after the show – would give way to what was beneath.</p>

<p>It was marvellous. And it beat ballet. Sorry, about that ballet. Whilst I would go on and complete a degree in Ballet and Theatre…, looking back, my heart belonged to plays and dramatic theatre then. It was where I wanted to be.</p>

<p>After graduating, I ended up becoming a journalist and took over the entertainments section (which more or less very quickly became the Arts section) of the local newspaper. All leading to the idea that one day, I might work in the theatre. And all the time, I was learning more and more about Alan and his plays, seeing what plays I could, reading all I could by and about him, buying old posters and programmes wherever I found them. Essentially – and unwittingly – preparing myself for my future career.</p>

<p>And whilst working as a journalist I had my first true encounter with Alan Ayckbourn. My first interview with the man who I admired so much. Never has there been less eye-contact in an interview than this. Alan contemplative, me shy in the presence of a personal hero. We may never have looked at each other, but I think it was an OK interview.</p>

<p>Several years later, I ended up working for Alan at his home theatre – the <a href="http://thesjt.alanayckbourn.net/page1/styled-3/">Stephen Joseph Theatre</a> in Scarborough – as the Deputy Box Office Manager. A dream fulfilled, finally working in the theatre which had inspired me all those years back. Several years later and having taken over the <a href="http://ayckbourn-archive.alanayckbourn.net/page-5/page-16/">Stephen Joseph Theatre’s Archive</a> (from where today’s illustrative material is drawn), Alan Ayckbourn and his wife, Heather, asked if I would like to become Alan’s Archivist.</p>

<p>Would you believe me if I said no?</p>

<p>Of course not. In 2005, I got the chance to live the dream. Ever since then, I spend my working days (and quite a bit of my non-working days) immersed in the plays of Alan Ayckbourn, working in an office converted from his old sound studio in the building where he lives and where, before him, his inspiration and mentor <a href="http://thesjt.alanayckbourn.net/styled-49/steohenjoseph.html">Stephen Joseph</a> lived.</p>

<p>I said earlier, I’m a geek. I’m a <em>Star Wars</em> geek. I’m a Disney geek. Arguably a bit of a Lego geek. But mostly, I’m an Ayckbourn geek. And one who gets to spend every day of their professional life in that wonderful world which all began with a trip to see <em>Henceforward…</em> thirty-two years ago.</p>

<p>I have the best job in the world and I am so grateful to the Ayckbourns for that. I work for someone who inspires me daily and who has inspired me since I was 16 years old. Who could ask for anything more?</p>

<p>And I work in the theatre and have the privilege to share my love, passion and knowledge of all things Ayckbourn with people around the globe.</p>

<p>I can’t think of anything more appropriate to share than that on World Theatre Day!</p>
