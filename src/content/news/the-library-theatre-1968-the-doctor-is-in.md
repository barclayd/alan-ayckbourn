<p>Throughout 2025, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1968 and an unusual season which also saw the debut in Scarborough of an actor who would become renowned for hi adventures in time and space…</p>

<p><strong>1968: The Doctor Is In </strong>by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>One of the joys of curating the recent <em>The Library Theatre at 70</em> exhibition at Scarborough Library was seeing the response to the photographs on display and which of those generated unexpected interest.</p>

<p>One of the photos which caught many people’s eyes with instant recognition was the actor Tom Baker appearing in the 1968 Library Theatre season. I doubt many attending the exhibition actually saw Tom perform that year, but the recognition and pleasure of knowing such a famed actor from that period appeared at the Library Theatre was palpable.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>The photo from The Library Theatre at 70 exhibition featuring Tom Baker in 1968 (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Now I have to come clean here – I picked the photo for precisely that reason. I’m a fan of the world’s longest running Sci-fi series <em>Doctor Who</em> and Tom Baker was the first actor I saw playing the Doctor and still remains the quintessential time-traveller to me. And I had a feeling that quite a few other people might have the same feeling and enjoy the fact one of the most beloved Doctors performed for a summer in Scarborough.</p>

<p>Tom’s one season appearance also ties into what makes 1968 an interesting season for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre as well as unique in the history of the company.</p>

<p>So let’s board our TARDIS and travel back to 1968 to something of a turning point for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre with two very notable omissions and one interesting addition.</p>

<p>In October 1967, the company’s founder, Stephen Joseph, died at his home in Scarborough. Although he had not been actively involved in the 1967 season, he was still closely connected with the theatre and regarded as a figurehead.</p>

<p>1968 then marked the start of a new era for the company.</p>

<p>Also missing from that year was Stephen and the company’s most famous protégé, Alan Ayckbourn. Although synonymous with the company, he had joined the <strong><a href="/career/bbc-career">BBC</a></strong> as a radio drama producer in 1965 and was now just writing occasional plays for Scarborough such as <strong><em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></em></strong> (1965) and <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-sparrow">The Sparrow</a></strong></em> (1967).</p>

<p>Not in 1968 though, which stands as one of just four years between 1957 and the present day where Alan neither acted, directed or wrote a play for the company. It was, in fact a very unusual summer about which very little of which is held in archive.</p>

<p>The season ran for 12 weeks and was overseen by Director Of Productions (Artistic Director) Rodney Wood, another of Stephen’s protégés and who would direct three of the five plays. Unusually for this company, only one of which was a new play and that by a writer who had never been produced before or since by the company.</p>

<p>The writer was David Bradley who was living in West Ayton, Scarborough, at the time; actually a bit of a rarity, the number of Scarborough based writers who have written for the company in the past seven decades can be counted on one hand. He’d had some success as a screen, radio and prose writer, but this was his first – and apparently only – stage play. </p>

<p>The play was <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-19/page-3/page-23/page32.html">A Boat in the Backyard</a></strong></em> and it featured the Scarborough debut of an actor who would go on to become one of the most recognisable faces on British television during the 1970s.</p>

<p>Tom Baker joined the company two years into his career as an actor, which had notably begun playing the Bear in an acclaimed production of&nbsp;<em>The Winter’s Tale</em> which had visited the Edinburgh Festival, Cambridge and Venice in 1966. He had also been involved in the1967 summer rep season in Henley-on-Thames.</p>

<p>He joined a core company of nine actors, none of whom – uniquely in the SJT’s history – had worked previously with the company nor would any do so again. Tom appeared in four of the five plays with roles in <em>A Boat In The Backyard</em>, Noël Coward’s&nbsp;<em>Hay Fever</em>, Carl Sternheim’s&nbsp;<em>The Strongbox</em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em>Arden Of Feversham</em>; the only play he didn’t appear was <em>The Promise</em> by Alexei Arbuzov.</p>

<p>And it’s worth taking a look at that list of plays again – this is a small regional UK summer theatre in 1968 with no stars, very little money and just one familiar show with a Coward play. The rest of the season consists of a new work alongside obscure works from the Elizabethan period, early 20th century Germany and a Russian play about the siege of Leningrad, probably only produced as it had had a measure of success the previous year in the West End in a production with Ian McKellen and Judi Dench before largely disappearing from view.</p>

<p>And it was a season that largely did well with audiences, which says a lot about the Scarborough audience and its tastes. As Alan Ayckbourn recently noted on this blog. Stephen Joseph didn’t believe in safe theatre and it’s obvious that trend continued in the years following his death.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Tom Baker (centre) in Arden of Feversham (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Anyway, returning to Tom Baker, we don’t actually have any images of him in his debut play <em>A Boat in the Backyard</em>, but there are pictures from&nbsp;<em>Hay Fever</em>,&nbsp;<em>Arden Of Feversham</em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em>The Strongbox</em> where he is very recognisably the actor who would go on to star in&nbsp;<em>Doctor Who</em>&nbsp;six years later.</p>

<p>Tom was also featured in a newspaper article – probably from The Scarborough Evening News –  which is the only article in archive about the Library Theatre season that year. It offers details of the productions as well as some of the actors involved, including Tom.</p>

<p>The only reviews from Tom’s time with the company are drawn from The Stage’s online archive but one of which – for&nbsp;<em>The Strongbox</em>&nbsp;– draws a familiar picture of the actor to anyone who had seen the actor on stage or screen over the years.</p>

<p>“Tom Baker as the photographer has a prodigious part and his antics, his florid speech, his foppish and extravagant mannerisms are a joy to watch.”</p>

<p>Tom left the company at the end of the summer season and did not return to Scarborough. He would join the National Theatre from 1969 to 1971, but increasingly worked in television and film. Famously he was in a bit of a lull and working on a construction site when he was cast in 1974 in the BBC series&nbsp;<em>Doctor Who</em>, taking over the role of the Doctor from Jon Pertwee.</p>

<p>He would play the part of the Doctor between 1974 and 1981, appearing in 178 episodes, and is arguably the most well-known and loved Doctor from what is now called its’ classic’ phase (1963 – 1989). He returned to the show briefly in 2013 for the 50th anniversary episode of the show as the mysterious Curator and has subsequently reprised his role in full cast audio dramas.</p>

<p>Coincidentally, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre has another significant link with <em>Doctor Who</em> – particularly Tom’s Doctor – as the year after Tom left, the actress Elisabeth Sladen joined the company in 1969.</p>

<p>Elisabeth became well-known as the Doctor companion Sarah Jane Smith between 1973 and 1976 and is regarded as one of the most popular companions from the entire history of the show; she returned to the show for several episodes when it returned in its new incarnation in 2006 and headed her own spin-off series,&nbsp;<em>The Sarah Jane Adventures</em>, from 2007 until her death in 2011.</p>

<p>Elisabeth worked in Scarborough for two years and – amongst other plays – appeared in the world premiere of two of Alan Ayckbourn’s plays, playing Fiona Foster in&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves">How The Other Half Loves</a></strong></em>&nbsp;and Jenny in&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/family-circles">The Story So Far…</a></strong></em>.</p>

<p>But that’s another year and another story. To get to 1969, we’ll have to step into the TARDIS again, leave Tom behind and return to the blog soon for more tales from the archive about Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>
