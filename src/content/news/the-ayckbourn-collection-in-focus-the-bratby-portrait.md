<p><em>In August 2023, The Ayckbourn Collection was donated to Scarborough Museums and Galleries by Sir Alan and Lady Ayckbourn and the playwright’s Archivist, Simon Murgatroyd.</em></p>

<p><em>It is a collection of approximately 60 significant items drawn from Sir Alan’s Archive and Simon’s personal collection exploring the contribution of Alan Ayckbourn, Stephen Joseph and theatre in the round to Scarborough’s cultural heritage and history.</em></p>

<p><em>Currently, items from the exhibition are on display at Scarborough Art Gallery. To mark the exhibition, Simon Murgatroyd – who curated the Collection and facilitated its donation – is taking a weekly look at some of the individual items within the display, their significance and why they were chosen for the Collection.</em></p>

<p><strong>The Bratby Portrait</strong></p>

<p>During the 1970s, Alan Ayckbourn was enjoying his first significant success as a playwright and had become a nationally recognised theatrical figure.</p>

<p>In the mid-1970s, Alan cannot recall the precise year, the artist John Bratby rang him enquiring whether he would like to have his portrait painted. Bratby was a well-known painter associated with the ‘kitchen sink’ movement and noted for his celebrity portraits.</p>

<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2023/smg-ayckbourn-portrait-1-c2a9-tony-bartholomew-w800.jpg" alt="" width="800" height="556" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn with the second of the portraits by John Bratby, which is currently on display in Scarborough Art Gallery and is part o the Ayckbourn Collection held by Scarborough Museums and Galleries (© Tony Bartholomew <a href="http://www.bartpics.co.uk">www.bartpics.co.uk</a>)</sub></figcaption></figure>

<p>At the time – as the artist candidly told the playwright – Bratby was taking commissions prolifically to pay alimony to his first wife, Jean. Alan went to the artist’s home in London and was painted in the kitchen with the artist revealing the complete portrait after a 10 minute sitting, as recalled by Alan.</p>

<p>“Bratby said, ‘what do you think?’ and I said, ‘erm… yeah.’ He said, ‘I’ll do another one.’ I said, ‘No, no, don’t bother,’ and he said, ‘No, no, I’ll do another one, I’ll do another one. You obviously don’t like it.’</p>

<p>“I said, ‘Well, I’m just getting used to it.’”</p>

<p>Bratby’s distinctive painting technique epitomised the style for which he became known for as part of the ‘kitchen sink’ art movement during the 1950s.</p>

<p>“He never used the brushes,” said Alan of the portrait. “He just used a palette knife, the paint was so thick when he put it on.”</p>

<p>The second portrait took half-an-hour and if it still didn’t quite ring a bell with the subject, his wife – Heather Stoney – thinks it did at least capture a side of the playwright’s personality.</p>

<p>Alan argues it’s “the dismayed side”.</p>

<p>Alan decided to buy both portraits, but not out of love for them. “We had to buy it from him. I bought both to keep them out of circulation! I think most people bought them because they were so distressed by the pictures.”</p>

<p>The portraits were apparently briefly displayed at the playwright’s home in Scarborough, before being placed, during the 1980s, into a closet, never to see the light of day again until 2023.</p>

<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2023/bratby-03-w762.jpg" alt="" width="762" height="507" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>The first and second portraits by John Bratby, held respectively in the Ayckbourn Archives at the Borthwick Insitutue for Archives at the University of York and the Ayckbourn Collection at Scarborough Museums and Galleries (Photos © Simon Murgatroyd and Tony Bartholomew)</sub></figcaption></figure>

<p>That year, the second of the portraits was loaned to Scarborough Museums and Galleries for its 2023 portraiture display,&nbsp;<em>Who Am I?&nbsp;</em>with the gallery having been tipped off to its existence by the theatre’s press officer, Jeannie Swales, who knew of the ‘Bratby in the closet.’ The portrait was then donated to Scarborough Museums and Galleries as part of the Ayckbourn Collection, facilitated by Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist Simon Murgatroyd, which was donated by the Ayckbourns in August 2023.</p>

<p>The first portrait was similarly donated to the Borthwick Institute for Archives at the University of York in autumn 2023 which holds the Ayckbourn Archive, the single largest collection of material relating to Alan Ayckbourn in the world.</p>

<p>The playwright hopes there they will be cared for and appreciated.</p>

<p>And that he will never have to see them again.</p>

<p>The Bratby portrait of Alan Ayckbourn can be be seen in The Ayckbourn Collection display at Scarborough Art Gallery.</p>
