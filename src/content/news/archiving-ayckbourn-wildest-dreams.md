<p>Whilst visiting the RSC to see the wonderful new production of <em>A Midsummer Night’s Dream</em>, it led me to reflect on Alan Ayckbourn’s only significant connection with the RSC – coincidentally another dream, but of an entirely different type.</p>

<p>For in 1993, the RSC staged – as of writing – its only Ayckbourn production with <em>Wildest Dream</em>s, a play which premiered in Scarborough on 6 May 1991 and, thanks to the RSC, received its London premiere at the Barbican on 14 December 1993.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn directed the production which later transferred to The Swan at the RSC from 16 March for four weeks. It’s a production about which, as Alan’s Archivist, I know surprisingly little about as the Ayckbourn Archive – held in the Borthwick Institute for Archives at the University of York – holds surprisingly little relating to this significant production. In fact, there is just one major item – a programme, reproduced here along with its interior company page.</p>

<p>To say, in retrospect, this is such a significant event in the career of Alan Ayckbourn, it is surprising that more was not kept for the archive and that we also have no great insight into Alan’s experiences with the production. Alan has never spoken about his time at the RSC and the production has next to no mention is his biography by Paul Allen other than “it performed well enough at the box office.”</p>

<p>The RSC production featured a mix of actors who had appeared in the original Scarborough production alongside several new faces including Brenda Blethyn, who played Hazel alongside Barry McCarthy reprising his role of her husband Stanley from Scarborough. Peter Laird and Gary Whittaker also reprised their roles from the world premiere and were joined by Andree Evans, Sophie Thompson, Paul Bentall and a young Jenna Russell, who in 2022 took the lead role in Chichester Festival Theatre’s production of Alan’s <em>Woman In Mind</em>.</p>

<p>As for how Alan Ayckbourn came to be at the RSC in 1993, the credit for that goes entirely to Tom Erhardt, who was then Alan’s agent. The story goes that Tom was having lunch with the RSC’s Artistic Director Adrian Noble in 1991 and discussing various plays and playwrights. At the end of the meal, Tom subtly mentioned Alan’s play,&nbsp;<em>Wildest Dreams</em>, and how its content probably made it an ideal fit for the RSC. Of course, he  just happened to have a copy of the script with him, which Adrian took and read. This led to Alan being invited to produce <em>Wildest Dreams</em> for the RSC and to make his directorial debut with this prestigious company.</p>

<p>It’s an interesting play in retrospect and unfairly ignored within the Ayckbourn play canon. <em>Wildest Dreams</em> was ahead of its time, inspired as it was by role-playing games and people’s desire to escape reality into imagined worlds. Since it was written in 1991, that trend has increased beyond belief with physical and digital gaming now an accepted and worldwide phenomenon.</p>

<p>In 1991, it was just 17 years since <em>Dungeons and Dragons</em> had debuted and although popular, role-playing was still a fringe – and occasionally misunderstood and controversial activity. It was by no means the global success it is today as it celebrates its 50th anniversary this year. <em>Wildest Dreams</em> is arguably more pertinent now than it has ever been.</p>

<p><em>Wildest Dreams</em> marked Alan’s only sojourn to Stratford-upon-Avon, but that should still be celebrated. It’s nice to think there is a link  between the world’s greatest playwright and one of this country’s most popular and successful living playwrights.</p>
