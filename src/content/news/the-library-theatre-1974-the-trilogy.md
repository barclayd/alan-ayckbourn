<p>Throughout 2025 and 2026, the Ayckbourn Blog is celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>Focusing on <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> – the company’s first home between 1955 and 1976 – we’re celebrating the venue and people involved as well as its historic connection to Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p><em>The Library Theatre</em> is a series of regular articles looking at key moments from each year highlighting the significance and achievements of the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>We now move to 1974 and a rivalry which made national headlines…</p>

<p><strong>1974: Conflict </strong>by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>Despite surviving for seventy years, it’s worth reflecting how theatre in the round’s existence in Scarborough has often been precarious over the decades.</p>

<p>Stephen Joseph’s closure of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in 1965 was one such flashpoint, as was a decision in 1974 which led to Alan Ayckbourn threatening to leave Scarborough.</p>

<p>This was, however, a conflict the theatre found itself unintentionally embroiled in, which made news headlines and became a point of much contention.</p>

<p>The core of the argument was the town’s former Opera House theatre and an overly-ambitious attempt to breathe new life into the recently closed building.</p>

<p>All that remains today of the Opera House now is the name of the casino built on its former site, but the actual theatre opened in 1908 on the site of a former building, the Charles Adams Grand Circus, from 1876.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn circa 1974 (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>In 1908, the original building was demolished aside from the outer walls and cast iron columns within and rebuilt and re-opened as the New Hippodrome.</p>

<p>From the start, it was a venue for both live performance and film and was renamed twice to the Opera House &amp; Hippodrome in 1910 and later the Grand Opera House in 1914. From 1947, it was predominantly used as a repertory and receiving theatre until it was closed in 1971.</p>

<p>Interest in re-opening the theatre emerged soon afterwards and in 1972 a campaign was launched to save the Opera House; this garnered popular support with 13,500 signatures and the backing of several prominent Scarborians.</p>

<p>Concurrently, Alan Ayckbourn and Scarborough Theatre Trust were actively searching for a new home for the company given the limitations of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>The suggestion of a rival theatre project – then the Save The Opera House Society which later became the Opera House Preservation Society – generated much unease at the Trust as the minutes from a meeting on 12 November 1972 demonstrate.</p>

<p>“Secretary reported that the Theatre [Opera House] was on the open market and agents were asking £50,000 (or offer) as it was a Listed Building. He continued and informed the meeting of another interested party trying to create enthusiasm in the town to save the building. Secretary reported he was attending their meetings out of curiosity and would report back to the Trust.”</p>

<p>It all sounds very clandestine!</p>

<p>But there was a serious issue here. Scarborough Theatre Trust has been fundraising for a new home for the past five years. Despite no success in finding a suitable venue, it was feared a rival theatre bid might derail the Trust’s work.</p>

<p>“One view expressed was that this Society might launch an Appeal for money which could seriously affect our own Appeal.”</p>

<p>A prediction which proved correct when a report about the Opera House was given to Scarborough Theatre Trust in March 1973.</p>

<p>“I think we should now regard the Preservation Society as <strong>opposition</strong>. We know very little about their intentions except they are buying the Opera House and are to launch an appeal for Funds in May of this year.”</p>

<p>Of most concern was the news the Society hoped to become a Theatre Trust to pursue similar grants as Scarborough Theatre Trust. That news even prompted the consideration whether the Trust itself should buy the Opera House; which was not feasible as due to its Listed status, the proscenium arch and layout inside was protected.</p>

<p>It was not until 1974 that the worst fears of the Trust were confirmed though, when in September both the Trust and Society applied to Scarborough Borough Council for a grant towards a new theatre; in a closed meeting the Council was recommended to only consider the Trust’s application and to ignore the Opera House request.</p>

<p>At the same time, the Society contacted the Trust suggesting the two groups work together, but the Trust was quick to point out two theatres could not prosper in Scarborough nor were they interested in a proscenium arch theatre.</p>

<p>That there was now seen to be an actual rivalry between the two projects is evident from newspaper reports at the time with the Scarborough Evening News noting in October that the town council had ignored the society’s grant application.</p>

<p>“Scarborough Theatre Trust, it seems, is now laps ahead of the Opera House Preservation Society in the race to put on year-round live theatre in Scarborough.”</p>

<p>It was at this point though that matters took an interesting turn. During an earlier town council meeting, it had been proposed that a theatre-in-the-round be part of a proposed – but never realised – extension to Scarborough Library, taking over the former Christ Church (now the home of the Iceland store on Vernon Road).</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2025/screenshot-2025-11-17-at-09-25-36-w784.png" alt="" width="784" height="1022" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Councillor Erkki Lahteela</figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>This was news to the Libraries Committee – a County Council based committee which earlier in the year had taken over running the Library from the town level. A key member of the committee was County Councillor Erkki Lahteela, described by the newspaper as ‘a prime mover and indefatigable worker in the fight to save the Opera House’ – who was reported as saying.</p>

<p>“We have never indicated that the Library Theatre should have a place in any extension.”</p>

<p>Conflict of interest, anyone?</p>

<p>This appears to have been the keystone moment in all that followers. At this point, the Society had until January 1975 – less than three months – to secure £30,000 to buy the lease of the Opera House. And they were not leaving without a fight.</p>

<p>The Trust was asked again – and again declined – to meet the Society and reconsider its position. At approximately the same time, the Trust applied for a 40 week season for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre; it appears to have been assumed this would largely be a formality based on previous year’s experiences.</p>

<p>Except everything was different now the Country Council rather than the Town Council controlled the Library.</p>

<p>On 23 November, the Scarborough Evening News reported North Yorkshire County Libraries Committee had turned down the application more than two to one when County Councillor Lahteela “spearheaded opposition to the Library Theatre proposal” suggesting the theatre’s presence would “take facilities away from organisations” in the town.</p>

<p>Given his position as Chairman of the Society, it seems incredible that he would have been allowed to be involved in such a critical decision for another theatre.</p>

<p>Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre’s Artistic Director, Alan Ayckbourn, was enraged by the decision and wrote a full-page article for the Scarborough Evening News on 25 November 1974 in which he both threatened to quit the town and also leapt into the Opera House fray.</p>

<p>“Nobody can have been unaware of the so-called dispute between the Opera House Preservation Society and the Scarborough Theatre Trust. I say so-called because, to date, there has been little or no comment made by the Scarborough Theatre Trust itself concerning the issue… It is with great reluctance that I feel I have to comment on the projected Opera House scheme at all. Speaking as one who relies for his living upon keeping theatres open, it would never be my wish to close one unless I felt, as in the case of the Opera House, that the building itself was so archaic in size, so uneconomical to run, and so lacking in any worthwhile features as a theatre.”</p>

<p>During the course of the article, he tore into the lack of realistic financial planning for running the theatre, wondering where the £75,000 a year needed to run such a venue was going to come from without subsidy.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2025/1974-11-27-scarborough-evening-news-library-w350.jpg" alt="" width="350" height="209" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>The Scarborough Evening News interview with Alan Ayckbourn (© The Scarborough News)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Aware that the Society had made public its belief that the Trust should join forces with them to restore the Opera House, Alan neatly turned it on its head, whilst striking a defiant tone.</p>

<p>“I would like to reverse the invitation and ask them to join us. We, after all, have been proven to work, have a considerable national reputation and are already, within the limits of our grant, economically afloat. I would happily accept all the funds so far raised by the Opera House, purchase and demolish their outmoded playhouse, and build in its place a sensible scaled theatre that Scarborough could enjoy and afford to run.”</p>

<p>Given the Listed Building situation, this was almost pure provocation by Alan and he concluded by noting that there was scarcely a country in the world where a theatre had not produced which had originated in Scarborough and that, if the Library did not change its mind, it would result in his “own departure from Scarborough.”</p>

<p>This led to the eye-catching headline story: “Ayckbourn says he will quit if Library Theatre is refused a longer season.”</p>

<p>And it wasn’t just Alan, the entire Scarborough company had unilateral voted to join Alan in his threat to leave Scarborough, which almost immediately became more than an empty threat. For, to add fuel to the fire, Hull Arts Centre made an offer to house Alan and the company; interestingly the Arts Centre would later become the Spring Street Theatre, home of Hull Truck Theatre.</p>

<p>As a result of this, Alan was called into a crisis setting with Scarborough Borough Council whilst Councillor Lahteela’s continued to defend his decision purely on his desire to protect the Scarborough Library’s facilities.</p>

<p>On the 28 November, the media reported the Opera House Preservation Society had offered a 50-50 administrative split with Scarborough Theatre Trust, if it immediately invested £15,000 to help obtain the lease for the Opera House and the two organisations amalgamated their funds and efforts.</p>

<p>Judging from contemporary reports, it’s clear the Society was either being deliberately provocative or was just completely clueless – particularly given its suggestion there was no necessity for theatre in the round in any new theatre development as “Alan Ayckbourn’s plays are performed in the West End in proscenium arch-type theatre not as theatre-in-the-round.” With hindsight, the kindest interpretation is the Society was clueless and, in Lahteela, was led by an arrogant and ill-informed councillor used to getting his own way.</p>

<p>As would be seen later, it was also – in all probability – actually a desperate play for money by the Society, which was not quite in the financial position it had painted itself as – having suggested there was at least £15,000 in the coffers.</p>

<p>December came with several more letters to the Trust inviting them to join the Society’s scheme, at which point Alan agreed to meet the Society – though for what purpose is not clear given there was so little common ground between the two.</p>

<p>Alan later reported back the meeting had produced nothing of value and that “he had found it difficult to convince them that the aims of the two associations were totally separate.”</p>

<p>Time for the Society was running out – as it was for its figurehead. Scarborough Borough Council made a request to the County Council for a special meeting regarding the Library Committee’s decision and members of the council publicly accused the County Council of being out of touch with the people of Scarborough and that there was overwhelming support for Alan Ayckbourn and Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>With the conflict now being reported in the national press and his actions as both councillor and chairman of the Society being portrayed in a less than positive light, The Stage reported on 5 December 1974 that Councillor Lahteela had resigned as chairman of the Society.</p>

<p>Never knowingly wrong, Lahteela went on to make a public statement which was reported nationally due to the rather extraordinary declaration that neither he nor any other county councillors had realised “how famous Alan Ayckbourn was.” It is not recorded what his fellow councillors thought of this sweeping statement, but it was perhaps not the wisest of comments.</p>

<p>This at the time that Alan had made national headlines with a record-breaking five plays running simultaneously in the West End, his work in constant repertory around the UK and reports of his first Broadway success!</p>

<p>In the wake of that, North Yorkshire County Council agreed to the extension of a 40 week season, but in the understanding that this would be the company’s final year at the Library and they would vacate the building by January 1976.</p>

<p>As for the Opera House Preservation Society, the deadline of 10 January was reached and it was revealed that despite all the sound and fury, the fund-raising efforts had amounted to… nothing. It was reported the Society had not even reached £500 of the £30,000 target.</p>

</div>

<p>Later that year, local businessman Don Robinson – whose company Millet Investments had bought the Opera House for £22,500 in 1973 and were behind the purchase offer to the Society – began restoring and refurbishing what would be called the Royal Opera House. The purchase and restoration was put at more than £175,000 – far in excess of the £10,000 quoted by the Society at one point which would be all that was needed to restore the venue!</p>

<p>It re-opened on 16 June 1976 with the production <em>Carry On Laughing </em>although, true to Alan’s prediction, it was not able to survive as a theatre and it later became a cinema with the occasional live performance.</p>

<p>Its final film was the blockbuster <em>Batman</em> in 1995, after which it closed down and was, essentially, left to rot. The entire building was demolished in 2004 and the Opera House Casino built on its site.</p>

<p>But for Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, the clock was ticking. The company needed a new home and as they entered 1975, there was just a year to find it.</p>
