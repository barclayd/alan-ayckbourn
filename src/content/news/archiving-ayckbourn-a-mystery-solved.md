<p><em>Archiving Ayckbourn is a regular feature in which Alan Ayckbourn’s Archivist, Simon Murgatroyd, delves into the archives to highlight items of interest relating to the playwright and theatre in the round.</em></p>

<p><strong><em>Relatively Speaking</em>: A Mystery Solved</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p><strong><em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></em></strong> is, undoubtedly, one of Alan Ayckbourn’s most successful and popular works. Almost 60 years after its debut, it is still frequently revived by both professional and amateur theatre companies.</p>

<p>It has proved perennially popular and is a play renowned for how skilfully it manages its narrative and dialogue which create a tightly-wound play of misunderstandings. However, if one was to find fault with it, it does have one glaring issue.</p>

<p>Anyone who has produced the play will know that structurally it has an issue. The relatively short first scene – set in Ginny’s flat – immediately leads onto a second scene in a completely different locale, Philip and Sheila’s garden.</p>

<p>There is no interval, just a quick change of scene which sees the entire set of the first scene replaced by the garden and patio set. A designer once told me it was a logistical nightmare and not something that Alan would have done later in his career; it’s worth noting Alan was only 26 when he wrote <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, which was his seventh full-length play.</p>

<p>In 2007, one of Alan’s most frequent collaborators, the designer Michael Holt, explained the difficulties in an interview.</p>

<p>“The designer has got to make everything crystal clear. You can’t cut anything. You have to have a bathroom full of flowers; you have to see them or Ginny’s lie could remain just a lie…</p>

<p>“It can pose huge logistical problems. There has to be a bathroom and a front door and you have to see both of them. They can’t be the same door as there has to be two exits. In the Proscenium Arch, you really have to create two doorways and that means you have to be able to get rid of it all and the bed. They’re not small problems; beds are big!</p>

<p>“The scene-change is a difficult challenge as the first scene isn’t long enough to be an act. The change should come as a shock.”</p>

<p>With the best will in the world – unless you have a massive budget and large theatre – the transition between scenes generally takes several minutes due to its scale. The bed and doors have to be removed alongside all the propping and then the garden introduced in whatever form has been decided. All between two scenes – there is no comfort or leeway of an interval break.</p>

<p>The question is, why did the playwright do this? Why didn’t he realise it might be problematic? How did he envision it being staged? How was the problem originally dealt with?</p>

<p>The playwright himself has previously said he can’t remember how the scene change was done originally as he wasn’t involved with the production and didn’t direct it. The world premiere – the play then called <em>Meet My Father</em> – was directed by <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong>, the most influential figure in Alan’s life,  and the first Alan saw of the production was on press night.</p>

<p>And it’s here the answer lies. Staring this archivist in the eyes for the past two decades! Have a look at the photo below taken from the world premiere production at <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong>, Scarborough. in 1965. Let’s see if you can work it out before I return to it…</p>

<p>In the meantime, let’s fast forward a bit. The problem that Alan has noted with many of his plays is they are taken out of context.  The most significant productions of his work have historically been regarded as the West End premieres. Which, as the playwright has frequently noted, is wrong.</p>

<p>For as anyone familiar with Alan Ayckbourn’s plays knows, his work is conceived and written for in the round performance, predominantly for his home theatre in Scarborough where he believes the definitive productions of his work are generally started.</p>

<p>When the plays are produced in the West End – regardless of whether he or someone else has directed it – they are frequently on a proscenium or end-stage and a step away from the playwright’s original intentions and already – to some extent – compromised. Naturally, this varies depending on the play, but you only have to look at <em><strong><a href="/plays/taking-steps">Taking Steps</a></strong></em> to see how the original intentions are ruined in the proscenium. It really doesn’t work and certainly never works how the playwright initially intended it to.</p>

<p>However, the idea still stands that the West End somehow reflects the definitive production of the work. And this doesn’t help <em>Relatively Speaking</em>. The incredibly successful 1967 production – which thrust Alan into the limelight – presented both Ginny’s flat in all its glory as well an opulent garden complete with patio, the back of a house and French doors. The full works.</p>

<p>What’s wrong with this picture? Walls and screens, essentially. The walls are covered with posters, screens conceal the kitchen area. Below are the actual opening stage directions as written by the playwright for the world premiere production. Notably, these are the only introductory stage directions Alan has ever written himself for this scene – alongside his notes for the second scene.</p>

<p>No wall or screens. Obviously. We’re in the round, you can’t have walls or screens. Half the audience wouldn’t be able to see anything at all if you did. It’s a simple set – very simple. Because that’s what Alan was writing for at the time. He was writing for an in the round space with two voms (entrances) and minimal budget. He couldn’t do a detailed room or a garden, the bare minimum was all that was required or affordable.</p>

<p>And here’s another point to ponder. The majority of Alan’s published works from the 1960s to the 1980s are based on the West End end-stage productions and feature stage-directions which reflect this.</p>

<p>They weren’t written by Alan.</p>

<p>Alan didn’t go back and revise his manuscripts post-production for publication. Most commonly, the stage manager’s book from the West End production would be used and the stage directions lifted from that. Alan has very rarely written in-depth stage directions and certainly not for proscenium productions. </p>

<p>If you look at the detailed stage directions and props and settings list in either the Samuel French or Evans publication of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, they’re nothing to do with Alan. He didn’t write or compile them. They were taken from the book for the West End production and subsequently it’s been taken for granted they reflect the author’s intentions.</p>

<p>They don’t really. They just reflect what a very popular production of the play looked like, which Alan didn’t direct and had very little input into.</p>

<p>The majority of stage directions you read in the published edition of some of his most popular works – such as <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em>, <em><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves"><strong>How The Other Half Loves</strong></a></em>, <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em> and <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></strong></em> – were largely based on the stage manager’s notes or the book from the West End production or were written specifically for the publication by someone else as the publishers wanted a play to reflect how it would be performed by the majority of people wanting to produce it i.e. end-stage / proscenium. Not in the round as Alan had conceived it.</p>

<p>Now what Michael Holt said isn’t inaccurate. For the play to work, all these design and prop elements have to be considered, but they obviously weren’t deemed essential when Alan wrote the play or Stephen Joseph directed the play. There wasn’t a designer back in 1965 at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. Stephen Joseph – as director – would have decided what the propping and layout of the stage would be like.</p>

<p>And we also have to remember, this was a very quick production. There was no interval – as seen by the programme extract above – and very little time between scenes. Why? Well, predominantly because the performance started at 8pm and everyone – audience and cast included – had to be out of the library by 10pm. There was no leeway, the contract with Scarborough Library stated the Concert Room where the plays were staged had to be clear by 10pm, when the building would be locked up.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn recalls how when he was running the theatre, the man on duty each evening would frequently emerge into the auditorium – even if the play was still running – at 9.50pm and begin shaking his keys! Time to go, even if you have to finish mid-scene…!</p>

<p>So, <em>Relatively Speaking</em> ran for a maximum of 1 hour 45 minutes, most likely 90 minutes or so; <em>Relatively Speaking</em> has an approximate running time of 120 minutes but we know Stephen cut approximately a quarter of the play for the original production. Stephen also liked to have time for the audience to talk to the company post-show.</p>

<p>So there wasn’t time in <em>Relatively Speaking</em>  for a big change of set after the first scene – certainly not of the scale seen in the West End or subsequent revivals.</p>

<p>So how did the scene change work? Alan knew the play was on a strict time limit and would have considered that. So what are we missing? Did Alan create something that was always awkward or are have we not considered the conditions in which the play was produced?</p>

<p>Here’s another photo of the original production. Have you noticed what has been missed when considering how Alan wrote the play and it was then staged?</p>

<p>The bed is still there on the stage, at the back of the photo in front of audience. As is the coffee table and carpet from the flat. It never went off stage in the original production. It isn’t a misinterpretation of the photos either as they’re not staged rehearsal images. The only surviving photographs were taken during an actual performance where the audience is clearly visible. The photos reflect what was on stage during the actual performance.</p>

<p>From the surviving images, it seems clear the stage was cut in half. One half being Ginny’s (or Gina as it was then) flat and the other half, the garden. The flat is reduced to bed and coffee table, the garden just a table, several garden chairs and something covering the library’s parquet flooring. Presumably a green carpet or cloth to suggest a garden.</p>

<p>It’s not elegant, I don’t think it’s particularly well thought out, to be honest. But it would have worked. The first scene would have zipped straight into the second scene. Without an interval, there was no other time to remove the bed, so it stayed there for the entire performance. It’s patently a low bed too, so it wouldn”t intrude on the audience’s sightlines.</p>

<p>It was literally a production of two halves.</p>

<p>And those images have been in archive for six decades! Ever since it became a huge hit in the West End, people have asked why did Alan write a play with such a difficult transition between the first two scenes?</p>

<p>Well, he didn’t. We have to realise Alan wasn’t writing the play with the West End in mind then – or any other theatre than the one he’d been to commissioned to write for. By past experience, he was relatively safe to assume <em>Relatively Speaking</em> would probably never be performed again outside of Scarborough. He wrote it for theatre in the round, knowing Stephen Joseph would solve any problem he posed problem easily. Alan wasn’t thinking in terms of whether the staging would be a problem for future productions or how would it work in a proscenium. Why would he?</p>

<p>Two years later, when <em>Relatively Speaking</em> took the West End by storm, he might well have considered whether it was the ideal scene change and bore it mind in the event of future success. But when he wrote the play, he would have been thinking of nothing more than a hoped for success at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre in Scarborough.</p>

<p>So mystery is solved. Not terribly satisfactorily though as I don’t think any of us would ever want to go back to how the production was initially staged! I think we’ll just let the very clever designers and stage managers do what they’ve been doing since 1967 and let them handle the stage transition with flare and aplomb!</p>
