<p>Today marks the 60th anniversary of the opening of the first West End production of an Alan Ayckbourn play with <strong><a href="/plays/mr-whatnot">Mr Whatnot</a></strong>.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/1964-mr-whatnot-flyer-01-w350.jpeg" alt="" width="350" height="522" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>Flyer for the West End production of Mr Whatnot (© TBC)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>The play opened at the New Arts Theatre on 6 August 1964 starring Ronnie Barker, Judy Campbell and Peter King. It received some of the most vitriolic reviews of Alan’s career and two weeks later it closed.</p>

<p>The experience was so traumatic that it knocked Alan’s confidence enough for him to announce he was going to give up playwriting and he joined the BBC as a Radio Drama Producer for five years.</p>

<p>Yet why did <em>Mr Whatnot</em> fail so badly in London? What is frequently forgotten is the play was optioned by the West End impresario Peter Bridge because it was so successful when first produced at the Victoria Theatre, Stoke-on-Trent, in 1963.</p>

<p>The original production, directed by Alan with an ensemble company led by Peter King as the titular character, was a huge early success for the company. The Staffordshire Sentinel noted “There was laughter galore, in a way I have never seen on the stage before.” There was a good review in The Stage and it was credited with having helped the theatre enjoy a 10% rise in audience figures over the previous year; apparently it was the most successful show at the Victoria in 1963.</p>

<p><em>“It was my most successful play to date. It became a very, very successful production, in its Stoke form, and was the first original, major smash we had there.”</em></p>

<p>On the back of that, Peter Bridge – who had seen the production in Stoke – optioned it for the West End. This was reported in newspapers such as The Times and the Daily Telegraph and The Stage even ran a front page photograph of Peter King as Mr Whatnot when the production was officially announced.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>The front page of The Stage (23 July 1964) with Peter King and the announcement of Mr Whatnot (© The Stage Media Company Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>So what went wrong? Well, essentially, a play written for a simple production in the intimacy of a small in-the-round theatre with an ensemble who had worked closely together for some time was replaced with an overthought, overproduced and over-sized West End production that lost all that had made it successful to begin with.</p>

<p><em>“It was another lesson I learnt very quickly: and that was that writing plays for companies was a very delicate business, and transferring them to London and then letting West End actors – however good – lose on them, often destroys the fabric of the play if it wasn’t very strong.&nbsp;Mr Whatnot&nbsp;didn’t succeed, and it ran for three weeks in London, and although it was perfect in Stoke-on-Trent, with six actors who knew each other, it fell to pieces literally once we tried it in London.”</em></p>

<p>There was no forewarning the play would be such a disaster. Indeed, Alan recalls people telling him this production would be his making. So confident was he on the back of such advice, that he left the Victoria Theatre in spring 1964 in order to concentrate on his writing and to be involved in the transfer of <em>Mr Whatnot </em>into the West End.</p>

<p>Of course, one of the first issues was Peter Bridge wanted a new director. It was all very well Alan directing in the regions, but it was never going to happen for the West End. The experienced director Warren Jenkins came in and then the ensemble company was all but replaced by several star names.</p>

<p><em>“I think, in retrospect,&nbsp;Mr Whatnot&nbsp;was a charming piece done in the round in Stoke. It was a team of actors who’d worked together for a long time and joined in the spirit of it and it then got the West End treatment, which was to echo through the rest of my life. In the West End, it was over-produced. It had a very starry cast, Ronnie Barker, Ronnie Stevens, Marie Law, Judy Campbell and all the right ingredients, but it just didn’t add up. And what is more, it was funny when we did it in Stoke.”</em></p>

<p>As Alan notes, his West End experience of <em>Mr Whatnot</em> was just a foretaste of what was to come. Whilst the vast majority of his shows were far more successful in the West End, he was never entirely happy with the experience and the compromises he had to make over the subsequent decades.</p>

<p>The West End production budget of <em>Mr Whatnot</em> was massive compared to the original and the joy of a play which left everything to the imagination thanks to its beautifully conceived sound plot left little to the imagination once it got into the West End. Essentially all that had made it a success in Stoke was jettisoned or lost in the process of bringing it to London.</p>

</div>

<p>And the critics were waiting…</p>

<p><em>“That was a sort of St Valentine’s Day Massacre. I mean, everyone had their machine guns out, and quite rightly as it turned out! That was the case of a play growing out of a small regional concept and being presented vastly overblown in a West End context. It was like a grossly-over-dressed Christmas tree that fell over.”</em></p>

<p><em>“There were some real tigers prowling around then [when&nbsp;Mr Whatnot&nbsp;opened in London]. Levin was in full swing, Shulman at his most venomous. I went to bed for two days after their reviews and seriously thought of going back to acting and directing. Now I look at the returns rather than the reviews, but I’m still painfully sensitive to criticism.”</em></p>

<p>The reviews were shockingly bad – in the case of Shulman, it’s actually difficult to reproduce it verbatim as it would now be deemed extremely offensive in its language. It was, as Alan notes, as though he were being put back in his place and told to go back where he belonged in the regions.</p>

<p>The West End experience didn’t mark the end of <em>Mr Whatnot</em> though – probably largely because of the number of people who had seen and enjoyed it in Stoke and saw it as it was meant to be. Within a few years, it had been made available for production and did become popular. Never as much as, say, <em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></strong></em> or <em><strong><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves">How The Other Half Loves</a></strong></em>, but professionals and amateurs revived it and its reputation was largely restored.</p>

<p>But <em>Mr Whatnot </em>will always have the unfortunate distinction of being the least successful of Alan’s West End ventures. Yes, <em><strong><a href="/plays/jeeves">Jeeves</a></strong></em> is heralded as his biggest West End flop, but it ran for as twice as long as <em>Mr Whatnot</em> and the reviews whilst harsh, were nowhere near as destructive.</p>

<p>Of course, there is the question, what would have happened if <em>Mr Whatnot</em> had been a success in the West End? How would it have affected Alan’s life and career?</p>

<p>The answer, I suspect, is more profound than many would realise given the failure of <em>Mr Whatnot</em> is an inflection point in Sir Alan’s life. Had it not failed, he wouldn’t have joined the BBC, Stephen Joseph wouldn’t have commissioned him to write what became <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, he likely wouldn’t have reconnected with the Library Theatre in Scarborough.</p>

<p>Without these, Alan’s life and career would have taken a very different direction and, personally, I find it hard to believe he would have achieved the same success and significance as he would go on to enjoy because of the failure of <em>Mr Whatnot</em>.</p>

<p>But that’s another article for another day.</p>
