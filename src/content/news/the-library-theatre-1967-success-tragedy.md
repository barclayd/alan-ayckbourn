<p>As visitors to the blog will be aware, we’ve been celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in 1955.</p>

<p>With the anniversary year in full swing, the blog comes closer to home with a look at all the plays Alan Ayckbourn wrote for <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> between 1959 and 1976.</p>

<p>Over the coming months, we’ll be looking at each of his Library Theatre plays in-depth, providing some insight and background into the creation of some of the playwright’s most famous – and obscure – works.</p>

<p>We begin in 1959 with the play which launched one of the most successful British playwriting careers of the 20th century with <em><strong><a href="http://thesquarecat .alanayckbourn.net">The Square Cat</a></strong></em>.</p>

<p><strong>The Square Cat (1959)</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>In 1959, Alan Ayckbourn’s first play <em>The Square Cat</em> premiered at the Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, beginning a remarkable career which has brought the playwright international success and acclaim for more than six decades.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn had been writing in some form ever since he was a child, largely thanks to his mother who was a successful writer herself. There is evidence of Alan writing throughout his school career, although he apparently had no intention of becoming a playwright (he did briefly consider journalism for a career) and his initial steps into theatre were as an actor.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Alan Ayckbourn (as Jerry Wattis) in The Square Cat on a promotional postcard (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>In 1957, he joined the Studio Theatre Ltd company based at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, where he met Stephen Joseph, who had founded the company in 1955 with the intention of promoting both new writing and performance in-the-round.</p>

<p>It was a fortuitous and life-changing meeting. Stephen was an inspirational figure who would become a mentor and father-figure to Alan and have an impact on the rest of his life.</p>

<p>Stephen became aware of Alan’s interest in writing and encouraged Alan to try his hand at play-writing. Between 1957 and 1959, Alan estimates that he wrote going on for a dozen unproduced play-scripts, most of which were subsequently lost or destroyed.</p>

<p>In Christmas 1958, Alan expressed dissatisfaction with the role he was playing in David Campton’s play, <em><strong><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-19/page-3/page44/page44.html">Ring of Roses</a></strong></em>, and complained to Stephen about the quality of roles he was being offered. Stephen’s reply was that if he wanted a better role, he should write it himself – but to write the main part for himself (presumably in the belief that he would work hard not to humiliate himself as an actor if he had to star in the play).</p>

<p>That the play he wrote, <em>The Square Cat</em>, was centred around the lead character Jerry Wattis (and Alan Ayckbourn) can be seen from the fact it is literally an all-singing and dancing part; Jerry plays the guitar, sings and dances. Despite writing the role for himself, Alan could do none of these things well, if at all!</p>

<p>Until 2001, it was widely assumed and repeatedly reported the play that Alan had disliked so much was John van Druten’s <em>Bell, Book And Candle</em>. As Alan’s biographer, Paul Allen, correctly ascertained this patently couldn’t have been the case as the van Druten play was in the same season as <em>The Square Cat</em> and both were advertised on the first flyers for the 1959 summer season.</p>

<p>Considering Alan had been telling the story for at least 20 years (it features in Ian Watson’s <em>Conversations With Ayckbourn</em> from 1981, but was purportedly being told years before even that), it’s extraordinary to think it took so long to discover the truth behind this pivotal moment in the playwright’s life.</p>

<p>The truth, as Paul Allen discovered whilst interviewing the playwright David Campton, was that Alan made his complaint whilst appearing in Campton’s <em>Ring Of Roses</em> in December 1958. Alan subsequently changed the title of the offending play in interviews so as, presumably, not to upset a friend and fellow playwright. Subsequent to the biography’s publication, Alan Ayckbourn confirmed this was actually the true version of events.</p>

<p><em>The Square Cat</em> was written during the Studio Theatre Ltd company’s tour in early 1959 (probably at some point between January and March) and research suggests Alan was writing the play whilst appearing in a production of Harold Pinter’s play <strong><em><a href="http://plays.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/offsite-19/page-3/page68/page68.html">The Birthday Party</a></em></strong>, which was directed by Pinter himself in his professional directing debut.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>David Campton, William Elmhirst &amp; Dona Martyn in The Square Cat (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>This production came about after Stephen Joseph – who knew Pinter from Central School of Speech and Drama – offered him the chance to re-stage the play following the original production’s critical mauling in London. Alan has said on many occasions Pinter is one of his major writing inspirations and that playing Stanley in <em>The Birthday Party</em> and being directed by Pinter was an extraordinary experience.</p>

<p><em>The Square Cat</em> took two weeks to write and was credited to a pseudonym, Roland Allen, as it was actually a joint effort between Alan and Christine Roland (Alan’s first wife who he married later that year in May 1959); the pseudonym comprises elements from both their names. In a contemporary interview, Christine expressed surprise at how quickly they had written the play noting, “Alan’s mother, Mary James, writes for women’s magazines, and he seems to have the same talent for writing.”</p>

<p>Paul Allen believes the collaboration was based on Christine’s knowledge of stage-craft and Alan’s “nascent gift for plotting and dialogue.” Alan later said the pseudonym of Roland Allen served two purposes, the first to credit Christine’s contribution to <em>The Square Cat</em> and, the second, to differentiate the writer from the actor, as Alan was still intent on following the acting route at this point in his career.</p>

<p>Even so, the true author of the play was no great secret as it was reported in several articles within regional newspapers covering the Scarborough area. Perhaps what is more confusing is Roland Allen was misspelt in the original Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre programme for the play as Roland Allan!</p>

<p>The title of the play, which was subtitled ‘A cool comedy’, was a play on 1950s slang language suggesting someone who was both boring and cool. While ‘square’ survives as a connotation of dull and boring, ‘cat’ was then slang for someone who was cool. This describes the two aspects of the lead character: the introspective young man, Arthur Brummage, and his extrovert alter-ego rock-star, Jerry Wattis. The play itself had three acts and was played without an interval; which was one of Stephen Joseph’s quirks with the majority of productions of Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre performed without an interval during its formative years.</p>

<p>Although it would be wrong to suggest the play offers much insight into the playwright Alan Ayckbourn would become – and Alan has noted it had no aspirations other than to entertain – <em>The Square Cat</em> significantly incorporates an unhappy marriage between Sidney and Alice Glover. Although there is a happy ending, a notable cut scene in which the pair face off with Alice noting her husband’s short-comings, lends credence that this is the first example of what will become a very common Ayckbourn theme, that of – as the critic Michael Billington labels it – “unassuageable female discontent.”</p>

<p>Although not acknowledged at the time, Alan realised more than twenty years after writing it, the play was significantly influenced by Anouilh’s play <em>Dinner With The Family</em> in which the aristocratic protagonist meets a simple girl whom he falls in love with and creates an alter ego to appeal to her. His family find out and attempt to thwart him, but love prevails despite all.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Faynia Jeffery &amp; Alan Ayckbourn (as Arthur Brummage) in The Square Cat (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Correspondence held by Scarborough Library indicates <em>The Square Cat</em> was not initially planned to be part of the 1959 summer season. In a letter to the Mervyn Edwards, Director of Libraries, dated 11 March 1959, Stephen Joseph named the plays planned for the summer season which did not include <em>The Square Cat</em>. Alan believes he may not have finished writing the play at that point and the play was later incorporated into the season once Stephen had seen it and judged it was of high enough quality to produce; this seems plausible given the winter tours traditionally ran from January to March and Alan would still have been on tour when Stephen wrote the letter.</p>

<p><em>The Square Cat</em> premiered at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre on 30 July 1959, directed by Stephen Joseph and starring Alan alongside David Campton, William Elmhirst, Faynia Jeffery and Dona Martyn. It was an immense success for the company, following on from a poorly received adaptation of Mary Shelley’s <em>Frankenstein</em> by David Campton.</p>

<p>In a letter to the Arts Council the day after <em>The Square Cat</em> opened, Stephen Joseph noted the opening night had seen the best house of the season so far. Such was its success that Stephen decided to cancel the second run of <em>Frankenstein</em> (the theatre was already working to a repertory season) and to extend the second run of <em>The Square Cat</em> by a week; in a letter Stephen Joseph wrote: “The advance booking for <em>The Square Cat</em> has been so heavy that we felt it only fair to the author to make the most of his success.”</p>

<p>This was the first time a play at the Library Theatre ran for two consecutive weeks and Alan received £47 from these performances. According to the financial accounts for the season, the play was seen by 3,340 people during its three weeks of performance and made £695. A number of the performances were booked to capacity and a contemporary newspaper report quotes Christine Roland as saying she had met one woman who had enjoyed the play so much she had seen it six times!</p>

<p>The success of <em>The Square Cat</em> led Stephen Joseph to commission Alan to write a second play almost immediately. The regional press announced in August that Alan was working on a second script, <em><strong><a href="/plays/love-after-all">Love After All</a></strong></em>, for the winter season at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p><em>The Square Cat</em> was produced only once more when the Studio Theatre company went on tour in early 1960 and it was performed at the Municipal Hall, Newcastle-under-Lyme, with Barry Boys performing the lead role; the role being re-cast due to Alan being called up for his short-lived National Service.</p>

<p>By the mid-1970s, it was largely believed the play no longer existed as Alan, having withdrawn his early plays, noted on several occasions that he intended to destroy every copy of his early scripts. Fortunately, this was not the case as at least five original manuscripts of the play still exist today. Original manuscripts are held in the Ayckbourn Archive at the <strong><a href="https://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a></strong> at the University Of York, The Ayckbourn Collection at <strong><a href="https://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Scarborough Museums and Galleries</a></strong>, in The Lord Chamberlain’s Collection at the British Library and in the Stephen Joseph Collection in the John Rylands Library at the University Of Manchester.</p>

<p>Sixty-six years after it was premiered in Scarborough, it’s strange to think this play – which would not have been seen as a particularly significant at the time – was pivotal for both Alan and Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre.</p>

<p>It is the play which launched one of the most successful British playwriting careers of the twentieth century and, in so doing, it enabled Alan Ayckbourn to achieve the success needed to ensure the Scarborough company survived and continued past Stephen’s death in 1967, preserving his legacy in a theatre which still survives today, still working to Stephen Joseph’s founding principles of producing new writing in the round.</p>
