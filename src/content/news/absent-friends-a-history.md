<p><em>2024 marks the 50th anniversary of the world premiere of Alan Ayckbourn’s classic play, <strong><a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a></strong>.</em></p>

<p><em>Here, the playwright’s archivist Simon Murgatroyd M.A. looks at the history of the play from its conception to world premiere in Scarborough, its disappointment in the West End to launching a star in New York.</em></p>

<p><strong>Absent Friends</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>In 1973, Alan Ayckbourn wrote his most ambitious work yet with <strong><em><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></em></strong> trilogy. Three inter-connected plays chronicling the events of a single weekend from the perspective of three different locations.</p>

<p>It was an extraordinary challenge to both write and direct and when he came to consider his play for the following year, he knew he had to go in a completely different direction. In the wake of his suburban epic, he wrote a play which was the polar opposite in scale, but no less ambitious or important.</p>

<div>

</div>

<p>In fact, the playwright himself considers <em>Absent Friends</em> to be one of his most significant works in his development as a writer.</p>

<p><em>Absent Friends</em> actually developed from a moment in<em> The Norman Conquests</em> when Alan realised he had taken a positive step in a new direction. At one stage in <em>Living Together</em>, he needed to write a single scene which had no significance, did not carry the plot forward nor contained any singular revelation.</p>

<p>It was a pause in the proceedings with several characters just sitting down and talking. It was, he later admitted, the hardest part of the trilogy to write and the one that scared him the most as he had never attempted such a scene before. Once performed, he was delighted by the reaction to the scene and he decided to write a play entirely driven by six people who, essentially, just sat down and talked.</p>

<p>A play that had little in terms of plot, but was driven entirely by the actions and reactions of six people in a living room. A play in which he notes “nothing happened.” </p>

<p>Despite having this idea, it was still not easy to bring to fruition judging by the surviving hand-written notes held in the Ayckbourn Archive at the Borthwick Institute for Archives at the University of York.</p>

<p>What <em>Absent Friends</em> tends to be known for is it was Alan Ayckbourn’s first play to be set in ‘real time’ – elapsed stage time being the same as actual time elapsed. However, his notes indicate its was not conceived with this in mind and had a very different structure to its eventual form.</p>

<p>The earliest notes indicate the play was to be either three or four acts and set in a dining room. The play would centre on dinner with one act viewed entirely through the eyes of the men, followed by the same dinner viewed through the eyes of the women.</p>

<p>The men and women would then be brought together for the final act, presumably highlighting the different perspectives and differences of experience of the dinner. This concept was abandoned before Alan began writing the play, but offers an intriguing insight into a play which appears to have been closer related to the more technical plays which preceded it.</p>

<p>By the time Alan had settled on the structure of the actual play and began writing, he had stripped it of all artifice and made it structurally the simplest of all his plays so far with a single setting, two act play set performed in actual playing time.</p>

</div>

<p>Indeed, the real time element is really what kick-starts the play giving it an extraordinary intimacy and almost voyeuristic feel for the audience who feel they are almost in the living room with these characters. This technique offered an extreme “close-up” of the play’s themes and characters, which – according to the critic Michael Billington – makes it a play of “ruthless simplicity.”</p>

<p>There is also a very real sense, acknowledged by the playwright, that he was drawing himself in. <em>The Norman Conquests</em> had been a remarkable success but had required an immense amount of work from writing to directing in order to bring it to fruition. For its follow-up, Alan wanted to create something that was diametrically opposite in both style and tone.</p>

<p>It was also a risky play dealing with – as described below – death and our attitudes to it as well as, in the playwright’s words, “the death of love”. He once described <em>Absent Friends</em> to Peter Hall, then Artistic Director of the National Theatre, as “a miniature” and something he was compelled to write following the trilogy.</p>

<p>A major inspiration for the play was also – unusually for the playwright – a specific event during his life in which he attended a tea party for a woman whose husband had died during their honeymoon. Alan’s fascination with his and the attendees reaction to her insistence theirs would have been a perfect marriage and how their reactions reflected on their own relationships directly inspired the play.</p>

<p>As was normal for the period, Alan wrote the play very late in the day, but in this case, he took it to the extreme. Alan’s writing pattern during the 1960s to late 1980s generally involved an idea gestating for the better part of a year before, essentially fully realised, he would either hand-write or dictate it in the final week before rehearsals began.</p>

<p><em>Absent Friends</em> was scheduled to open the summer season at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, with rehearsals beginning on Tuesday 28 May. However, subsequent to the planning of the Scarborough season, <em>The Norman Conquests</em> was optioned by Greenwich Theatre for its London premiere. Alan was committed to helping its director, Eric Thompson, with rehearsals during April and May meaning the window between rehearsals periods was alarmingly tight.</p>

<p>Alan left <em>The Norman Conquests</em> rehearsals in London for Scarborough on Thursday 23 May and, having not written a single word of the play, began writing <em>Absent Friends</em> that day, working through the night and the next day. He finished the play on the night of Friday 24 May, collapsing into bed, before the play was typed up and duplicated at his home on the Saturday before being delivered to the actors on the Sunday. The read-through took place on Monday 27 May with rehearsals starting the next day.</p>

<p>It is extraordinary that Alan delivered <em>Absent Friends</em> fully-formed in just two days. There were apparently very few alterations to the script during the rehearsal periods, as again was the norm for him.</p>

<p><em>Absent Friends</em> opened the 1974 summer season on 17 June at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, where Alan was entering his second year as full-time Artistic Director. It starred Janet Dale, Christopher Godwin, Ronald Herdman, Stephen Mallatratt, Eileen O’Brien and Heather Stoney.</p>

<p>Considering that <em>Absent Friends</em> is considered a classic Ayckbourn of the period, it is worth noting that it had two very different receptions. The world premiere production received unanimously positive press and had a sold out run. The Scarborough Evening News reported that up to 100 people were being turned away at many performances and extra matinees were scheduled to cope with demand. Such was the popularity of the production that it had its run extended by a week from 23 – 28 September.</p>

<p>The audience reaction was very positive and Alan’s biographer, Paul Allen, notes it demonstrated Alan’s ability to hold “an audience’s attention without a dazzling plot or an innovative narrative device or a bombardment of comedy.” </p>

<p>This is vital to consider in light of what happened when the play transferred to London. One of the major recurring problems of studying Alan Ayckbourn’s plays is the emphasis put on the London productions and the reaction to them. This is understandable, but fails to appreciate the relationship Alan has with theatre in the round in Scarborough, and the importance it has to him as writer and director.</p>

<p>Alan premieres his plays in Scarborough in the venue they are conceived for (i.e. the round). He has complete control over the casting and directs the play, again worth emphasising, predominantly in the intimacy of the round. The world premiere is not a try-out for London or other venues, it is the play produced as the playwright intended; often it is considered the definitive version of the play. When it transfers to London, even with Alan directing, it is frequently a large step away from his original vision – rarely with the cast he would ideally choose and almost never in a suitable venue.</p>

<p>In many cases, this has not necessarily affected the success of the play or how it was perceived; with <em>Absent Friends</em> it was almost a disaster.</p>

<p>Alan’s regular London producer Michael Codron had optioned the play for the West End immediately after it opened in Scarborough and there was a relatively quick turn-around. The play opened 13 months after premièring in Scarborough whilst <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em> and <em>The Norman Conquests</em> were still running in London. With five plays running simultaneously in the West End, Alan had set a new record.*</p>

<p>But, critically, it also meant <em>Absent Friends</em> was sharing the same spotlight as two of Alan’s most notable West End successes.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Eileen O’Brien &amp; Janet Dale in the world premiere of Absent Friends (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Eric Thompson, who had so successfully tackled <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> and <em>The Norman Conquests</em> in the West End, was brought in to direct <em>Absent Friends</em> and he assembled a notable cast which included Richard Briers as Colin and Peter Bowles as Paul. But Eric’s correspondence with Alan indicates he was not entirely comfortable with the play and wondered whether this was a play too far for him, particularly as he was also concerned and pre-occupied with thoughts of directing the New York premiere of <em>The Norman Conquests</em> later in the same year.</p>

<p>He was right to be concerned.</p>

<p><em>Absent Friends</em> opened at the Garrick Theatre on 23 July 1975 and was met with a frosty reception from the critics; the cast received plaudits, the play did not. It was a response that Alan largely shared himself, later saying he did not care for the London production.</p>

<p>What seems obvious in hindsight was the play struggled to move from the intimate theatre in the round space it was intended for, to a large proscenium arch theatre such as the Garrick. Alan once described <em>Absent Friends</em> as an almost voyeuristic play with the audience practically eavesdropping on events and that it was best suited to intimate spaces, not the cavernous spaces of the West End.</p>

<p>Only the noted critic Harold Hobson, one of the most influential critics writing at the time, was very taken by the play: “If it is the saddest and most moving thing he has written, it is also the most clear-sighted and the funniest.”</p>

<p><em>Absent Friends</em> would run for a respectable nine months in London, but could not compare in success to <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> and <em>The Norman Conquests.</em> With Alan and Andrew Lloyd Webber’s doomed musical <em><strong><a href="/plays/jeeves">Jeeves</a></strong></em> closing after just a month also in 1975, it was a significant time for Alan in London and must have played a large part in Alan’s increasingly uncomfortable relationship with the West End. It would also mark the last time Eric Thompson would direct one of Alan’s play in London.</p>

<p>Change was coming.</p>

<p>Running concurrent with the period from Alan writing <em>Absent Friends</em> to its West End staging was a correspondence between the playwright and Peter Hall, Artistic Director of the National Theatre. Hall wanted Alan to write a new play for the soon to open new home of the National Theatre on London’s South Bank. He very much admired <em>Absent Friends</em> and showed considerable interest in staging it until it was confirmed that Michael Codron was taking the play into London.</p>

</div>

<p>Hall then wrote to Codron asking him if there was any chance the National might have it, given there were already four Ayckbourn plays running in the West End. Codron’s response was that he could not see the request being granted if it were the other way round! The enthusiasm with which Hall pursued the play and Alan can only have re-enforced Alan’s perception after the critical reaction to <em>Absent Friends</em> and <em>Jeeves</em> that there must be a better way to transfer his work to London. He agreed to write a new play for the National Theatre with <em><strong><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></strong></em> premiering in 1977.</p>

<p>This began a period of 14 years where the London premieres of his plays would be roughly evenly split between the National Theatre and the West End.</p>

<p>Both <em>Absurd Person Singular</em> and <em>The Norman Conquests</em> had also had successful transfers to America and there was some early interest in taking <em>Absent Friends</em> to New York too. An initial production in 1976 at the Long Wharf Theater, New Haven, directed by Eric Thompson, led to a revival of the production the following year at the Kennedy Center, Washington, with the obvious intent of transferring to Broadway, but this did not occur.</p>

<p>It would not play in New York until a subsequent revival in February 1991 by the Manhattan Theatre Club at the City Center Stage which featured Brenda Blethyn (winning the Theatre World Award for her portrayal of Diana), Peter Flechette and, in her first major professional theatre role, Gillian Anderson as Evelyn. The young actress followed the play soon afterwards by stepping into the role of Agent Scully in the hugely successful and long-running conspiracy television series <em>The X Files</em> before becoming one of the most noted stage and screen actors of her generation.</p>

<p>Despite its issues in the West End, the play found its home in the UK in regional theatres. Almost as soon as it was available, it was snapped up and produced numerous times by professional revenues around the country as well as Germany, where it was particularly successful. Its reputation began to be restored by more sympathetic productions in venues better suited to its small scale.</p>

<p>It was also helped by the BBC’s 1985 television adaptation, which marked a rare occasion when Alan was largely satisfied with the adaptation of one of his plays to television.</p>

<p>This was significantly helped by two of its performances with a – at the time – rare television appearance by the Ayckbourn veteran, Tom Courtenay as well as a role-defining performance by Julia McKenzie’s performance as Diana; Alan largely credits this as later securing her the role of Susan in the West End production of <em><strong><a href="/plays/woman-in-mind">Woman In Mind</a></strong></em>.</p>

<p>Although not a perfect adaptation of the play – and the playwright has considerable issues with Disndale Landen’s interpretation of Paul – it remains one of the strongest television adaptations of his work. Unfortunately it has never been released commercially, although in 2019, it was screened cinematically as part of the Ayckbourn Film Festival at the Stephen Joseph Theatre celebrating the playwright’s 80th birthday.</p>

<p>The reputation of Absent Friends has grown over the decades and it has become one of the most frequently revived Ayckbourn plays in the UK with Alan himself returning to the play twice with revivals at the Alley Theatre, Houston, in 1982 and in Scarborough in 1997. For that, the role of Evelyn was given to the actress Tamzin Outhwaite in her first major acting role. Soon after she would get a high profile part in the long-running soap opera <em>EastEnders</em> and go on to even further success on screen and stage.</p>

<p><em>Absent Friends</em> remains one of Alan’s favourite plays – in no small part due to how it initially was treated in London; he always favours the plays he feels have been treated less well than others – and it stands as one of his most significant plays of the 1970s. </p>

<p>By eschewing the technicality of his previous works which he had become associated with and reducing the play to its barest minimum constituent parts, he clearly demonstrated that he required no more than six people in a single room to create captivating and challenging drama.</p>

<p>It was a significant moment in his development as a playwright and would have an enormous influence on his subsequent character-driven works.</p>

<p>Fifty years on, it inarguably stands in the pantheon of great Ayckbourn works.</p>
