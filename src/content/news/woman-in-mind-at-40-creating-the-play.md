<p>2025 marks the 40th anniversary of Alan Ayckbourn’s award-winning and acclaimed play <em>Woman in Mind</em> – soon to be revived in the <strong><a href="http://www.womaninmindplay.com">West End</a></strong> starring Sheridan Smith.</p>

<p>With the exciting news of this major revival during the play’s anniversary year, the blog is looking back to the creation of <em><strong><a href="/plays/woman-in-mind">Woman in Mind</a></strong></em> and how Alan Ayckbourn came to write one of his most memorable works.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2025/1985-woman-in-mind-w350.jpg" alt="" width="350" height="488" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption><sub>The poster for the world premiere of Woman in Mind in 1985 (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><em>Woman In Mind</em> was inspired by Alan’s desire to write a play which was essentially cinematic in approach without cutting away from the main character or featuring cumbersome scene changes.</p>

<p>It is – uniquely for Alan – a subjective play told entirely from the stand-point of its protagonist, Susan, who never leaves the stage. Everything we see is essentially through her eyes; we experience what Susan experiences, what she sees, what she  hears. Consequently, as we discover, that information may not be entirely reliable.</p>

<p>The play had been indirectly inspired by the film <em>Dead On Arrival</em>, which is notably a first-person story in which the final revelation (Spoilers ahead…) is the narrator is dead. Alan had also recently read Oliver Sacks’s acclaimed book <em>The Man Who Mistook His Wife For A Hat</em>, which explored how the brain functions and the consequences when something goes wrong. That is not to say <em>Woman in Mind</em> draws on actual cases or conditions – as Alan emphasised in contemporary interviews, he did not do any actual medical research into the lead character Susan’s condition, instead drawing it from observation, experience and imagination.</p>

<p>Alan initially conceived the play with a man as the central character, who suffers a heart attack as a direct result of which he is able to hear what was going on around him but is unable to communicate with anyone. Oddly, this mirrored Alan’s own experiences 21 years later when he had a stroke and one of the short-term after-effects was he found himself swapping ‘yes’ for ‘no’ and vice versa. At one point, he desperately recalls wanting a drink of water, but every time he was asked he said ‘no’, despite frustratingly wanting to say ‘yes’!</p>

<p>However, when it came to writing the play, Alan realised a female protagonist would make the play much more powerful and the protagonist much more sympathetic.</p>

<p><em>Woman in Mind</em> also drew upon wider social issues. As the critic and one of his biographers Michael Billington has noted, this was a period when Alan was tackling significant societal issues such as the pervading influence of the media in <em><strong><a href="/plays/man-of-the-moment">Man of the Moment</a></strong></em>, morality in the modern world in <em><strong><a href="/plays/a-small-family-business">A Small Family Business</a></strong></em> and the nature of the creative artist in <em><strong><a href="/plays/henceforward">Henceforward…</a></strong></em>. Billington felt <em>Woman in Mind</em> was tackling organised religion in the late 20th century and its possible shortcomings in contemporary society.</p>

<p>At this point in his career, Alan was still writing to the latest possible deadline – occasionally the day before rehearsals began! <em>Woman in Mind</em>, written in Scarborough during April 1985, was unusual in that he finished it a good week earlier than anticipated. He immediately sent it to his agent Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay noting he was excited by the direction it had taken and that he felt it a step forward in his writing.</p>

<figure><figcaption><sub>Ursula Jones as Susan in the world premiere of Woman in Mind (© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Peggy liked the play but had doubts that audiences would easily take to such a demanding work. Alan disagreed, noting – as he had done before and since – that audiences are too often underestimated. That there is nothing wrong with either challenging them or presenting  plays which might generate wildly differing responses depending on the experiences of the audience. He notably said of the play, “There are always going to be those who find the tragedy outweighs the comedy and vice versa. And, depending on the night of the week, this balance will alter quite drastically.”</p>

<p>There has often been the question of whether the play is to any extent autobiographical and draws on his experiences with his own mother and her own breakdown. Alan has always been quick to deny this though and that he feels it no more reflects his life and experiences than his other works. “Most of my plays at some level are autobiographical. I think <em>Woman in Mind</em>, in the end, is no more and no less than many others.”</p>

<p>However, he did once tell the actor Martin Jarvis – who played Susan’s husband, Gerald, in the West End production – that his character was inspired by real life. He told Jarvis, “Gerald’s based on my step-father, you know. I remember once when he was saying something sanctimonious one mealtime, my mother picked up a bowl of potatoes and emptied the bowl over his head. And the expression on his face was something like long-suffering, sacrificial ‘forgive her lord for she knows not what she does’, something like that.’</p>

<p>Like the vast majority of Alan’s plays, <em>Woman in Mind</em> premiered in Scarborough. It opened on 30 May 1985 at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round – the company’s home between 1976 – 1996. That it was a play unlike anything Alan had previously written was certain – but it was also a complete surprise to audiences as they had no expectations primarily as a result of how late Alan wrote his plays.</p>

<p>This is exemplified by the extraordinary copy promoting the play in the theatre’s summer brochure: “At the time of going to press a high wall of secrecy surrounds this project. Some have the theory that the reason for this is to protect such highly original comic material from the risk of plagiarism. Others, more cynical, suggest that it could be due to the fact that the author hasn’t started on it yet and is anxious not to commit himself.” It is a testament to the relationship between Alan and his Scarborough audience that they took his work on faith and turned up no matter how much or how little they knew about the play!</p>

<p>The production featured the company stalwart Russell Dixon as Susan’s husband Gerald, Ursula Jones as Susan and introduced Barry McCarthy as Bill; he would go on to become a fixture of the company and is now regarded as a quintessential Ayckbourn actor. The company was completed by Tom Bowles, Robin Herford, John Hudson, Heather Stoney and Caroline Webster.</p>

<p>The run of the play was a huge success and practically full to capacity, yet the critics were divided in their reactions to the play. Several felt this was a brave move into new territory for the playwright, whilst others were concerned Alan was moving into far darker territory than he was generally associated with and questioned whether audiences would stick with him or the play. Considering the plays which followed, they had no idea how dark his plays would turn during this period!</p>

<p>The reviews did not deter Alan’s long-time West End producer Michael Codron, who was determined to take the play into London. In his biography, Codron is quoted as saying “I thought – and still think – that it’s in his very top rank.”</p>

<p>The only problem was who to cast as Susan? A number of high profile names such as Judi Dench and Penelope Wilton were considered, but the final decision – bizarrely – was someone who Alan and Codron had earlier dismissed as unsuited to the role!</p>

<p>Julia McKenzie had been considered – having appeared in the West End premiere of <em><strong><a href="/plays/ten-times-table">Ten Times Table</a></strong></em> and the West End recast of <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></strong></em> – but both Alan and and Codron initially thought she wasn’t quite right for the role and that, largely known as a musical theatre actress at the time, it was too much of a gamble to cast her.</p>

<p>And then Alan saw the BBC’s television adaptation of his play <em><strong><a href="/plays/absent-friends">Absent Friends</a></strong></em> in September 1985 and was dazzled by her central performance as Diana.  He thought she was ‘superb’ in the role and wrote to Codron suggesting “I do think our thoughts that she might be too lightweight for Susan in <em>Woman in Mind</em> should be retracted,”</p>

<p>Even Julia herself was initially unconvinced she was right for the role, but with Alan as a steady hand and guide, she took on this extremely emotionally and physically challenging role and proved, undoubtedly, the decision to cast her had been inspired. She was joined by Martin Harris as Gerald alongside Christina Barryk, Benedick Blythe, Peter Blythe, Daniel Flynn, John Hudson and Josephine Tewson.</p>

<figure><figcaption><sub>Julia McKenzie as Susan in the West End premiere of Woman in Mind (© John Haynes)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>The play opened at the Vaudeville Theatre in London in September 1986 and was greeted with a very different set of reviews to that of Scarborough. The critics agreed the play had matured between Scarborough and London and that it seemed better suited to the proscenium arch; Alan disagreed with this observation – understandably given his love of in-the-round and how the play had been conceived for theatre-in-the-round – believing the production instead had become too complex in its staging.</p>

<p>Julia McKenzie was gifted with stunning reviews and would win the Evening Standard Award for Best Actress.  The production would run for 10 months and include a cast change which saw Pauline Collins take over as Susan.</p>

<p>The success of <em>Woman In Mind</em> in London could not have come at a better time fro Alan. It was the final play he wrote before he took a two year sabbatical from Scarborough to become a company director at the <strong><a href="/career/national-theatre">National Theatre</a></strong>. <em>Woman in Mind</em>‘s West End production coincided with the start of Alan’s tenure at the National which would see him win great acclaim for his award-winning production of Arthur Miller’s <em><strong><a href="/plays/miller-arthur">A View From The Bridge</a></strong></em> and his latest play, <em>A Small Family Business</em>.</p>

<p>The darker themes of <em>Woman In Mind</em> carried through to <em>A Small Family Business</em> (1987) and beyond as Alan’s next play <em>Henceforward…</em> (1987) ventured into even darker territory. All of these led to critics re-evaluating Ayckbourn, noting both his gifts as a director and also that there was far more depth to his playwriting than many had previously given him credit for.</p>

<p>The legacy of W<em>oman In Mind</em> was also a powerful one. The audience reaction to the piece in London went above and beyond anyone’s expectations with both Alan and Julia receiving correspondence from people who had experienced similar traumatic events in their lives and empathised with what Julia was portraying on stage. For both actor and playwright, it must have been a sobering realisation of the ordeal some women were going through in their lives. That is not to say the laughter was not there too and although both men and women enjoyed the play, Alan subsequently observed: “I think the women in the audience stop laughing long before the men.”</p>

<p><em>Woman In Mind</em>&nbsp;has subsequently comes to be regarded as a defining play in Alan Ayckbourn’s writing career and is frequently cited as featuring one of the standout and most challenging stage roles for women written during that time.</p>

<p>On Broadway, Stockard Channing gave an award-winning performance in 1988 with Helen Mirren tackling the role in Los Angeles in 1992. In 2008, Alan Ayckbourn revived the play himself with Janie Dee as Susan and saw the production transfer to the West End in 2009 – his directorial swan-song in the West End – with a widely praised production. More recently, in 2022, Jenna Russell tackled the role for Chichester Festival Theatre.</p>

<p>And now, on its 40th anniversary, <em>Woman in Mind</em> is being revived with Sheridan Smith as Susan and Michael Longhurst directing. There is every possibility this will not only bring the play to a new generation of audiences, but also cement the play’s reputation as one of the single most significant works of Alan Ayckbourn’s long and lauded playwriting career.</p>
