<p><strong>The Road to By Jeeves</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p><em>“I knew if I did revive Jeeves, every press person in the land would come running, wanting to see a disaster a second time. Which was quite nice because they didn’t get to.” (Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<p>In 1996, Alan Ayckbourn and Andrew Lloyd Webber unveiled their – mostly – new musical <em><a href="/plays/jeeves">By Jeeves</a></em>. It was a practically complete re-write of their flop 1975 West End musical <em><a href="/plays/jeeves">Jeeves</a></em> and its fortunes could not have been more different.</p>

<p>Whilst the creation of <em>By Jeeves</em> was nowhere near as torturous or eventful as that of the original musical <em>Jeeves</em> (as we previously chronicled in the article <a href="http://archivingayckbourn.home.blog/2020/04/23/behind-the-scenes-of-jeeves/">here</a>), there is still an interesting story to be told of just how the idea of rehabilitating <em>Jeeves</em> entranced both Alan Ayckbourn and Andrew Lloyd Webber and how it took 20 years to realise that dream.</p>

<p><em>Jeeves</em> was a musical inspired by the novels of P.G. Wodehouse with book and lyrics by Alan Ayckbourn and music by Andrew Lloyd Webber. It closed at Her Majesty’s Theatre, London, on 24 May 1975 after just 38 performances. It made a financial loss into the tens of thousands of pounds and critical notices were unremittingly harsh. That, realistically, should have been the end of <em>Jeeves</em>.</p>

<p>However, neither Lloyd Webber nor Alan were inclined to let their one flop escape them and, more significantly, they both had a genuine affection for the musical and a desire to do it justice.&nbsp;</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Andrew Lloyd Webber &amp; Alan Ayckbourn during the press launch of By Jeeves at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round in 1996<br>(© The Scarborough News)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Fortuitously, despite the immediate disappointment of the musical’s failure, Lloyd Webber had been objective enough to listen to the words of the great American musical producer Hal Prince, who Lloyd Webber recalled advised him to “bank the score after <em>Jeeves</em> flopped” confident one day it would find its home.</p>

<p>Despite the set-back of <em>Jeeves</em>, it’s crucial to emphasise it made barely a dent in the theatrical fortunes of either men. Both went on to follow <em>Jeeves</em> with great success with their next works: Alan with <em><a href="/plays/bedroom-farce">Bedroom Farce</a></em> – which he had finished writing on the day <em>Jeeves</em> closed in the West End – and Lloyd Webber with <em>Evita</em>.</p>

<p><em>Jeeves</em>, however, still lurked in the background of both men’s minds and the first mention of a possible revival can be found in the Ayckbourn Archive during September 1980 when Alan made the suggestion of reviving the musical – albeit with considerable alterations.</p>

<p><em>“The fact is that Jeeves must still be the hottest dormant property around…. I think that we must meet and talk about an imminent revival staged by us – in the theatre here [Scarborough] in a small scale way – with a hand-picked cast. Of course this would mean extreme work on the book by me, a revision of the score to suit a smallish ensemble and a lot of clever ingenuity from me as director… Does this interest you?” (Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<p>It’s fascinating to think that 16 years before it was written, Alan has already distilled the&nbsp;elements which would define <em>By Jeeves</em>: small scale using a small ensemble with a new book and score relying on directorial ingenuity than scale and ambitious technicality.</p>

<p>However, Alan was also pragmatic about the suggested re-writes and noted: “If we can’t get it right, we will bury it alongside Anne Brontë.”; a reference to the fact that Alan Ayckbourn lives a couple of minute’s walk from the burial place of the famed Brontë sister in Scarborough.</p>

<p>For an unrecorded reason, something drastically altered in Alan’s perception of the musical though, for just a month later, he wrote back noting “Re: <em>Jeeves</em>. I’ve had a total rethink on this. I don’t think I will do it. The dead are best buried.”</p>

<p>But this is not to say that all interest in the musical had died with Alan’s loss of interest. There was a small but steady flow of enquiries from companies interested in reviving the play throughout the ‘80s and early ‘90s and, during 1980, Lloyd Webber noting there was interest in adapting <em>Jeeves</em> for television. All these enquiries were met with the consistent response the creators had hopes off revisiting the musical, hence it wasn’t available for production.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/1981-jeeves-cover-w315.jpg" alt="" width="315" height="400" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>The programme cover for Dulwich College’s 1981 production – and final ever outing of – of Jeeves.<br>(© Dulwich College)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>There is an interesting caveat here though – and what is, essentially, the endnote to the original <em>Jeeves</em> musical. Between 1894 and 1900, P.G. Wodehouse was a pupil of Dulwich College and to celebrate the centenary in 1981 of the author’s birth (and the opening of a new theatre at the college), permission was doggedly sought to stage <em>Jeeves</em>. Because of the Wodehouse connection, it was agreed to let Dulwich College produce the show and Lloyd Webber loaned the school various copies of the script and music with the proviso it be re-shaped into a format which would work for Dulwich College Junior Dramatic Society. This then was a unique production of <em>Jeeves</em>: the first and last performance of <em>Jeeves</em> following its West End run and the only amateur production of the play ever staged. This unique arrangement of the musical ran from 9 – 13 December 1981 and was considered a great success by the college. Having agreed to the production though, both Alan and Lloyd Webber had misgivings with the former writing: “I think we should pray that the show attracts little attention.”</p>

<p>The first serious attempt to re-stage the play came in 1982, when Lloyd Webber suggested to Alan they consider staging it at the Sydmonton Festival during 1984. This is an annual festival founded by Lloyd Webber in 1975 which takes place in the grounds of Lloyd Webber’s home, Sydmonton Court, Hampshire, and is where many of Lloyd Webber’s musicals have had their first public performance (although not <em>By Jeeves</em> – despite what you may read on some sites!).</p>

<p>Alan’s PA – now wife – Heather Stoney responded to Lloyd Webber that not only was Alan keen on this idea, but he wanted to transfer it to his home theatre in Scarborough afterwards; another essential element in the creation of <em>By Jeeves</em>.</p>

<p><em>“Alan would be interested in pencilling in a month in 1984 to re-work Jeeves for Sydmonton… Ideally, he would like first to work on the show for there and then immediately transfer it to Scarborough for a month, say to really get to grips with it as an entire production.” (Heather Stoney)</em></p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/1996-by-jeeves-sydmonton-cover-w350.jpg" alt="" width="350" height="400" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>An example of a programme for Sydmonton Music Festival<br>(© Really Useful Company)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Both Lloyd Webber and Alan are on record several times confirming this plan for 1984, but – as would repeatedly happen over the years – it did not come together. This is largely because of both men’s schedules – particularly Alan who was not only writing and directing premieres and revivals of his work in both Scarborough and London, but as Artistic Director of the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round, was also responsible for the day-to-day running of the popular regional theatre.</p>

<p><em>“I quite agree the time is about ripe for us to disinter the bones and lay the ghost… We aren’t going to have it ready for Sydmonton this year [1984], are we?… I want to overhaul the script totally and, of course, tighten it completely. There are musical things you want to do. It’s quite a task… I am extremely keen to work on it and with you again.” (Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<p>So although both men were committed to the idea of resurrecting <em>Jeeves</em> in some new form, practically it was proving extremely difficult. From 1986 to 1988, Alan took a sabbatical from Scarborough as a Company Director for the National Theatre and, no sooner had he returned to Scarborough, then plans began to come together to convert the town’s former Odeon Cinema into the first purpose-built home for the Stephen Joseph Theatre company. This £5.2m project, however, would eventually open the door for the revamped musical.</p>

<p>In correspondence from 1992, Alan noted “Andrew and I mutter occasionally about mounting a concert version [of <em>Jeeves</em>] in 1995 to ‘celebrate’ the 20th anniversary of the production but, of course, that’s a long way off.” Now whilst this undoubtedly refers to Sydmonton, Alan would have been aware that the original plan was for the new Stephen Joseph Theatre to have opened by or open during 1995. Surely no coincidence? Open the new musical in Sydmonton and then transfer it to the new theatre in Scarborough.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/1995-by-jeeves-lyrics-c2a9-haydonning-ltd-w400.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="274" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Alan Ayckbourn’s – largely indecipherable – hand-written lyrics for the first verse of the song By Jeeves!<br>(© Haydonning Ltd)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Coincidentally, Lloyd Webber was also thinking about the revival too and wrote to Alan in April 1993 with a new proposal.</p>

<p><em>“I would love to get together with you anyway just to talk about the possibility of having a look at Jeeves again since the 20th anniversary of the Sydmonton Festival falls next year and coincides with the similar anniversary of our show. I think it would be quite possible to overhaul it with a few new songs and a concert performance of it.”</em> <em>(Andrew Lloyd Webber)</em></p>

<p>He also had an intriguing plan up his sleeves – which sadly never came to fruition. At the time, the actors Stephen Fry and Hugh Laurie were enjoying enormous success as the leads of the television show <em>Jeeves &amp; Wooster</em>. Although the BAFTA Award series would end during 1993, Lloyd Webber intended to ask them to reprise their roles on stage.</p>

<p><em>“The thing is, I think we could get Hugh and Stephen to do it for us as I know them both. We just need a little planning.”</em> <em>(Andrew Lloyd Webber)</em></p>

<p>Alan was certainly up for this, despite now being deep in the midst of the Odeon conversion and about to launch a massive fund-raising appeal. However, in correspondence with Lloyd Webber, Alan confirmed he had given serious thought to the idea, drawing on his extensive subsequent experiences with musicals; bear in mind that in 1975, Alan had never written any lyrics before being asked to write <em>Jeeves</em>, but by the ‘90s, he had four full-length musicals, 10 revues and hundreds of songs under his belt as well as having directed a score of musical productions.</p>

<p><em>“I think from my side, the number one priority will be to tighten the book. So far as I can remember, it’s actually not a bad story-line (though you’d never have guessed it at the time). All it needs is sharpening, as they say. I think (I hope) I am a slightly better dramatist than I was then. I am certainly a rather better lyricist than the tyro who was thrust upon you originally… Sydmonton would be a good place to look at it.”</em> <em>(Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<div><figure><figcaption>The original By Jeeves company at the Stephen Joseph Theatre during 1996.<br>(© The Scarborough News)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>Alas, time again proved to be the greatest enemy and, during early 1994, Alan found himself firefighting on two fronts. First the cost of the Odeon project was increasing substantially and the projected opening date put back and, secondly, cuts in Arts Council funding to the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round meant Alan was having to hastily rethink his entire programme for the year as well as having to write an unplanned new play to help boost takings at the theatre. Naturally, the plan for <em>Jeeves</em> was delayed again – although Alan by this stage had also realised this was not going to be either a quick or simple job.</p>

<p><em>“It should have been this year but I couldn’t fit it in. Anyway, having done some preliminary work on the script, I realise the whole thing needs re-drafting. Andrew has used most of the songs in other shows – time needs to be spent on it.”</em> <em>(Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<p>Alan’s experiences with musicals subsequent to <em>Jeeves</em> had also crucially altered his perception of what a musical should be. He was of the firm belief that songs needed to be integral and further the plot rather than just being for decorative purposes. Something he would later say was essential to re-writing <em>Jeeves</em>.&nbsp;</p>

<p><em>“Before, we had ‘A Madrigal’, which was lovely but did nothing for the story. Now, we have a number called ‘It’s A Pig’, which moves the plot on an entire cycle, yet the song maintains a structural integrity and you can listen to it outside the visual action.”</em> <em>(Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<p>With both Alan and Lloyd Webber keenly aware the scale of the show needed to be vastly reduced, Alan suggested the new book should work on the principle of Bertie Wooster’s adventure literally being thrown together from props which might be found in the village hall where his ‘aborted’ banjo concert is being staged.</p>

<p>Alan began looking at the original book to see if anything was salvageable. Having decided to remove Wodehouse’s villain Roderick Spode from the piece, Alan quickly realised it caused a domino effect on the musical making it untenable to revise. In an interview during 1996, he bluntly offered: “I picked up the original book I wrote and threw it away!” Alan decided the best idea was to write a new book largely from scratch which could incorporate several existing songs from <em>Jeeves</em> alongside a number of new songs. As a result, the mistaken-identity plot was created, which drives the final musical but which is not based on a single Wodehouse work, instead containing elements from various Wodehouse’s novels alongside an original Ayckbourn-created antagonist.&nbsp;</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Steven Pacey as Bertie &amp; Malcom Sinclair as Jeeves in the world premiere of By Jeeves at the Stephen Joseph Theatre during 1996.<br>(© Tony Bartholomew)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>By July 1994, Alan had completed a draft of the script he was satisfied with, which incorporated six songs from the original <em>Jeeves</em> and five new songs. It featured a cast of 10, was infinitely simpler to stage and, in his own words, was “virtually a new book.” Satisfied with this, he sent the new script to Lloyd Webber along with the new title, <em>By Jeeves</em>.</p>

<p><em>“Alan sent me a totally new play except for the characters! There was a new story and totally new script… It bears little resemblance to the original. Alan and I knew what interest there would be if we did it again and we don’t want to make the same mistakes again. It’s not Sunset Boulevard and it is not intended to be. It’s one of the most simple of musicals, with a small cast of 10 and a small orchestra of four. Most of the songs are newly composed, three have been kept.”</em> <em>(Andrew Lloyd Webber)</em></p>

<p>But what to do with the new work? Rather than open the piece at Sydmonton, it was decided to do workshop it for ten days during September 1995 with the intention that, if successful, it would open the new Stephen Joseph Theatre which had now been delayed until April 1996.</p>

<p><em>“He [Alan] said: ‘I think we should do it again with the opening of my new theatre.’ “Are you sure?’ I said. If it was going to be the opening of his theatre, then all of the major critics would come and see it, and I guess I was a bit frightened about that. But there was an element of both of us thinking it would be wonderful to get it right. We knew that buried in there somewhere was a piece that really was very sound, and it hadn’t been such a bad idea to make a musical out of the P.G. Wodehouse books.”</em> <em>(Andrew Lloyd Webber)</em></p>

<p>A cast for the workshop was arranged – amongst them Janie Dee – and after putting it through its paces, <em>By Jeeves</em> was unveiled to what Alan intended to be a small, invited audience. He got an invited audience, if not a small one…</p>

<p><em>“Andrew – with his usual discretion! – invited about a hundred people [to the first workshop] and when the cast heard that, they said, ‘I want to ask my mum.’ Those occasions are full of traps, but this one went very well and the piece needed very little alteration before it was to open the season in Scarborough.” (Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<p>The success of the workshop meant the green light was given for it to open the Stephen Joseph Theatre, Scarborough, during April 1996 with Alan directing. This, however, came as a surprise to virtually no-one in theatre circles!&nbsp; Whilst nothing had been officially announced, it was an open secret (that or a very badly kept one) that Alan hoped to open the theatre with <em>By Jeeves</em>. The local newspaper, the Scarborough Evening News, reporting as early as July 1995 a rumour the musical <em>Jeeves</em> would open the new venue; although the story does not mention it would be an entirely new musical.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2020/1996-01-31-scarborough-evening-news-by-jeeves-w400.jpg" alt="" width="400" height="275" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>My Scarborough Evening News front page breaking the news By Jeeves would open the Stephen Joseph Theatre.<br>(© The Scarborough News)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>The actual news that a revised version of <em>Jeeves</em> would open the theatre was unofficially announced on 31 January 1996, when it was a breaking front-page story on the Scarborough Evening News – written by an enthusiastic young arts reporter, yours truly! This news, however, had not officially been announced by the Stephen Joseph Theatre and, apparently, contracts has not been finalised. However, when it became apparent a rival regional newspaper was also looking to break the story, the Scarborough Evening News – with the complicit blessing of the SJT – broke the story&nbsp; and provided me with one of my proudest moments a a journalist!</p>

<p>Naturally, this led to many questions about why a flop musical was being revived – which, of course, wasn’t really happening – to open the theatre. Alan’s response was a surprise to many.</p>

<p><em>“So why are we doing it again? Well for fun, really. Sorry about that. Can’t think of a deeper reason, offhand.”</em> <em>(Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<p>By March, Alan and Lloyd Webber confirmed the actual facts: it was essentially an entirely new musical called <em>By Jeeves</em>, contained only three of the original <em>Jeeves</em> songs and would be small-scale with a cast of 10 rather than 21. In truth, six songs were originally recognisable from the original <em>Jeeves</em>, but half of them had gone through substantial alterations.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption><em>Steven Pacey as Bertie &amp; Nicholas Haverson as Bingo in the world</em> <em>premiere of By Jeeves at the Stephen Joseph Theatre during 1996.</em><br><em>(© Tony Bartholomew)</em></figcaption></figure></div>

<p><em>“I rewrote it entirely and in a much smaller way. The cast has shrunk by over fifty percent. Sets all but disappeared. It’s very simple now. We’ve gone back to what I think what we originally wanted. Before, it had run away from us. It’s now a celebration of theatre.”</em> <em>(Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<p>The Stephen Joseph Theatre opened on 24 April 1996 with <em>By Jeeves</em> performed in The Round (as of writing, the only time the musical has been performed professionally in-the-round). After a week of previews and special launch events, the theatre officially opened on 1 May 1996 with the ‘world premiere’ of <em>By Jeeves</em>. Prestn on the night was Lloyd Webber in what would turn out to be a very memorable night for him for other reasons too.</p>

<p><em>“The first night was very emotional for me, because Madeleine, my wife, was in hospital, giving birth to Isabelle, so I took with me as my mascot my first wife, who remains a great friend of mine. And of course she was the poor girl who had to live through all the traumas and disasters of the original Jeeves. We sat together, and both of us were in tears when some of the songs came on, and our daughter by that marriage, who wasn’t born when it was written, looked after both of us. Thank God it seemed to have worked this time.”</em> <em>(Andrew Lloyd Webber)</em></p>

<p>All waited with bated breath to see how the new musical would be received. Alan had the misfortune the first review he read was quite disparaging and had visions of a repeat of 1975. His fears were unfounded though and it was not a reflection of what was to follow, the majority of the critics loved the show and, crucially, it was a tremendous hit with audiences. It would go onto a sell-out run at the theatre and, notably, had a live original cast recording CD which was recorded on site and rush-released for sale as a Scarborough ‘exclusive’ during the run, making it one of the rarer Ayckbourn / Lloyd Webber collectibles.</p>

<p>Upon the run in Scarborough finishing, the production immediately transferred to the West End during summer 1996 where it ran for more than seven months before embarking on a UK tour. During the same year, Alan also directed it in North America, adapted and directed it for BBC Radio and a second cast album was recorded for the West End production.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Choreographer Sheila Carter, Andrew Lloyd Webber &amp; Alan Ayckbourn strumming the frying pans in celebration of By Jeeves success during 1996.<br>(© To be confirmed)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>As to why <em>By Jeeves</em> worked and was such a success, Alan had very strong thoughts about how the process was completely different to the one which had blighted the original production of <em>Jeeves</em> during 1975.&nbsp;</p>

<p><em>“By Jeeves was done in hindsight with a lot more experience than the first one [Jeeves]. More importantly, I had control over it. It was still a risk though, but we had control over it and brought a scale and elements to it that I could control. The best thing Andrew [Lloyd Webber] did was stand well back once his side of the piece was in. He came in to see the band and dealt with that side which is, of course, exclusively his own. But he was a very easy collaborator, which is very helpful. Although his reputation is quite fiery, he is very astute and knows when to intervene.”</em> <em>(Alan Ayckbourn)</em></p>

<p>The original <em>Jeeves</em> may have been an experience to forget, but <em>By Jeeves</em> proved to be something altogether different. A successful musical experience that Alan would always remember fondly.</p>
