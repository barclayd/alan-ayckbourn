<p>Continuing our look into some of this Archivist’s favourite items in the Ayckbourn Archive with Alan Ayckbourn’s second play <em><a href="/plays/love-after-all">Love After All</a></em> (1959).</p>

<p>The Ayckbourn Archive actually holds very little regarding <em>Love After All</em> and that’s perhaps not a surprise. 1959 marked Alan’s first year as a professional playwright, it would be surprising if he had the foresight to have started saving material for posterity so early in his fledgling career. Let’s not forget, Alan at the time considered himself first and foremost an actor and that appeared to be the trajectory upon which he was heading. The playwriting was a nice sideline that kept him in LPs and home comforts for his family!</p>

<p>The Ayckbourn Archive holds no more than two dozen contemporary articles and reviews for the only two productions of the play (it was produced separately by <a href="http://thesjt.alanayckbourn.net/page1/styled-45/">Studio Theatre Ltd</a> in Scarborough in both 1959 and 1960), a couple of programmes, a selection of publicity photographs and a scan of the original manuscript.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/1959-love-after-all-c2a9-haydinning-ltd-02.jpg" alt="" width="650" height="799" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>The cover of the sole surviving manuscript of Love After All held in the Lord Chamberlain’s Collection at the British Library (© Haydonning Ltd)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>The latter is my favourite item by far and away for <em>Love After All</em> and one of my all-time favourite items in the entire Archive. Why, you might well ask? It’s not as though it’s even the original item. Well, let’s head back in time.</p>

<p><em>Love After All</em> was written in a very brief window during the autumn of 1959 as a result of the success of Alan’s first play, <em><a href="/plays/the-square-cat">The Square Cat</a></em>. Having done stellar business, <a href="http://biography.alanayckbourn.net/styled-36/styled-37/">Stephen Joseph</a> – the Artistic Director of the <a href="http://thesjt.alanayckbourn.net/page1/styled/">Library Theatre</a>, Scarborough – asked his protege to knock out a second play for the forthcoming winter season. Alan agreed and, swiping inspiration from the plot of <em>The Barber of Seville</em>, wrote <em>Love After All</em>.</p>

<p>The Edwardian-set farce was another success and it was decided it would be revived for the following summer season. However, the original director Clifford Williams – who would go on to a long and successful international directing career and was hugely influential at the Royal Shakespeare Company – was unavailable during the summer of 1960 and a replacement, Julian Herington, was appointed. He disliked aspects of the original play and asked Alan to rewrite it for a contemporary setting with some notable changes. Alan didn’t like the new version, Stephen Joseph didn’t like the fact that Herington’s two productions consumed the summer season’s entire production budget and Herington never worked for Studio Theatre Ltd again. The play was also never performed again.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/1959-love-after-all-c2a9-haydinning-ltd-03-1.jpg" alt="" width="570" height="801" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>A page from Love After All highlighting one of the more interesting scenes (© Haydonning Ltd)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>By the time Alan started achieving major success – starting with <em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></em> in 1967 and consistently throughout the 1970s from <em><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></em> forward – there was an obvious interest in this playwright, where he had come from and what he had written previously. Alan was of a view that his early writing was just that, early writing showing nothing but a young man learning his craft. He had no desire for the early works to be dug out and performed, so he began batting the question away when asked about these plays noting he had either destroyed them all or was attempting to destroy them all.</p>

<p>Now it’s hard to know whether this was true – not many copies of the plays were produced in the first place and there’s natural wastage and loss. Original copies of <em>The Square Cat</em> (1959) are held in four Archive collections, <em><a href="/plays/the-sparrow">The Sparrow</a></em> (1967) in just one. It seems likely Alan did his best to ‘lose’ what manuscripts he came across. And with <em>Love After All</em>, he was very successful. When I took on the role of Archivist in 2005, it was accepted that <em>Love After All</em> – in both its versions – had been completely lost. It’s obvious in Paul Allen’s Ayckbourn biography, <em><a href="http://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/product/0413771369/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&amp;camp=1634&amp;creative=19450&amp;creativeASIN=0413771369&amp;linkCode=as2&amp;tag=alanaycksoffi-21&amp;linkId=KLFIM63TZIKX35MM">Grinning At The Edge</a></em>, that he has not found the play nor is there any indication of anyone else accessing it.</p>

<div><figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2019/1959-love-after-all-c2a9-haydinning-ltd-01.jpg" alt="" width="793" height="633" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>The sole surviving Love After All page held in the Ayckbourn Archive complete with Steven Ayckbourn’s drawing (© Haydonning Ltd)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>My early exploration of the Ayckbourn Archive turned up just a single sheet of the <em>Love After All</em> manuscript held in Archive – preserved not because it was a coveted piece of a lost manuscript but because his first son, Steven, had drawn a picture of a car with two people on it (possibly the earliest surviving portrait of Alan…). Obviously this was a highly prized manuscript if Alan was letting his sons use it for drawing paper!</p>

<p>And then the strangest thing occurred. In 2007, a long-thought lost Noël Coward play – <em>The Better Half</em> – was found by two Welsh researchers in the Lord Chamberlain’s Collection at the British Library (Prior to 1968, every play in the UK had to be submitted for approval by the Lord Chamberlain’s Office). Which led me to think, <em>Love After All</em> was written in 1959 and it would have had to be approved by the Lord Chamberlain’s Office – so presumably it was hidden somewhere in the British Library….</p>

<p>Sure enough, with the assistance of the British Library, I ventured down to London in 2007 and discovered, within the Lord Chamberlain’s Collection, there was a pristine copy of <em>Love After All</em>, apparently never once accessed since being stored. As both Archivist and geek, this is equivalent of the moment Indiana Jones discovered the Ark of the Covenant in the Well of Souls in <em>Raiders of the Lost Ark</em>. Admittedly, I had to deal with fewer snakes although the bag check system at the British Library can be quite intimidating….</p>

<p>It’s one of the high points in my career as an Archivist, finding <em>Love After All</em> and restoring the complete Ayckbourn play canon for the first time since, in all probability, the ’60s. Hence why it’s one of my favourite things, even in a scanned form.</p>

<p>There is an enduring mystery, I suppose – aside from what the second version of <em>Love After All</em> was like as, sadly, that has never been recovered. That mystery is why no-one had never previously looked to the British Library for the play. But one could ask that about the Coward play or any of the discoveries which turn up in apparently obvious places. I always presume that with Alan having said the play was lost, people took him on his word and, to be fair, Alan is a person perpetually looking forward to his next play or production and never had the inclination nor desire to seek out the play. But there were other, often far more qualified, researchers than myself looking into Alan’s work who missed this opportunity.</p>

<p>But that’s by-the-by. What we have is a manuscript of, admittedly, a fairly ropey play which will forever be something I’m inordinately fond of! It generated a fair amount of publicity too including coverage in The Times – which was slightly strange having previously been a journalist and more used to writing rather than being the news! It’s a script that is never going to ever strike anyone as a founding triumph of a playwright who would become one of the most successful British writers of the 20th century. But it’s a little bit of history which I’m proud to have contributed towards restoring.</p>
