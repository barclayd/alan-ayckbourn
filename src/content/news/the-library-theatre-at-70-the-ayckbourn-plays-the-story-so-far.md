<p>Here on the Ayckbourn Blog, we’re celebrating the 70th anniversary of <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> founding the UK’s first professional theatre in the round company in Scarborough in July 1955.</p>

<p>Alan Ayckbourn was a vital and significant presence at <strong><a href="http://www.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> and these articles celebrate the plays he wrote for the company between 1959 and 1976. They offer an in-depth look at the creation of and background to some of the playwright’s most famous – and most obscure – works.</p>

<p>In the previous article, we looked at his ninth play – <em><strong><a href="/plays/how-the-other-half-loves">How The Other Half Loves</a></strong></em> (1969), which was followed up by one of his least known plays of the 1970s, <em>The Story So Far…</em> (1970) – better known today as <em><strong><a href="/plays/family-circles">Family Circles</a></strong></em>. The majority of photos accompanying this article have never been published before and have only recently been rediscovered in archive.</p>

<p><strong>The Story So Far… </strong>(1970) by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p><em>The Story So Far…</em> or <em>Family Circles</em>&nbsp;as it was retitled is a neglected part of the Alan Ayckbourn play canon. First produced in 1970, it has never made a significant impact and for an Ayckbourn play of this period it is little known and infrequently produced.</p>

<p>As a result, for this article I’m going to not only cover how it was created, but also how close it came to success with the subsequent abortive attempts to take it into the West End. Given the string of successes which follow it, it’s perhaps instructive to explore how Alan’s path to West End domination during the mid 1970s was not quite as smooth as might be thought.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>A rarely seen image of Alan Ayckbourn circa 1970 (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><em>The Story So Far…</em> is also an interesting play standing as it does between the more plot-device driven <strong><em><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></em></strong>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em>&nbsp;and the emergence of Alan’s tragi-comic voice in <strong><em><a href="/plays/time-and-time-again">Time And Time Again</a></em></strong>. Alan himself has noted&nbsp;<em>The Story So Far…&nbsp;</em>acts as a bridge between these plays, taking the technical challenges of the previous plays to an extreme whilst also introducing some of the character and darker elements that typify his first character-driven work,&nbsp;<em>Time And Time Again</em>.</p>

<p>The summer of 1969 saw Alan Ayckbourn premiere and direct one of his most enduring successes,&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em>&nbsp;at&nbsp;<strong><a href="https://www.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-187/page-14/">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong>, Scarborough. The production was a sell-out success and it was becoming increasingly apparent that Alan was the heir apparent to the late <strong><a href="https://www.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-4/">Stephen Joseph</a></strong> who had founded the company in 1955. The company was already benefiting from the substantial royalties from <em>Relatively Speaking</em> in the West End and in provincial theatres and, in 1970, this would substantially increase with <em>How The Other Half Loves</em>‘ West End success.</p>

<p>At the time, Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre was still only operating on a seasonal basis, generally from June to September, and appointing its Director of Productions on an annual basis. Alan had taken on the role for the first time in 1969 and agreed to again for 1970. As part of the deal, he was commissioned to write a new play for the summer season.</p>

<p>Alan generally gave little insight as to the content of his plays until they had been written, although he would normally give a title for advertising purposes by the spring. Unusually for Alan, he announced his plans for the 1970 summer season months in advance when – during December 1969 – he told the board of Scarborough Theatre Trust that his next work would be… a musical.</p>

<p>Sadly, we know nothing else about this idea and the playwright has no recollection of what he intended. He must have had a solid idea to mention it to the board though and it’s fascinating to wonder what it might have been given Alan had shown no inclination to approach musicals prior to this. His first actual foray into the genre would be his disastrous collaboration with Andrew Lloyd Webber on <em><strong><a href="/plays/jeeves">Jeeves</a></strong></em> in 1975.</p>

<p>There is no more mention of his new work for the summer until 2 April 1970 when Alan revealed the title of his new play as <em>The Story So Far…</em>. Archivally, it’s quite interesting as there is no other Ayckbourn play of the period when the naming of a play can be so accurately judged. Alan’s agent&nbsp;<strong><a href="/life/influences/ramsay-margaret">Margaret Ramsay</a></strong>&nbsp;(better known as Peggy) sent a contract to <strong><a href="https://www.theatre-in-the-round.com/page-187/page-7/styled-10/">Ken Boden</a></strong>, manager of Theatre in teh Round at the Library Theatre, regarding Alan’s new play, cited in the covering letter as ‘Alan Ayckbourn – untitled play’. The accompanying contract, again dated 2 April, had the title&nbsp;<em>The Story So Far…</em>&nbsp;showing an obviously last minute decision regarding the play title.</p>

<p>Posters advertising&nbsp;<em>The Story So Far…</em>&nbsp;were distributed during May and carried no description of the play save: “The latest new comedy from the author of&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>&nbsp;and last year’s very successful&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em>.” The fact that Alan was still writing plays to the latest possible deadline was even acknowledged and typified by a press release from Ken Boden which declared that: “Finished only three days before rehearsals were due to start, a team of typists worked day and night to have the scripts ready in time.” Advance publicity of this nature was common for the world premiere of Alan’s plays until 1985.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>An advertising board for the world premiere of The Story So Far… (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>Amusingly – given the play had not been yet written and its eventual overly complicated structure – the original press release also states the play is “far more straightforward” than&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em>. It also notes the play centres around Edward and Emma Gray – the surname of the parents not being included in the original programme – and whose names were inspired by the names of the parents of his then wife Christine Roland.</p>

<p>Alan began writing the play at his home in Leeds during the latter part of the week of 27 July 1969 and finished writing it on 4 August. The following day he travelled to London to attend the West End premiere of <em>How The Other Half Loves</em> at the Lyric Theatre, travelling up to Scarborough the next day for rehearsals starting on 7 August. A somewhat busy few days!</p>

<p>The writing of the play was not without incident.&nbsp;Alan, at the time, tended to write during the night as he was busy rehearsing during the day. After finishing writing one night, he went to bed inadvertently leaving the manuscript pages on the top of the cooker. When his wife Christine woke up later, she didn’t notice the pages, switched the cooker on and ignited the hob, setting the pages on fire and completely destroying the previous night’s work! </p>

<p>The play, directed by Alan, was in rehearsal for two weeks – something of a luxury at the time – before opening on 20 August at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre. It was the final play to be introduced into rep that season and ran from 20 – 26 August and 3 – 12 September. Alan would direct three of the season’s four productions.</p>

<p>For the programme, Alan wrote a programme note which no doubt had audiences rushing to read at the interval given what was being presented to them!</p>

<p>“I suppose the question this play poses is one that many married partners might have asked themselves at some stage or other – either idly or, in some cases, in deadly seriousness. What would have happened had I married someone else? What would have happened to me as a person? Would have I been very different had I married, say, a booming extrovert rather than the nervous hesitant creature with whom I share my breakfast table? And what might have happened to them? Are they as they are, solely as a result of being exposed to the hard core radiation of my own personality? Intrigued, I explored the possibility. Six individuals are all, at one time or another during this play, married or about to be married to each other. Who’s best suited to who? You can take your choice. These six, at any rate, finally find their own happy solution. But that’s only half the story so far….”</p>

<p>Were you to take the first paragraph out of context, it could practically be describing either of Alan’s plays concerning choice such as&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/sisterly-feelings">Sisterly Feelings</a></em></strong>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<strong><em><a href="/plays/intimate-exchanges">Intimate Exchanges</a></em></strong>. Both plays incorporate actual choices as motivating factors demonstrating what happens when different choices are made by or for the characters (in&nbsp;<em>Sisterly Feelings</em>, all choices lead to the same destination; in&nbsp;<em>Intimate Exchanges</em>, each choice moves the characters’ lives in very different directions) and the decision-making is a vital aspect of these plays’ structures; whereas in&nbsp;<em>Family Circles</em>, it is the consequences of choice which are explored.</p>

<p>Alan has spoken very little about the play and never about its influences. As a result, everything is supposition and given that Alan does not consider himself an autobiographical writer, it is dangerous to read too much into the play. However, it is worth noting that by this time, his marriage to Christine Roland was effectively over and his relationship with his second wife, Heather Stoney, was in its formative stages.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Eve Shickle and Robert Peck as Emma and Edward (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>The production featured a notable cast which included Bob Peck (practically at the start of his professional stage acting career and appearing in his first Ayckbourn play), Stephanie Turner, Elisabeth Sladen, Piers Rogers, Joe Dunlop, Ronald Herdman, Heather Stoney and Eve Shickle. A number of the company had carried over from the previous year and several of the actors would work with Alan over the coming years.</p>

<p>The production was a tremendous success and Ken Boden wrote to Peggy saying it had “packed houses” and made £1032.0.6d at the box office. Alan though was not entirely happy with the play and began revising it, noting some years later: “I think audiences were a bit mystified by the last scene as seen in its premiere production in Scarborough.” </p>

<p><em>The Story So Far…</em> is now recognised as the rarest of Alan’s earliest plays in manuscript form with only one extant copy of the play known to exist, which was discovered in 2020 by the playwrights archivist, Simon Murgatroyd. This is significant as <em>Family Circles</em> has been published and it was long thought this version generally reflected the original play. It doesn’t. There are substantial differences between <em>The Story So Far…</em> and <em>Family Circles</em>. This makes the single surviving copy of the original production historically significant.</p>

<p>Thoughts now turned to transferring the play to London, despite Alan’s obvious misgivings about the play and the need to refine it. Peggy had already raised the presumption of interest from London producers on the back of the success of&nbsp;<em>Relatively Speaking</em> and the early interest shown in&nbsp;<em>How The Other Half Loves</em>, which had recently opened in London during July 1970. </p>

<p>Peter Bridge had produced these two plays but in correspondence, Peggy was of the mind that he was not automatically the right choice for taking&nbsp;<em>Family Circles</em>&nbsp;into London. “Alan and I are going to tell Peter Bridge that he cannot have first refusal on this new play. He is welcome to see it and make an offer for it, but we want other West End managers to have an opportunity of seeing it and to make an offer as well.”</p>

<p>Peggy hoped, ironically it would turn out, offering the play to other producers would result in a short turn-over between the Scarborough and London productions. There was still obviously some good will towards Peter Bridge as he initially did seem set to produce the play; unfortunately, Bridge was beset both with financial problems (which had seen Alan forego his final week’s royalties from the London production of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>) and ill-health. As a result of this, Alan was advised by Peggy’s lawyer, Laurence Harbottle, that the play might be better co-produced with Eddie Kulukundis, who had produced the American production of <em>How The Other Half Loves</em>.</p>

<p>It was suggested Kulukundis take on the role of executive producer with Peter Bridge receiving his usual producer billing. According to Paul Allen, author of Alan’s biography&nbsp;<em>Grinning At The Edge</em>, Bridge ‘angrily refused the offer’ and was eased out of the production with Kulukundis taking on full responsibilities as producer. He would set about organising a short pre-West End tour before a transfer to London, whilst Bridge would never again produce an Ayckbourn play in the West End despite several attempts to assert his ‘right’ to do so.</p>

<p>Alan, meanwhile, had revised the three-act play and re-titled it&nbsp;<em>Me Times Me Times Me</em>, although it seems staging the play was far from easy as less than six weeks from its planned opening, Peggy told Alan that Kulukundis was having difficulty casting it – although she also made clear he intended the play to have an “immediate transfer” to London. In August 1971, an announcement of the forthcoming production was carried in The Stage newspaper.</p>

<p>“Robin Midgley is to direct&nbsp;<em>Me Times Me Times Me</em>&nbsp;a new plays [sic] presented by be presented [sic] at Leicester Phoenix on August 25, presented by Eddie Kulukundis for Knightsbridge Theatrical Productions Ltd, Anna Cropper, Cherith Mellor and Bridget Turner play three sisters, with Peter Blythe, John Normington and David Wood as their husbands and Ambrosine Philpotts and John Robinson as their parents. After four weeks in Leicester the production will be seen at the Edinburgh Lyceum and in the West End in October. The designer is Adrian Vaux.”</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Piers Rogers and Heather Stoney as James and Polly (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p><em>Me Times Me Times Me</em>&nbsp;opened on 25 August 1971 and was scheduled to run until 18 September at Leicester’s Phoenix Theatre. Even as it opened there was a significant change as the actress Anna Cropper had dropped out of the play for health reasons (an insert in the programme bluntly states: “Due to the indisposition of Anna Cropper…”) and Heather Stoney, who had performed the part of Polly in Scarborough, was brought in as a last minute replacement.</p>

<p>The play then toured to Edinburgh for a week, but with additional changes. Uniquely for any Ayckbourn play, Alan was extensively revising and rewriting it whilst in production. It was now entitled<em>&nbsp;Me Times Me</em>&nbsp;and was no longer in three acts, but in two acts (a form which it kept through all subsequent revisions). Heather Stoney was credited in both flyers and the programme as playing the role of Polly. According to the original press reports, the play was scheduled to transfer from Edinburgh directly to London.</p>

<p>Instead, the play opened in Oxford. On 23 September, various regional newspapers reported&nbsp;<em>Me Times Me</em>&nbsp;would be visiting the New Theatre, Oxford, for a week commencing 27 September to 2 October. The majority of articles noted the play was still destined to open in the West End in October. Come October, the play transferred – again – not to London but to the Theatre Royal, Bath, for the week 18 to 23 October. If a surviving review is anything to go by, this is also the point where the play’s future must have been in serious doubt as it noted it had “the most sparsely attended first-night of the current season.”</p>

<p>The majority of reports and reviews of the Bath production still mention the play will transfer to London, but neither dates nor a venue are given. Unsurprisingly by this point, the play did not transfer to London, nor did the tour continue with the production ending in Bath on 23 October 1971.</p>

<p>While Eddie Kulukundis would later attribute the play’s failure primarily to the lack of star names in the cast, it appears the matter was not so clear cut. In correspondence with the play’s director, Robin Midgley, after the event, Peggy quite clearly felt it had a few teething problems.</p>

<p>“I personally saw the show in Bath, and I thought it much much inferior to Leicester – everyone was straining to be funny, and it had become farce rather than comedy. I came home deeply depressed…. [Michael] Codron saw that performance, and thought the production and everything to do with it – including the poor actors – a non-starter for the West End. A lot of the managers saw it too, and didn’t offer theatres – it’s the luck of the game.”</p>

<p>It also transpired that despite the play’s advertised intent of an immediate London transfer, there had been no prior deal with any London theatre and that Kulukundis hoped an offer would be made by a London theatre management or a theatre would become available during the run of the try-out tour; although this was not unusual at the time such was the demand for venues in the West End.</p>

<p>This was not the end of the play’s story though. Present in the Bath audience -as Peggy had intimated – was the producer Michael Codron who had begun the process of bringing Alan’s latest play,&nbsp;<em>Time And Time Again</em> (1971) to the London stage. He apparently notified Alan and Peggy that should the&nbsp;<em>Me Times Me</em>&nbsp;tour not go into the West End, then he would like the opportunity to try his hand at a transfer.</p>

<div>
<figure><figcaption><sub>Ronald Herdman and Elisabeth Sladen as  David and Jenny (© Haydonning Ltd)</sub></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<p>This optioning of the play for a new tour must have taken place relatively quickly after the original tour ended as Alan Ayckbourn reported to the Scarborough Theatre Trust board in February 1972 that rehearsals were under way for&nbsp;<em>Me Times Me</em>&nbsp;with Celia Johnson and Roland Culver contracted for a production “to be presented in the West End of London next April.”</p>

<p>Codron decided to mount an entirely new production and Alan extensively revised the play again – a process which Peggy believed had begun almost as soon as the play’s first tour ended in Bath. Basil Coleman came in to direct with a predominantly new cast of Celia Johnson – who had been so successful in the original West End production of <em>Relatively Speaking</em>, Roland Culver, Bridget Armstrong, Ann Penfold, Pamela Fairbrother (who would be replaced by Cherith Mellor for the run), John Nettleton, Peter Blythe and David Firth.</p>

<p>The new production opened at the Alexandra Theatre, Birmingham on 13 March 1972 before transferring to the Theatre Royal, Brighton, on 20 March 1972 for a three week run. Soon afterwards Michael Codron was interviewed by The Daily Telegraph and said the play was due to transfer to the West End in April – this given extra credence by the presence of Celia Johnson in the cast, who is unlikely to have agreed to appear in a production that was not bound for London. In the interview Codron explained why he was attempting to take the play to the West End so quickly after the failure of the first tour. “I liked it very well. It is a brilliant conception but it is difficult to say why it did not reach the West End.”</p>

<p>He may have had more insight into that decision when the plug was pulled soon afterwards during the run of the tour in Brighton. The unexpectedly abrupt announcement of its closure was recalled by Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p>“Michael Codron summoned the company into her [Celia Johnson’s] dressing room on the last week of the play’s tour to announce with due solemnity that the play was not to come in. We all left quietly and with suitable reverence for a dead play. Michael remained behind to console Celia who sat staring into her mirror, murmuring, ‘It’s not right. It’s not right’. Michael, assuming his philosophical expression, muttered solicitously ‘No, I know. But that’s theatre isn’t it? Like life, it can sometimes be cruel….’ ‘No,’ Celia interrupted, ‘I meant the play’s not right….'”</p>

<p>Alan would later say he was actually pleased the play had never reached London: “I’d hate to see it on Shaftesbury Avenue because I think people would expect of it something it hasn’t got.” On the plus side, it helped forge the relationship between Alan and Michael Codron which would see him produce the majority of his West End work over the next three decades.</p>

<p>The play was subsequently withdrawn and, ordinarily, that should have been the end of it, much like <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-sparrow">The Sparrow</a></strong></em> three years previously. Except Alan couldn’t quite let this one go – possibly because it had gone so much further than <em>The Sparrow</em>. By the mid-1970s, Peggy noted that “Alan has three versions of&nbsp;<em>Me Times Me Times Me</em>, and he has told me that he intends to look at it and make a definitive version.” Further correspondence from the period includes reference to&nbsp;<em>Family Circles</em>, indicating that Alan had not only revised the play again but come up with its final title.</p>

<p>This would be the version that would find success when it opened in 1978 at The Orange Tree at Richmond, directed by Sam Walters, and which Alan would direct in Scarborough at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round in 1985.</p>

<p>And yet, despite all this history, the fact it was eventually successfully produced and has been published and produced for the better part of four decades, Alan still isn’t convinced about the play noting after the 1985 revival, “I should have left it alone.”</p>
