<p>Last week’s Halloween post has raised a couple of enquiries about one of the more obscure pieces of Alan Ayckbourn’s long career.</p>

<p>Within the <strong><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Bg5zO2uTr6U&amp;list=PLB8BjEuBpjT8pV_VHru6ENDL-ixiqG8K-&amp;index=14&amp;t=4s">Halloween video</a></strong>, there is mention of <em>The Fearsome Threesome</em>, a monologue written by Alan in 1989 with just one production during Christmas that year. The question has been asked, what precisely was this piece?</p>

<p><em>The Fearsome Threesome</em> (also known as <em>Ghost Stories</em> or possibly <em>Ghost Story</em> – it’s complicated!) is a long forgotten work by Alan Ayckbourn, the existence of which only came to light during 2018.</p>

<p>Prior to that, it existed only as a vague listing in the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round’s 1989 winter brochure – there was no apparent connection to Alan Ayckbourn and next to no details has been recorded about the production nor could the playwright himself recall much about it.</p>

<p><em>The Fearsome Threesome</em> or <em>Ghost Stories</em> (as it was promoted) was a late night event in the Studio at the Stephen Joseph Theatre In The Round, Scarborough, during the 1989 / 90 winter season. It had just six performances on consecutive Fridays and Saturdays between 29 December 1989 and 13 January 1990 following the evening’s performance of Alan Ayckbourn’s revival of his classic play <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em>.</p>

<p><em>Ghost Stories</em> was advertised within the winter brochure as ‘a short season of late-night readings of ghost stories in the studio theatre’ – confusingly although this brochure names it as <em>Ghost Stories</em>, within the diary section, the piece is called <em>Ghost Story</em>.</p>

<p>There is no mention of Alan Ayckbourn either writing or directing the piece which suggests a late change to the planned production, as there is no indication any other ghost stories were read during the actual run. Just to confuse the title issue a little more, Alan’s script is actually called <em>The Fearsome Threesome</em> but which does not appear to have ever been used in advertising or promotion.</p>

<p>The piece is a monologue read by an actor and it is ambiguous as to the sex of the narrator as there exists scripts for both a male and female narrator and no-one recalls which of the script was used or whether different actors were utilised depending on availability on any given night. The narrator is introduced sat by a table with a bottle of wine and an empty glass, who becomes aware of the audience and tells their tale of ‘The Fearsome Threesome’.</p>

<p>Two years earlier, following an acrimonious divorce, the narrator unexpectedly meets a couple, Martin and Sheila, at the theatre and they immediately become fast friends. The initial warmth of the friendship soon becomes tainted by the narrator’s jealousy of Martin and Sheila’s seemingly perfect and happy relationship; the narrator feels they do not appreciate what they have nor experience the difficulties most people encounter in life.</p>

<p>They try to disrupt the couple’s happiness initially with petty acts of vandalism which gradually escalate until one is poisoned and the other seduced; by which point it has become obvious that the narrator is an unreliable – arguably unstable – narrator, who sees their dangerous and malevolent actions as an entirely rationale response to Sheila and Martin’a apparently perfect relationship.</p>

<p>When Sheila and Martin eventually shut the narrator out of their lives, they decide the couple are still being unreasonable in their happiness and decides to take away the one thing they believes makes them happy, their home. The narrator sets fire to the house, unwittingly – perhaps – killing Martin and Sheila in the process.</p>

<p>Having told their tale with no regrets, the narrator reveals it is the first anniversary of the fire as they opens the bottle of wine they mysteriously received shortly before the fire believed to have come from the person seduced as a ‘thank you’. The narrator drinks the wine before quickly collapsing back into the seat, managing a final toast to ‘absent friends’…</p>

<p><em>‘Behind her two charred. frightening figures mysteriously appear. Bespectacled. They are holding wine glasses. The two figures raise their glasses in mock salute. The women sees them and gives a great cry, goes rigid for a second and then slumps in he chair, dead. Distant laughter is heard as the two figures raise their wine glasses in a mocking toast. They vanish mysteriously as they appeared.’</em></p>

<p>The theatre’s press officer, Jeannie Swales, recalls at the end of the production, the apparitions in all their burnt glory materialised courtesy of a ‘Pepper’s Ghost’ effect with the two ghosts played by Jeannie and the box office manager Joy Beadle.</p>

<p>Given the small space in the Studio, it appears it was quite an effort to create a ‘Pepper’s Ghost’ which requires a large sheet of angled glass in order to produce the illusion of people and objects fading in and out of existence (one of the most famous uses of it is within Walt Disney theme parks in the ‘ballroom’ sequence of the <em>Haunted Mansion</em> ride).</p>

<p>Jeannie also recalls the effect wasn’t terribly effective! The two ‘ghosts’ were required to wear an ‘unpleasant’ costume which gave the appearance of burnt and melted spectres. Given the amount of effort involved to produce the ‘Pepper’s Ghost’ effect, it is perhaps surprising there is no specific mention of the effect in the sole-surviving manuscript, held in the Ayckbourn Archive at the <strong><a href="http://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Borthwick Institute for Archives</a></strong> at the University of York. Within the original script, the ghosts merely ‘mysteriously’ appears and disappear.</p>

<p>Although it is largely speculation on this archivist’s part, the Studio saw the world premiere of Stephen Mallatratt’s famed adaptation of Susan Hill’s <em><strong><a href="http://thewomaninblack.theatre-in-the-round.co.uk">The Woman In Black</a></strong></em> in 1987. It is on record that Alan was inspired by this to write his own ghost story to match the success of <em>The Woman In Black</em> – which eventually appeared as <strong><em><a href="/plays/haunting-julia">Haunting Julia</a></em></strong> in 1994. It does not seem far-fetched to suggest that <em>The Fearsome Threesome</em> was devised primarily to see whether a ‘Pepper’s Ghost’ illusion could be created by the theatre and perhaps incorporated into a play.</p>

<p>It is worth noting that with Martin and Sheila, Alan wrote one of his few ‘happy couples’ – as epitomised by Anthea and Richard in <em><strong><a href="/plays/joking-apart">Joking Apart</a></strong></em> – whose content and enviable relationship leads to a murderous envy in the narrator; although arguably in this case, Martin and Sheila may just be an ‘ordinary’ couple envied by someone unable to see how her own jealousy and narrational behaviour has blighted her own life.</p>

<p>The history and existence of <em>The Fearsome Threesome</em> / <em>Ghost Stories</em> only came about by a series of happy coincidences. Jeannie Swales in 2018 recalled the show and the ‘Pepper’s Ghost’ effect, but could not find any mention of it on <strong><a href="/">Alan Ayckbourn’s Official Website</a></strong> nor remember what it was called.</p>

<p>As Alan’s archivist, I was able to identify the piece as <em>Ghost Stories</em> from the Ayckbourn Archive’s collection of Stephen Joseph Theatre brochures. The final piece of the puzzle was a manuscript – <em>The Fearsome Threesome</em> – discovered by the writer Rob Shearman more than two decades previously, which he had given to me but which neither of us were able to trace the providence of other than to confirm it was written by Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p>Only in 2018, when I was looking through archived scripts and noticed the similarity between the plot and Jeannie’s recollection, did the pieces come together and it transpire that <em>The Fearsome Threesome</em> was actually the basis for <em>Ghost Stories</em>, confirmed by the playwright himself.</p>

<p>Sadly, the play has never been produced since 1989 and whose existence is known to only the most ardent of Ayckbourn fans. It is not even known how many people saw the work – although it could not have been more than 350 people – and, as a late night show, was probably considerably less.</p>

<p>It is now classified as one of the playwright’s ‘Grey plays’ having been produced professionally although it has never been published and is not available to produce.</p>
