<p><em>Continuing our look into the history of Scarborough’s Stephen Joseph Theatre through significant events, plays and people. The Library Theatre moves into a new decade as we reach 1960.</em></p>

<p><em>To set the scene, the SJT was founded by Stephen Joseph as the Library Theatre in Scarborough in 1955 and had, from the beginning, looked to forge links with Scarborough’s amateur drama community to help run elements of the theatre to keep it viable.</em></p>

<p><strong>1960: An Essential Element – Forgotten…</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>It is sad to note that in the history of the Stephen Joseph Theatre and how it was founded – and even how it later survived closure – one important element has all but been forgotten to time.</p>

<p>That the SJT exists today is largely due to the amateur dramatic community of Scarborough. Members of this community helped Stephen Joseph set up the theatre in 1955, they helped front-of-house throughout the 1950s and 1960s and when <a href="/life/stephen-joseph">Stephen Joseph</a> closed the Library Theatre in 1965, they were part of the driving force which saved and re-opened the theatre (more of which in several blogs time!).</p>

<p>Stephen Joseph firmly believed there was a place for the amateur community within professional theatre and, ideally, the two could work together. He writes about this in his seminal book, <em>Theatre In The Round</em>, but he also specifically pointed to how vital these groups were to the Library Theatre in Scarborough.</p>

<p><em>“Many Scarborough people are proud that theatre in the round virtually started its career here. From the start the venture had the support of local amateur groups. This extended beyond helping to set up and dismantle the theatre. Front of house help was recruited from volunteers, and prop-hunting, costume-finding, and the distribution of publicity material were all aided by voluntary help, under the supervision of Kenneth Boden. Besides helping us he has, as secretary of the local branch of the British Drama League, organised an amateur theatre in the round festival at Scarborough which looks like becoming an important annual event in the North of England.”<br>Stephen Joseph</em></p>

<p>The latter is what we’re focusing on today. In 1960, Stephen Joseph launched the UK’s first in-the-round amateur drama festival, encouraging amateur companies to try their hand at what was still a relatively unknown form of theatre within this country.</p>

<p>The festival is a significant part of the SJT’s history, although very little is held in the archive about the events which ran from 1960 to 1968 and 1977 to approximately 1987. In fact, the largest repository of material relating to the festival is Scarborough Library in the <a href="http://ayckbourn-archive.alanayckbourn.net/page-5/page-19/">Stephen Joseph Theatre Collection</a> rather than at the SJT.</p>

<p>What <a href="http://ayckbourn-archive.alanayckbourn.net/page-5/page-16/">The Bob Watson Archive</a> at the SJT does have is a quantity of press cuttings relating to the event and photographs of Stephen Joseph in his role as adjudicator of the first event.</p>

<p>The articles demonstrate there was significant interest in the festival as it received extensive coverage in the region’s major newspapers as well as being publicised as far as away as Glasgow.</p>

<p>The first event ran from 14 – 17 September, 1960, immediately following the end of the summer season at the Library Theatre. The first event was promoted as “A Festival Of Plays To Be Performed In The Round” but in subsequent years was changed to the somewhat catchier “In The Round Festival.”</p>

<p>Organised in conjunction with the British Drama League – of which the theatre manager Ken Boden was a key member – the event was announced to the media in April and attracted eight groups, who performed 10 productions.</p>

<p>The companies were: Whitby Youth Club, Pickering Drama Club, Scarborough Theatre Guild, Bradford Experimental Theatre-in-the-Round Group, Hull Garrett Players, Mexborough Technical College, Hugh Bell Evening Institute Middlesborough, Merseyside Unity Theatre.</p>

<p>Plays performed during the first festival included Shakespeare’s <em>A Midsummer Night’s Dream</em>, Priestley’s <em>A Glass Of Bitter</em>, Jonson’s <em>Volpone</em> and Beckett’s <em>Endgame</em> as well as several more experimental pieces.</p>

<div><figure><figcaption>Stephen Joseph adjudicating the 1960 in-the-round festival<br>(© Scarborough Theatre Trust)</figcaption></figure></div>

<p>In subsequent years, the festival attracted groups from York, Rotherham, Manchester, Leeds, Stoke-on-Trent, Derby and even the Greenock Players from Scotland.</p>

<p>The aim of the event, according to Stephen Joseph, was “to stir up theatre and see what it could do for amateur drama.”</p>

<p>Stephen himself adjudicated each performance and was encouraging, if notoriously honest, in his appraisals judging by the media reports at the time. The success of the event was noted by Scarborough Council and even used as part of the argument for an early push for the company to have a purpose-built home in the town, sadly something which would not be realised until 1996 and the move into the company’s present home.</p>

<p>The festival continued until at least 1968 at the Library Theatre with <a href="/life/influences/bradley-alfred">Alfred Bradley</a>, the famous northern BBC Radio Producer, adjudicating after Stephen’s death in 1967.</p>

<p>The festival was later revived by Alan Ayckbourn after the company’s move to the Stephen Joseph Theatre In The Round in 1976 with the playwright adjudicating several festivals. Sadly, no details are held in The Bob Watson Archive about the festivals held during this period.</p>

<p>What is interesting about the festival is it shows how important Stephen Joseph felt the amateur community was to the company during its early years. One of his earliest ambitions for the company was that professional and amateurs should be able to work in the same environment. This in itself paid dividends as its was the amateur companies that would later rally around and keep the theatre operating for the year after it was closed until it was re-opned as a professional organisation – predominantly through the efforts of Ken Boden, a man who bridged both professional and amateur communities.</p>

<p>The theatre maintained close links with the amateur community until the early 1990s. In both the Library Theatre and its second home, the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round, there was an annual amateur season alongside the in-the-round festival and many companies from the Scarborough area as well as schools performed during this slot.</p>

<p>By the mid-’90s, the links were becoming more tenuous though and, sadly, with the move to its present home, the Stephen Joseph Theatre, those links practically vanished completely. No doubt the amateur community itself has changed extensively since the early days of the SJT but that connection – whatever it might have developed into – is still something Stephen Joseph would have felt had been lost. Although, it should be noted that the SJT has more extensive links than ever with the wider Scarborough community thanks tho the impressive work and efforts of the OutReach department.  </p>

<p>But it must never be forgotten that the SJT owes its very existence to the amateur community which Stephen felt was an essential part of the company from the very beginning. Without the likes of Ken Boden and his army of volunteers drawn from the amateur community, who both helped the theatre and then stepped in to save it, there would be no SJT today. Alan Ayckbourn himself, who succeeded as Stephen Joseph as Artistic Director of the SJT and kept his legacy alive, acknowledged the significance of the amateur community to Stephen.</p>

<p><em>“Stephen was quite unique in his time in that he made friends with amateurs rather than snubbing them, and involved the local dramatic societies with the professional workings of the early theatre in the round company in various ways.</em>“</p>

<p>The In-The-Round festivals are a memory of Stephen Joseph’s intentions and belief  in encouraging amateurs and making them part of both the theatre community at large and the company he founded.</p>
