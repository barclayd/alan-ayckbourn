<p>Recently, the Blog has been looking at posters for Alan Ayckbourn’s plays over the decades.</p>

<p>As a result of this, I’ve had a few people email to ask what my favourite Ayckbourn posters are.</p>

<p>That’s quite a big question as I’ve literally seen thousands of posters over the past two decades from Alan’s world premieres to obscure amateur productions on the other side of the world.</p>

<p>Even if we only consider the world and West End premieres as well as other productions Alan has directed, that’s well over 400 different posters! So confining my choices to these parameters, here’s some of my favourites over the years. They’re presented in date order rather than actual preference (but you’ll probably guess my favourite…).</p>

</div>

<p><strong>Absurd Person Singular (1972):</strong> As an archivist, I find this image fascinating. It’s the first ever poster image for an Ayckbourn world premiere production. It’s extraordinary to think that prior to 1972 and his previous 11 plays, there isn’t an actual design image for the world premiere production of one of his works.</p>

<p>So this is one of my favourites because historically it’s so significant. It’s a lino print for the 1972 world premiere of <em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em> at Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre, Scarborough, and was only used on promotional flyers – which are now incredibly rare to find.</p>

<p>It also exemplifies a huge issue which has faced poster designers repeatedly over the decades – Alan’s plays generally don’t easily suggest promotional images, so you have to be quite creative – or, in the case, of <em><strong><a href="/plays/relatively-speaking">Relatively Speaking</a></strong></em>, just do increasingly dull variations on the theme of slippers! Here we’ve got an emphasis on the party aspect of <em>Absurd Person Singular</em>, but which is subtly unnerving with the rather ominous presence of the chef’s knife embedded in the table.</p>

<div>
<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/1979a-sisterly-feelings-w350.jpg" alt="" width="350" height="487" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>Sisterly Feelings (1979):</strong> This is another world premiere poster and it’s for <em><strong><a href="/plays/sisterly-feelings">Sisterly Feelings</a></strong></em> at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round, Scarborough, from 1979. It’s always felt to me a very striking image – even though it doesn’t really say anything about the play. However, it must have struck a nerve as the same image was used by the National Theatre for its London premiere the following year.</p>

<p>I spoke to the designer of this poster a couple of decades ago and he noted how, at the time, the cost of ink was expensive and the more colours a poster had, the more expensive it would be. So a clever printer could give the impression of multiple colours with the minimum amount of inks. In this case, apparently just two coloured inks were used. Creativity overcoming financial limitations – true to theatres today as much as then!</p>

<p>It’s quite interesting that because <em>Sisterly Feelings</em> has been revived so infrequently over the years, this image – or variations of it – have been reprinted far more frequently than is the norm for Alan’s plays. If it ain’t broke, don’t fix it!</p>

<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/1987-a-small-family-business-cover-w350.jpg" alt="" width="350" height="595" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>A Small Family Business (1987):</strong> The National Theatre has produced some memorable images over the years for its Ayckbourn productions and there’s two in my selection alone. The first one is for the world premiere of <em><strong><a href="/plays/a-small-family-business">A Small Family Business</a></strong></em> in The Olivier in 1987. This is just a very striking image and captures some of the themes and ideas within the play.</p>

<p>The poster was based on an actual photograph of the actress Elizabeth Bell’s booted leg with whip and £10 pound notes; Elizabeth played the role of Anita McCracken in the production and – spoilers – the outfit plays a prominent part in the play itself. There’s quite a well-known photograph of Alan himself preparing the photo-shoot for the poster whilst at the National.</p>

<p>Alan tells the story that there were some nervous reaction to the poster at the National at the time and even a concerted effort to change it to as it really wasn’t what the National Theatre did! Alan steadfastly refused to alter it, standing his ground as director (and Company Director at the National at the time). I think it’s one of the most distinctive Ayckbourn images produced for the West End / National Theatre, thankfully, it was not changed.</p>

<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/mr-as-amazing-maze-plays-1993-02-w350.jpg" alt="" width="350" height="429" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>Mr A’s Amazing Maze Plays (1993): </strong>I said I wasn’t going to list favourite, but – if you had to push me – this is my favourite Ayckbourn poster of all time. It was commissioned by the National Theatre for Alan’s revival of his first family play, <em><strong><a href="/plays/mr-as-amazing-maze-plays">Mr A’s Amazing Maze Plays</a></strong></em>, at the National Theatre, five years after it had premiered in Scarborough.</p>

<p>The image was created by the award-winning and best-selling illustrator Korky Paul and I think it’s just a delight. As a poster for a family play, it works fabulously well with its huge array of details and sense of fun and adventure – I keep finding hidden details in it whenever I look at the very large reproduction I have in the hall of my home! This is a rarity in that it’s an Ayckbourn poster which manages to encapsulate the play in one image.</p>

<p>If I can take one poster to a desert island with me, it’s this one. Don’t know where I’d hang it though…</p>

</div>

<p><strong>Wildest Dreams (1992):</strong> Back to the world premieres and this is <em><strong><a href="/plays/wildest-dreams">Wildest Dreams</a></strong></em> at the Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round in 1994. <em>Wildest Dreams</em> is a relatively little known – and very dark – Ayckbourn play about a group of role-players and the introduction to the game of a hugely disruptive new member; Alan was somewhat ahead of his time  given that pretty much everyone seems to be playing <em>Dungeons &amp; Dragons</em> and its ilk now!</p>

<p>This was actually the first Ayckbourn poster I ever bought and I’m still drawn to it with the dragon reflected in the eye. Perhaps it’s because I love dragons and there aren’t enough dragons in Alan’s posters…</p>

<p>More likely, on a level I can’t explain, it just seems to suit the play perfectly. Which, to my mind, is the mark of a good poster. You don’t have to know how it works, just that it strikes an emotional chord with you. It also has a good font for the play title too. As a former journalist, I’m always susceptible to a good font!</p>

<figure><img src="https://pub-4c23c36058c0491eaa4d6d55c25b33de.r2.dev/news/2024/1994b-haunting-julia-w350.jpg" alt="" width="350" height="498" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
</div>

<p><strong>Haunting Julia (1994):</strong> <em><strong><a href="/plays/haunting-julia">Haunting Julia</a></strong></em> is not only one of my personal favourites of Alan’s plays, but its poster has long since been one of my favourites for a world premiere production.</p>

<p>This one more obviously captures a feel of the play with the pencil drawing of Julia’s bedroom, her teddy bear, the sheet-music and lightly drawn – and very frequently missed – a ghostly image of Julia herself, lost in thought.</p>

<p>There’s something about this which, to my mind, captures the essence of the play. Yes, all the ingredients are there but the starkness and simplicity of the room feels as though it’s a prison rather than a bedroom. That it’s a place where Julia is trapped and that’s true of the play, Julia’s genius for music is a prison for her, she feels confined and trapped by her immense gift. She’s an unhappy, troubled soul in the play and the poster manages to reflect that with this simple, effective image.</p>

<p>The poster was obviously something Alan liked too as it was re-used for his 1999 revival of the play at the Stephen Joseph Theatre (albeit with a less interesting use of title and font).</p>

<p><strong>The Champion of Paribanou (1996);</strong> In 1996, Alan Ayckbourn moved the Scarborough company to its first purpose-built home, the Stephen Joseph Theatre converted from the town’s former Odeon cinema. His first new play at the building was a family play for Christmas, <em><strong><a href="/plays/the-champion-of-paribanou">The Champion of Paribanou</a></strong></em>, a fantasy inspired by the tale of <em>The Magic Carpet</em>, but incorporating a huge array of influences or as Alan himself put it: ‘It developed into something like&nbsp;<em>Mad Max</em>&nbsp;meets&nbsp;<em>Scheherazade</em>.’ I always liked the Financial Times critic Ian Shuttleworth’s description of it as ‘The Arabian Nights meets <em>Star Wars</em>‘ though!</p>

<p>This fusion of fantasy futurism is encapsulated with this striking poster with an exciting and colourful design which leaps from the page. As Alan’s first new play at an exciting new theatre, it seems like a statement of intent. In a play which incorporates automatons, magic, fantastic creatures, evil demons and sword-fights, the poster captures the magic and imagination of the production.</p>

<p>So that’s my choice of posters. What about you? Do you have any favourites or memorable posters from Ayckbourn productions over the decades? Let me know your thoughts and favourites. </p>
