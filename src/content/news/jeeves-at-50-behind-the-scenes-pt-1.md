<p><em>On 22 March 2025, Alan Ayckbourn and Andrew Lloyd Webber’s infamous musical <strong><a href="/plays/jeeves">Jeeves</a></strong> celebrates its 50th anniversary.</em></p>

<p><em>The musical premiered at the Bristol Hippodrome – prior to its West End debut – in 1975 and is where the sheer scale of its never resolved problems became glaringly apparent. </em></p>

<p><em>This adaptation of P G Wodehouse’s beloved novels has long been considered one of the great West End flops – even if this has been mitigated over time by the success of its award winning 1996 revision / rewriter By Jeeves.</em></p>

<p><em>To mark the 50th anniversary of the original Jeeves, Alan Ayckbourn’s archivist Simon Murgatroyd has been trawling through the records to present an archival perspective of one of the biggest upsets of Sir Alan Ayckbourn’s career.</em></p>

<p><strong>Jeeves at 50 – Behind The Scenes, part 1</strong> by Simon Murgatroyd</p>

<p>Theatre history records&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;as one of the most notorious flops to have been staged in London’s West End. Uniting the talents of the playwright Alan Ayckbourn and the composer Andrew Lloyd Webber, it proved to be a disastrous attempt to bring the characters of P.G. Wodehouse to the stage.</p>

<p>The story behind the musical is worth a book in its own right, yet despite its notoriety its history has rarely been touched upon in any depth. The <strong><a href="http://www.alanayckbourn.net/page-10/page18.html">Ayckbourn Archive</a></strong> – held in the Borthwick Institute for Archives at the University of York – holds a considerable amount of correspondence and material relating to&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>, which, although incomplete and predominantly containing material directly related to Alan’s involvement in the play, does offer at least some insight into how one of the West End’s most anticipated shows became one of its biggest failures.*</p>

<p><em>Jeeves</em> does not begin with Alan Ayckbourn though, but with Lloyd Webber and the lyricist Tim Rice who conceived a musical based around the&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;novels at least two years prior to Alan’s involvement. Both men were fans of Wodehouse novels and felt the stories of Bertie Wooster and his manservant Jeeves were prime material for transfer to the stage.&nbsp;</p>

<p><em>“I have to take the blame for having thought of the whole idea in the first place. I’ve always found P.G. Wodehouse books terribly funny and thought there was something to be done with them in musical theatre. ‘Jeeves’ was to be the follow-up to ‘Jesus Christ Superstar’ and ‘Joseph and the Amazing Technicolor Dreamcoat’. I was going to do it with Tim Rice, and we thought it would be great fun.”<br>Andrew Lloyd Webber</em></p>

<p>Together they produced a rough draft of the musical, but Rice was unhappy with his own contribution and realised he would have difficulty doing justice to the much-loved characters.</p>

<p><em>“It wasn’t very good, particularly the lyrics. The tunes were good, probably better than those in almost any other British musical, but the words were dire, they were mine, and the plot wasn’t working out. So I abandoned it.”<br>Tim Rice</em></p>

<p>The lyricist’s precise involvement in&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;from this point onwards is not clear as interviews with the key players contradict each other.</p>

<p>Lloyd Webber, however, was determined to continue despite Rice’s reticence and had even visited P.G. Wodehouse in New York to get the author’s blessing; he also began searching for someone to write the book for the musical, apparently in the hope that Rice would then be inspired to stay on board the project as lyricist.</p>

<p>Enter Alan Ayckbourn.</p>

<p>Encouraged by his agent <strong><a href="/life/influences/ramsay-margaret">Margaret ‘Peggy’ Ramsay</a></strong> to give serious consideration to writing the book for the musical in November 1973, Alan arranged to meet Lloyd Webber and Rice to discuss working together.&nbsp;</p>

<p><em>“We had a private meal between Tim Rice and myself and Andrew… and we talked about what a great idea it would be. Tim would write the lyrics and I’d do the book, which would be merely structuring them a storyline. And I said: ‘Well, I think I can hack one out for you.’ And Andrew had already got some music sketches.”<br>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p>Alan, also a Wodehouse fan, agreed to write the book, but Rice was still uncertain about his involvement and by March 1974 had decided to step away to concentrate on writing&nbsp;<em>Evita</em>. Alan and Lloyd Webber began discussing alternative plans – although at no point was it suggested Alan would write the lyrics.&nbsp;</p>

<p><em>“We’ll have, as they say, to hold ‘a most promising lyrics writer’ competition! Obviously the most important thing, finally, is to get someone who sparks you [Lloyd Webber] off and vice versa.”<br>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p>Despite the lyricist issues, the musical already had financial backing in the shape of the Australian producer Robert Stigwood. Based in Los Angeles with interests in music, television, film and theatre – but ironically apparently no interest in Wodehouse’s work according to Lloyd Webber – his diverse career had recently seen him produce the movie of Lloyd Webber and Rice’s&nbsp;<em>Jesus Christ Superstar</em>. Despite providing the finances, his presence was rarely felt and Alan recalls only meeting him twice and apparently only after&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;had opened in London. In his stead, Bob Swash was appointed to act as the executive producer and go-between for Stigwood.</p>

<p>The project also lacked a director and, for this, Alan suggested Eric Thompson. He had successfully directed Alan’s plays&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/time-and-time-again">Time And Time Again</a></strong></em>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/absurd-person-singular">Absurd Person Singular</a></strong></em>&nbsp;in the West End and shared a great rapport with Alan; he was also directing&nbsp;<em><strong><a href="/plays/the-norman-conquests">The Norman Conquests</a></strong>&nbsp;</em>for Greenwich Theatre and due to transfer&nbsp;<em>Absurd Person Singular</em>&nbsp;to Broadway later in the year. Alan argued that not only was Thompson on his “wavelength” but also that “if we’re going to catch the Wodehouse flavour, it needs to be the essence of comic timing – both musically and verbally”; although it later transpired Thompson had no experience at all of Wodehouse’s writing</p>

<p>The central creative team was now assembled for&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>. None of them had ever worked on a West End musical before.</p>

<p>Meanwhile, discussions had – perhaps inevitably – turned towards Alan contributing the lyrics</p>

<p><em>“Andrew said I could do the lyrics because that’s a piece of cake. It’s taken me 20 years to come to terms with that! They were the very first lyrics written by me.”<br>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p>Having agreed to now provide both the book and lyrics, Alan’s submitted his first attempt at the songs – “for better for worse” – to Lloyd Webber on 2 May. Alan’s uncertainty highlighted when he wrote: “Writing them is like having chicken pox – almost impossible to stop picking at them – and that doesn’t necessarily make them any better.” He also added that if Lloyd Webber was not happy, he could always turn to an unspecified Plan B.</p>

<p>The situation seems to have been fluid though as by mid-May, Bob Swash confirmed to Margaret Ramsay that Alan would write the lyrics jointly with Thompson; although Thompson did make some initial contribution, it has never been clarified what his exact contribution was, which led to contractual disagreements between him and Stigwood later.&nbsp;</p>

<p>The apparently haphazard approach to the musical shifted up a gear on the weekend of 4 July 1974, when Alan, Lloyd Webber and Thompson came together for the first time to begin, as a spokesman for <strong><a href="http:///www.a-round-town.com">Theatre in the Round at the Library Theatre</a></strong> in Scarborough phrased it: “hammering out tunes, lyrics and ideas for three days.</p>

<p>The trio were based in a bedsit in Scarborough complete with a piano and a six week old kitten owned by the theatre’s scenic designer Helga Wood – who had also provided the piano. By the end of the weekend, the basic shape and plot of&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;had been agreed, lyrics had been approved, altered and drafted (with Thompson convinced he had positively contributed towards them) and an agreement made for a first draft to be completed by September, when the trio would meet again in America</p>

<p>The events of the weekend were chronicled several days later in the Yorkshire Post, in which Lloyd Webber spoke about Alan’s involvement in the collaboration.</p>

<p><em>“I saw Absurd Person Singular at London’s Criterion Theatre last year and decided that I must write to Alan and try to persuade him to join me in Jeeves. Alan is, without doubt, the best English comedy writer around today, and Eric Thompson is a director with an extraordinary talent for seeing humour in any given situation. I couldn’t have found a better team to work with… if it works.”<br>Andrew Lloyd Webber</em></p>

<p>Alan meanwhile offered an insight into his thoughts on adapting Wodehouse for the stage: “The treatment we shall give&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em> will have a contemporary slant. It won’t be a museum piece. The books are so dramatic in terms of theatre that one has to be fairly drastic.”</p>

<p>By 14 July, Alan had supplied an expanded draft of the work (considered the first draft and called A<em>n Evening With Bertram Wooster</em>) which Lloyd Webber worked on for the New York meeting, now scheduled for the first week of October. The most obvious problem at this stage – and one which would haunt&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;– was Alan admitted his inexperience with musicals meant the script was far too long.</p>

<p><em>“The script was heavily over-length, because the other thing, of course, I wasn’t used to was incorporating the songs: and as I’d written the lyrics I’d been pacing myself on a play, so we had something about one-and-a-half times too long.”<br>Alan Ayckbourn</em></p>

<p>That Alan and Lloyd Webber were not in full control of the project was made clear in September when Swash told Lloyd Webber he was anxious for casting to start by October at the latest as he had theatres lined up for March; it is a sobering thought that casting did not actually begin until January, yet the show was still lined up to open in Bristol less than three months later.</p>

<p>Clearly, the producers were dictating the pace of the show and had fixed ideas about how long the process should take. Alan’s response to this was sage, but perhaps prophetic: “I hope Bob Swash’s urgency won’t panic us into a rush job.”</p>

<p>At the start of October, Alan travelled to New York for the opening of&nbsp;<em>Absurd Person Singular</em>&nbsp;on Broadway and met Lloyd Webber and Thompson as arranged with a complete draft of the musical. Stigwood had also arranged for them to visit Wodehouse at his Long Island home to hear the musical for the first time, although as Alan noted Wodehouse “was very, very deaf.”</p>

<p>The group was taken to the home of an experimental composer who possessed a piano and, much to Lloyd Webber’s horror, it transpired he had invited friends along to hear the music too. What was intended as a private audience for Wodehouse had become an impromptu performance with Lloyd Webber playing the piano and, unexpectedly, also singing. At the afternoon’s end, Wodehouse declared he was happy with what he may or may not have heard and was shuffled off home by his wife Ethel.&nbsp;</p>

<p>With Thompson now free to concentrate all his energies on&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>, the first major doubts about the scale of the musical began to emerge. Alan had incorporated some elaborate staging including the exterior and interior set of Totleigh Towers on a revolve, the idea being that over the course of the play, Bertie and his antics wreak havoc on the stately home – as the stage directions illustrate.</p>

<p><em>A pleasant looking country house frontage, complete with balconies, trellis work with climbing plants etc.<br>Jeeves and Bertie pull up at the front door and dismount. Jeeves packs away the car. The are admitted by the butler. They enter.<br>The house revolves through 180 degrees so we now look into it from the back, like a cross section of a open doll’s house.</em></p>

<p>This then leads at the climax of the play to:</p>

<p><em>The house revolves. We see the front for the first time since the beginning. It is now a complete wreck. All the downstairs windows are broken. Shutters are torn off. Creeper pulled away. Balcony wrecked.</em></p>

<p>However, Thompson was worried about what could realistically be achieved on stage and within the budget. This would soon prove to be moot with the appointment of Voytek, an avant-garde and apparently very strong-minded designer, who had very clear ideas about what&nbsp;<em>Jeeves</em>&nbsp;needed, which tallied with Thompson but not Alan.&nbsp;</p>

<p>Unfortunately Voytek and Thompson both happened to be working together in Holland, while Alan and Lloyd Webber were in England, unaware of what was happening. When Alan realised a significant part of the script didn’t work – specifically the use of Bertie’s Drones as a chorus – he decided he should cut them.&nbsp;</p>

<p>Unfortunately, Thompson and Voytek had decided the exact opposite and made the Drones an integral part of the staging which had replaced Alan’s physical set with a series of abstract silk backcloths, the constant flying of which would noticeably slow down the pace of an already overlong musical.</p>

<p>As 1974 drew to a close and the new year opened, the real problems were only just beginning. In less than three months, a huge West End production was due to be launched and no-one had any conceivable idea of just how badly it could all go…</p>
